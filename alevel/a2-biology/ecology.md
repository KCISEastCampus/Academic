## From a count to an explanation {#start}

Finding fewer plants in shade describes a pattern. Explaining it requires a mechanism and evidence that separates light from other differences, such as soil moisture or grazing. This page builds that distinction through population change, sampling, competition experiments and succession. Start with species, averages and plant resource needs in [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/).

In a pond, there are fish of one species, several plant species, water and sediment. Identify one population, the community and the ecosystem. Explain why the fish population alone is not the ecosystem.
<!-- /task:E0 -->

<details markdown="1">

Widen the boundary from one species to all organisms, then include the non-living surroundings.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check E0</summary>

The fish of that species form a population. All populations of living organisms form the community. The community plus its non-living surroundings form the ecosystem; water and sediment are part of that system.

## Niches and changing population size {#populations}

A habitat is where an organism lives; its **niche** includes its use of resources, interactions and tolerances. Two species may share a habitat but use food at different times or heights, reducing competition. Adaptations help explain where a species persists, not an intention to occupy a role.

Population size changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Abiotic factors include temperature, water availability, pH, salinity and light. Their effects depend on physiology: for example, reduced light can lower photosynthesis and growth, while drought can reduce survival. More of an abiotic factor is not always better; temperature outside a tolerable range can reduce performance.

$$\Delta N=(B+I)-(D+E).$$

Here N is population size, B births, I immigration, D deaths and E emigration during the same interval. A flat population curve can hide substantial turnover if gains equal losses. Early rapid growth may slow as resources per individual fall; a carrying capacity is conditional on the environment and may change, rather than a permanent fixed number.

**Intraspecific competition** occurs between members of the same species, often with similar resource needs. **Interspecific competition** occurs between species. Competition requires a shared resource to be limiting; organisms need not touch or fight. As density rises, lower resources per individual may reduce growth or reproduction and increase mortality.

Predation can reduce prey abundance. More prey can later support more predators, and a subsequent prey decline may be followed by a predator decline. This mechanism can generate a time lag, but food supply, disease and weather can also drive both populations. Two oscillating curves alone do not establish the entire cause.

## Sampling: match the method to the organism and question {#sampling}

For immobile organisms, mark out the study area and use randomly generated coordinates for quadrats. Choose a consistent rule for boundary individuals. Count density, presence/absence for frequency, or percentage cover as appropriate; cover is useful when individuals cannot be separated easily. Different species can overlap, so summed cover need not be below 100%.

Use sufficient independent quadrat locations and report variation as well as the mean. If habitats differ strongly, sample randomly within each stratum and weight the final estimate by stratum area. Throwing a quadrat or choosing accessible patches is not equivalent to random coordinates.

For a gradient, place quadrats at stated intervals along a transect and record an abiotic variable. Replicate transects in independent locations. This estimates change with position; it is not automatically an unbiased estimate of the whole habitat. An observed relationship still needs alternative explanations to be considered.

**Worked estimate — Self-written; constructed data:** counts in five 0.25 m² quadrats are 3, 5, 4, 2 and 6. The mean is 4 per quadrat, hence 16 m⁻². An area of 200 m² would contain an estimated 3200 individuals if the sampled locations represent the whole area. This is an estimate, not a census; five samples alone may poorly represent patchiness.

$$\text{density}=\frac{\text{mean count per quadrat}}{\text{quadrat area}},\qquad \widehat N=\text{density}\times\text{total area}.$$

For mobile animals, a mark–release–recapture model can sometimes be used: mark M individuals, release and allow mixing, then capture C individuals, of which R are marked. The estimated total is MC/R. It assumes a sufficiently closed population, retained harmless marks, mixing, and similar capture chances. Trap avoidance or mark loss can bias the result; no recaptures make this simple estimate undefined. Use minimal disturbance and relevant permissions; the equation is not justification for unsafe animal handling.

## RP9: competition as an experiment {#competition}

To investigate seedling density, use the same species, seed batch, pot area, soil amount and nutrient concentration. Define the response, such as mean dry mass per surviving seedling after a set interval, and also record germination and survival. Keep light, water supply and temperature comparable. Density is set per pot, so **the independently treated pot is the experimental unit**; seedlings within it share resources.

Use several pots per density, randomise positions and rotate positions if a light gradient is present. For each pot, calculate a mean; compare those independent pot means. Twenty seedlings in one pot are not twenty independent density treatments. Increasing density can increase total pot biomass while reducing biomass per plant: these are different questions.

To separate intraspecific from interspecific effects, compare a focal species grown alone with it grown alongside a competitor, keeping total density and the chosen comparison clearly defined. An effect of another species might also involve shading or chemical inhibition; evidence should identify the mechanism rather than label every reduction “competition”.

Use approved soil/compost, wash hands, avoid inhaling dust and handle drying equipment under supervision. Do not release introduced species outdoors.

Four pots at each density are independently assigned and grown for 21 days. The mean total dry masses per pot are 1.2 g with 4 surviving plants and 1.6 g with 16 surviving plants. Calculate mean dry mass per plant at each density. Complete the causal chain from increased density to lower mass per plant, and state why the result does not show that total production decreased.
<!-- /task:E1 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint E1</summary>

Divide each pot mass by its own number of survivors. Name a limiting resource and the process it affects.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer E1</summary>

Mass per plant is 0.30 g and 0.10 g. Greater density can reduce light or mineral ions available per plant, lowering photosynthesis or synthesis of growth materials, and hence growth. Total dry mass increased from 1.2 to 1.6 g per pot. These treatment means alone do not establish statistical significance; inspect variation among the independent pots.

</details>

## Succession: organisms alter the conditions for the next community {#succession}

Primary succession begins on a surface with little or no developed soil, such as newly exposed rock. Pioneer species tolerate the initial conditions. Their activity and death can contribute to weathering and organic matter accumulation, improving water retention and nutrient availability. This permits species with different requirements to establish.

Later species change the environment again. Taller plants may reduce light near the ground and outcompete early colonists; root growth and litter alter the soil. Thus the explanation is **species traits → environmental change → changed establishment or competitive success**, not just a memorised list of plant names.

A relatively stable climax community can develop under the prevailing climate and conditions, but disturbances, grazing, fire and environmental change can redirect or interrupt succession. Biodiversity often changes during succession, but it need not increase at every step. Secondary succession begins where soil remains after disturbance and may proceed differently.

**Mechanism demonstration — Self-written:** grasses establish after soil develops; shrubs later shade them. The shrubs do not “want to replace” grasses. Their height changes light availability, lowering the competitive success of shade-intolerant grasses while supporting other species.

## Conservation is a choice of objectives and evidence {#conservation}

Protecting a habitat may require **managing succession**, not simply leaving it untouched. Grazing or mowing can suppress woody growth and maintain open habitats for particular species. Elsewhere, restoring woodland may be the appropriate objective. State the species or ecosystem service being protected, the intervention mechanism and the trade-off.

Conservation can protect genetic diversity, food webs, soil, water regulation and future resources, and can also have ethical value. Compare costs, livelihoods and opportunity costs alongside ecological benefits. Monitor the intended outcome with consistent sampling, untreated/reference areas where feasible, and repeated years; a single before-and-after count cannot exclude weather or other changes.

## Independent evidence tasks {#practice}

<!-- task:E2 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — E2**

A student puts one tray of 40 seedlings in shade and one tray of 10 seedlings in full light. The shaded seedlings have lower mean mass. Explain two reasons why the result cannot isolate an effect of density, and redesign the experiment to test density.
<!-- /task:E2 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint E2</summary>

Look for both a confounding variable and a missing level of replication.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer E2</summary>

Light and density both differ, so either could explain the mass difference. There is only one independently treated tray at each density. Use several trays per density under comparable light, randomise positions, keep tray area, soil, water and seed batch comparable, and compare tray means over a fixed interval while recording survival.

</details>

<!-- task:E3 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — E3**

A reserve stops mowing. After five years, shrub cover rises and a rare grassland orchid declines. Explain a plausible mechanism, one alternative explanation and evidence needed to decide whether mowing should resume. Include one trade-off.
<!-- /task:E3 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint E3</summary>

Connect the orchid's resources to vegetation change, then separate plausibility from proof.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer E3</summary>

Shrub shading may reduce the orchid's photosynthesis, growth or reproduction. Drought, herbivory or altered pollination could also contribute. Compare light, orchid survival/reproduction and replicated mown/reference plots over time. Resuming mowing may protect open-habitat species while reducing shrub habitat or disturbing nesting animals; the choice depends on reserve objectives and timing.

</details>

<!-- task:E4 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — E4**

Retry: 60 animals are marked and released. In a later sample of 50, 10 are marked. Estimate the population. Predict the direction of bias if some marks were lost before recapture, keeping the actual number of animals and capture behaviour unchanged.
<!-- /task:E4 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint E4</summary>

Consider which term in MC/R is underestimated.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer E4</summary>

Estimated population = 60 × 50 / 10 = 300. Mark loss makes recognised recaptures R too small, so the estimate is biased upwards. More arithmetic precision cannot repair a violated sampling assumption.

</details>
