## A stock is not a rate {#start}

A field can contain a large biomass but produce little new biomass this week. **Biomass** is a stock at a stated time; **production** concerns new biomass or its chemical energy over an interval. State the area or volume, time and quantity before comparing ecosystems. This page connects [photosynthesis](/alevel/a2-biology/photosynthesis/) and [respiration](/alevel/a2-biology/respiration/) to food webs and decisions about farming. For units and percentages, use [BL05 data skills](/alevel/a2-biology/data-skills/).

A pond contains 5 g m⁻² of algae and 10 g m⁻² of herbivores on one day. Does this show that the herbivores receive more energy per year than the algae fix? Identify the missing measurement.
<!-- /task:EN0 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN0</summary>

Compare a standing stock with a quantity transferred over a time interval.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN0</summary>

No. Algae can grow and be consumed rapidly, maintaining a small stock but high annual production. Measure energy production/transfer per area over the same year; the one-day biomass values do not supply that rate.

</details>

## Food webs and three different pyramids {#pyramids}

Producers fix energy into organic molecules. Primary consumers eat producers; secondary consumers eat primary consumers. Food-web arrows point **from the food to the consumer**, indicating transfer of material and chemical energy. Omnivores can feed at more than one trophic level, so a web cannot always be reduced to one chain.

A pyramid of **numbers** counts individuals; one tree can support many insects, so it may be inverted. A pyramid of **biomass**, commonly using dry mass per unit area, accounts for organism size but still records a stock. A rapidly turning-over producer stock can support a larger consumer stock. Water content makes fresh mass a poor basis for comparing chemical stores.

A pyramid of **energy production or transfer**, using the same area and interval, accounts for rates and energy content. In a correctly defined feeding chain, consumer production is less than the energy supplied in food, because some is not assimilated and some is used in respiration. Account for imported food and boundaries before comparing an open ecosystem; a stock pyramid cannot establish these flows.

## Primary production: gross, respiratory loss and net {#primary-production}

Gross primary production (GPP) is the rate at which producers fix energy into organic material. Some of this energy supports the producers' own respiration. Net primary production (NPP) is the remainder available for new plant biomass, including tissues later eaten or entering detritus. It is not all necessarily harvested or consumed.

Use consistent units, for example kJ m⁻² year⁻¹, for all terms. R here is **producer respiration**, not all respiration in the ecosystem.

$$NPP=GPP-R.$$

**Worked account — Self-written; constructed data:** GPP = 24 000 and producer R = 14 000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ give NPP = 10 000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹. NPP/GPP × 100 = 41.7%. This percentage describes retention after producer respiration; it is not the fraction of incoming sunlight captured. Calculating that fraction needs incoming light energy as the denominator.

## Consumer production: specify the boundary {#consumer-production}

Not all plant production is eaten. Of the food ingested by a consumer, some is egested in faeces, some chemical energy leaves in urine, and some is transferred during respiration. The remainder supports new biomass, including reproduction. Faeces represent material not assimilated; urine contains excreted metabolic products. Do not treat these as the same process.

$$N=I-(F+U+R).$$

| Symbol | Meaning in the consumer account |
| --- | --- |
| I | Chemical energy in ingested food |
| F | Chemical energy in faeces |
| U | Chemical energy in urine |
| R | Energy used in respiration |
| N | Net production of new consumer biomass |

![Original teaching energy budget: ingested energy branches into faeces, urine, respiration and net production. Arrows show destinations; widths are not quantitative. Decomposers can receive organic waste, but heat is not recycled into food.](/assets/images/a2-biology/bl03-energy-budget.svg)

**Worked account — Self-written; constructed data:** in one interval, I = 1000 kJ, F = 300 kJ, U = 40 kJ and R = 500 kJ. Net production is 160 kJ. The percentage of **ingested** energy retained is 16%. If the question instead asks for production relative to **assimilated** energy and defines assimilation as I − F, use 700 kJ as the denominator, giving 22.9%. Read the definition rather than selecting whichever percentage is familiar.

Between adjacent trophic levels, a production-transfer efficiency can be defined as next-level production divided by previous-level production, multiplied by 100. Keep the area, time and energy units the same. The value is not universally 10%.

$$\text{transfer efficiency}=\frac{\text{production at next trophic level}}{\text{production at previous trophic level}}\times100\%.$$

Energy used in respiration ultimately becomes less available for biological work, with much dissipated as heat. Energy is conserved, not destroyed. Faeces, dead tissue and uneaten material can support decomposer food webs. Their elements can cycle back to plants, but the heat is not recycled by photosynthesis: ecosystems need continuing energy input.

**Self-written / 自拟题 — EN1**

Complete the explanation: an animal eats only some of the available plant biomass; some ingested material is ______; some assimilated energy is used in ______. Explain why this limits the number of trophic levels a given producer energy supply can support.
<!-- /task:EN1 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN1</summary>

Follow energy past the gut and then into metabolism. Avoid saying that energy vanishes.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN1</summary>

Some is egested in faeces; some is used in respiration. Consequently only a fraction becomes new biomass for the next consumer. Successive transfers leave less production available to support higher levels, so a fixed producer supply cannot sustain an unlimited chain.

</details>

## Farming: explain a mechanism and its costs {#farming}

Removing weeds can reduce competition for light, water and minerals. Pest control can reduce crop consumption; disease control can preserve productive tissue. These interventions can increase the share of production available to people, but do not make all incoming light usable. Fertiliser can remove a mineral limitation; irrigation can remove a water limitation. Their gains depend on which factor currently limits growth.

Chemical pesticides may act rapidly but can harm non-target organisms, select for resistance and require repeated applications. Biological control uses a pest's natural enemy; establishment takes time and the agent may affect other species. Integrated pest management combines monitoring, thresholds and several methods to reduce unnecessary treatment. Removing too many species can simplify food webs and weaken other services, such as pollination or natural pest control.

For livestock, reducing excessive movement and maintaining a suitable temperature can reduce energy spent on activity and thermoregulation. More assimilated energy may then be available for growth. Yet heating uses external energy, crowded housing can increase disease transmission, and severe movement restriction affects welfare. Food conversion, financial profit and whole-system energy efficiency are different measures. Diet digestibility, mortality, veterinary costs and environmental waste also matter.

Eating producers directly often avoids losses through an extra trophic level, but a particular farming decision also depends on land unsuitable for crops, human nutritional needs, usable by-products and local circumstances. Make a conditional judgement from the stated evidence.

**Self-written / 自拟题 — EN2**

In a constructed grassland budget, herbivore net production is 600 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹ and represents 12% of producer NPP. Producer respiration is 7000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹. Find NPP and GPP. State why adding herbivore respiration to NPP would not calculate producer GPP.
<!-- /task:EN2 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN2</summary>

Work backwards from 12% before applying the producer equation.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN2</summary>

NPP = 600/0.12 = 5000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹. GPP = 5000 + 7000 = 12 000 kJ m⁻² year⁻¹. Producer GPP includes the producers' own respiratory use; herbivore respiration belongs to a different organism group and energy account.

</details>

<!-- task:EN3 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — EN3**

Two constructed livestock budgets, per animal over the same 30 days, are shown below. Calculate net production and N/I × 100 for each. Evaluate whether system B is necessarily the better system for a farm.

| System | I / MJ | F / MJ | U / MJ | R / MJ | External heating / MJ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 120 | 35 | 5 | 60 | 0 |
| B | 120 | 35 | 5 | 45 | 30 |

Include the difference between animal-level feed efficiency and whole-system energy use, and one welfare consideration.
<!-- /task:EN3 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN3</summary>

External heating is not a term in the animal's ingested-food budget. It still matters when defining the farm's total inputs.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN3</summary>

A: N = 20 MJ and N/I = 16.7%. B: N = 35 MJ and N/I = 29.2%. B retains more ingested energy as biomass, consistent with lower respiratory costs. A simple broader ratio N/(I + heating) is 16.7% for A and 23.3% for B; this still omits other farm inputs. Profit requires energy prices, sale value and other costs. Housing may improve thermal comfort but restricting movement or crowding can harm welfare or spread disease. The table alone cannot decide the best system.

</details>

<!-- task:EN4 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — EN4**

A farm starts with an empty building on day 1. Each complete production cycle, including cleaning, lasts 36 days and yields 4000 saleable birds. Calculate the number sold from complete cycles in 365 days. Explain why rounding 365/36 to the nearest whole number can give an invalid production estimate.
<!-- /task:EN4 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN4</summary>

Only a finished cycle supplies the stated saleable output.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN4</summary>

Ten complete cycles fit: 10 × 36 = 360 days. Output = 40 000 birds. Any unfinished cycle cannot contribute a full 4000 birds. Use the number of complete cycles, not an unrestricted rounding rule.

</details>

<!-- task:EN5 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — EN5**

Retry: a pesticide raises harvest but also reduces insect pollinators. Give a mechanism for a possible short-term benefit and a longer-term cost. State one measurement needed before recommending repeated use.
<!-- /task:EN5 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint EN5</summary>

Separate tissue lost to feeding from reproduction dependent on pollinators.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer EN5</summary>

Killing crop-feeding pests can immediately reduce biomass loss. Fewer pollinators may later lower fruit or seed production in pollinator-dependent crops. Monitor pest damage, pollinator abundance and reproductive yield in treated and comparable untreated plots over several seasons, alongside non-target effects and costs.

</details>
