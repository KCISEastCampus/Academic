## Build a testable comparison {#design}

The **independent variable** is the treatment you deliberately change. Define the **dependent variable** as something you can record, including its unit and observation period. “Growth” is too vague: change in shoot length over seven days and dry mass after seven days answer different questions. A **control treatment** supplies a baseline; **controlled variables** are other conditions kept comparable so that they do not explain the difference.

The **experimental unit** is the smallest unit independently assigned a treatment. If a whole pot receives a watering treatment, the pot is the treatment unit. Ten seedlings in one pot provide subsamples, not ten independently treated pots. Reading one seedling three times can check measurement consistency, but does not sample three organisms.

In the [RP9 handbook exemplar](#rp9), each dish receives one sowing density. The dish is the experimental unit. Seeds in a dish share moisture and space. To estimate variation between treatment units at the same density, use more than one independently treated dish per density.

Random **sampling** helps represent a population; random **allocation** helps distribute pre-existing differences between treatments. Neither guarantees that groups are identical. Blocking can compare treatments within similar starting conditions, such as a shelf or donor plant. A blind measurement code can reduce expectation bias. Record exclusions before seeing which result they favour; investigate an outlier rather than deleting it automatically.

Use these distinctions when evaluating results:

| Term | Meaning in a specific method | What an improvement can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | Closeness to the true/reference value | Calibration can correct a balance offset; more readings alone cannot |
| Precision | Small spread in repeated measurements under stated conditions | A more readable scale may reduce reading uncertainty |
| Repeatability | Agreement with the same method, operator and equipment | Use consistent timing and criteria for deciding when to stop |
| Reproducibility | Agreement when relevant conditions, such as operator or apparatus, change | Compare independent groups using a sufficiently specified method |
| Biological variation | Real differences between organisms or treatment units | Sample independent units and show the spread; do not call all variation “error” |

A useful evaluation names **the problem → its effect on the measurement or inference → a feasible change → a remaining limit**. Repeating a leaky gas collection method does not repair the leak.

## RP1 · Temperature and trypsin {#rp1}

[OxfordAQA student method, pp. 56–57](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=56)

**Safety.** Wear splash goggles and use teacher-prepared trypsin solution. Proteases can damage eyes and skin and may trigger allergic reactions. Keep hot water baths stable and handle tubes with the teacher's approved holder to avoid scalds.

- **1–3.** Mark an X at the same height on three tubes to standardise the visual threshold. Add 10 cm³ milk suspension to each to standardise substrate amount. In three separate tubes, combine 2 cm³ trypsin with 2 cm³ pH 7 buffer to keep enzyme amount and pH comparable before contact with the milk.
- **4–5.** Put all six tubes in the 20 °C bath for 10 min so the milk and buffered enzyme reach the test temperature before mixing.
- **6–9.** Combine one pair to start the reaction, stopper and invert about five times for even mixing, then return it to the bath to maintain temperature. Use the remaining pairs for repeat measurements at the same setting.
- **10–11.** Time from mixing to the first sight of the X and record each time individually. As trypsin breaks down casein, the suspension clears. The common threshold makes times comparable and individual records show spread.
- **12–14.** Repeat at 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C with the same method. Tabulate individual times and plot a processed rate proxy such as 1/time against temperature. This compares how quickly each mixture reaches the same degree of clarity.

**Self-written practice.** One group puts room-temperature reagents straight into a 50 °C bath and mixes immediately. Explain why its recorded time cannot be interpreted as a reaction time at 50 °C.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Distinguish the bath temperature from the temperature inside the tube.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

The reaction begins while the mixture is warming, so the endpoint time includes reaction at changing temperatures. Let both reagents reach the target temperature separately, then mix and start timing.

</details>

## RP2 · Potato tissue water potential {#rp2}

[OxfordAQA student method, pp. 60–61](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=60)

**Safety.** Use the cutter and scalpel on a stable tile and cut away from fingers to reduce the risk of cuts.

- **1–3.** Label six tubes spanning 0.0 to 1.0 mol dm⁻³ sucrose in 0.2 steps. Use stock and water to make 20 cm³ per tube, including water alone at 0.0. Check each reaches 30 °C in the bath. This keeps identity, volume and temperature comparable across the concentration series.
- **4–5.** Cut six peeled chips from one potato with the same cutter and length to standardise geometry and tissue source. Blot without squeezing so surface liquid has minimal effect. Match chip labels to tubes and weigh each chip for its own baseline.
- **6–7.** Transfer chips promptly into their matching tubes, time 20 min, then blot as before and reweigh. Equal exposure and consistent blotting make mass changes comparable.
- **8–9.** Calculate 100 × (final mass − initial mass)/initial mass to account for differences in starting mass. Plot this against concentration and estimate the zero crossing, where the solution has approximately the same water potential as the tissue.

**Self-written practice.** A chip goes from 2.40 g to 2.28 g after blotting. Calculate its percentage mass change. Predict how weighing it with solution still on its surface would affect the result.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Use the initial mass as the denominator. Which weighing includes the extra liquid?

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

The change is 100 × (2.28 − 2.40)/2.40 = −5.0%. Retained liquid raises the final reading, making the percentage change less negative. Use the same blotting procedure before both weighings.

</details>

## RP3 · Leaf pigment chromatography {#rp3}

[OxfordAQA student method, pp. 64–65](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=64)

**Safety.** Wear splash goggles and work in a well-ventilated room or fume cupboard. Keep the flammable solvent away from ignition sources, stopper the tubes between operations to reduce vapour exposure and use the school's solvent waste procedure.

- **1–2.** Label two tubes A and B, each with 3 cm³ of the same solvent. Stopper them to limit evaporation. Label the paper and draw an origin 2 cm above its bottom in pencil. Pencil provides a reference without introducing soluble ink.
- **3–4.** Cut a disc from leaf A away from major veins for comparable leaf tissue. Crush it with a glass rod at the origin to release pigments onto a defined starting spot.
- **5.** Hang the paper with its tip in solvent and the origin above the surface. This lets solvent climb towards the spot without washing pigments straight into the reservoir. Keep the tube still to avoid disturbing the run.
- **6–8.** Stop the run just before the front reaches the top and mark the front immediately before evaporation obscures it. Replace the stopper to limit solvent vapour. Dry the paper and outline spots to preserve their positions.
- **9–10 and calculation.** Repeat with leaf B under the same conditions. For each spot divide the distance from the origin to the spot centre by the distance from the origin to the solvent front. This gives a relative migration distance for comparison.

**Self-written practice.** A spot travels 36 mm and the solvent front travels 60 mm. Calculate Rf and evaluate a claim that a spot with the same Rf in a different solvent must be the same pigment.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Consider what influences a pigment's migration relative to the solvent front.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

Rf = 36/60 = 0.60, with no unit. Different solvents can change relative migration. Run both samples and a known reference under the same conditions. A match supports identification, while co-migration of different pigments remains possible.

</details>

## RP4 · Root-tip mitosis {#rp4}

[OxfordAQA student method, p. 69](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=69)

**Safety.** Hydrochloric acid is corrosive. Wear splash goggles and have the teacher dispense the acid into the beaker on its bench mat. Leave the beaker on the mat to reduce spill risk. Press the coverslip gently to avoid breaking the glass.

- **1–3.** Prepare the labelled work area and use the teacher-approved hydrochloric acid setup on a bench mat to contain spills. Expose about 2 cm of root tip to acid for 15 min to soften the tissue. Set up the microscope during this time so it is ready for the prepared slide.
- **4–5.** Rinse with distilled water to remove acid. Take the terminal 1 mm for the slide to sample tissue rich in dividing cells.
- **6–8.** Stain with toluidine blue for chromosome contrast. Tease the tissue apart with a mounted needle and gently spread it under a coverslip using folded filter paper to blot excess stain. A thinner preparation reduces cell overlap.
- **9–11.** Score several fields of view to broaden the sample. Tabulate dividing and total cells, then calculate dividing cells ÷ total cells to estimate the fraction in mitosis.

**Self-written practice.** A student chooses only microscope fields where chromosomes are easy to spot. Predict the effect on the mitotic index and propose a sampling rule.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Consider which cells are more likely to enter the numerator when fields are chosen this way.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

Fields rich in dividing cells can be overrepresented, inflating the index. Choose fields with a pre-set rule independent of chromosome visibility, such as a systematic traverse with a random start, and count all scorable cells in those fields.

</details>

## RP5 · Exercise and pulse recovery {#rp5}

[OxfordAQA student method and timing diagrams, pp. 71–72](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=71)

**Safety.** Participation is voluntary. Follow school screening for exercise restrictions and offer a data-analysis alternative. Use a stable bench and clear floor to reduce falls. Stop exercise and inform the teacher if you feel dizzy, have pain or become unusually breathless.

- **1–3.** Rest seated for 10 min to reduce the effect of recent activity on the baseline. Remain seated and locate the wrist pulse with your index finger. Count for 20 s at 1 min intervals for 5 min to check the stability of the resting baseline.
- **4.** Perform repeated supported squats for a chosen duration of at most 5 min, subject to teacher approval. The exercise provides the change in activity. Record its duration to interpret the response.
- **5.** Start counting immediately after stopping and use 20 s windows at 30 s intervals for 5 min. Immediate, more frequent sampling captures the initial elevation and subsequent recovery.
- **6–7.** Record counts and measurement times. Multiply each 20 s count by three to express pulse in beats per minute and plot it against time to compare baseline and recovery.

**Self-written practice.** A participant has 24 beats in the first 20 s after exercise. Estimate the pulse in beats per minute. Compare the effect of a one-beat counting error and the effect of ongoing recovery if the count is extended to 60 s.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the multipliers used to convert counts to beats per minute and the time each count represents.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

The estimate is 72 beats per minute. One beat changes the 20 s estimate by 3 beats per minute and the 60 s estimate by 1. The longer count averages over more of recovery and can give a lower estimate as pulse falls.

</details>

## RP6 · Potometer and water uptake {#rp6}

[OxfordAQA student method, pp. 75–76](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=75)

**Safety.** Wear goggles, keep fingers clear of the secateurs, and secure the stand. Ask the teacher to help with tight connections to avoid breaking the capillary. Wipe up basin spills promptly to prevent slips.

- **1–2.** Prepare a basin of water, immerse the cut end promptly, then recut about 5 cm above the first cut underwater to remove the segment where air may have entered the xylem.
- **3.** Flood the potometer and use the syringe as needed to displace bubbles. Close the tap to the syringe so the measurement uses the intended water pathway.
- **4–5.** Recut underwater where the stem fits tightly, keep the 6–12 leaves dry, and attach underwater to maintain the water column. Clamp the shoot and seal the joint with petroleum jelly to prevent movement and leaks.
- **6–7.** Dry the outside of the capillary for a clear reading and check movement towards the plant. Record temperature, the initial meniscus position and positions every 3 min for about 12 min on one running clock. These readings show uptake over elapsed time.
- **8–10.** Tabulate readings and plot displacement against time. Multiply displacement by the capillary cross-sectional area if reporting volume. Repeat under a different environmental condition, keeping other factors comparable. Uptake is a proxy for transpiration because the shoot may retain some water.

**Self-written practice.** Two shoots produce the same meniscus displacement in 12 min, but tube A has twice the internal radius of tube B. Compare the uptake rates and give a reason the rates may differ from transpiration.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Use cross-sectional area to convert movement to volume.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

Area is proportional to radius squared. With the same displacement and time, A gives four times the volume uptake rate. Some absorbed water can change tissue water storage, so uptake may differ from water lost to the air.

</details>

## RP7 · Light and photosynthesis {#rp7}

[OxfordAQA student method and setup diagrams, pp. 78–79](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=78)

**Safety.** Keep the lamp, plugs and leads clear of water to prevent electric shock. Switch off and let a hot bulb cool before handling it to avoid burns.

- **1–3.** Fill the tube with 1% sodium hydrogen carbonate solution as a carbon dioxide source. Cut the approximately 5 cm shoot at an angle under solution to limit air entry and expose the cut surface. Position the cut end upwards, about halfway down the tube, to observe rising bubbles consistently.
- **4–6.** Start 10 cm from the bulb. Allow 5 min for the plant's response to settle under the test light level, then count bubbles over 1 min. Repeat counts to assess variability under the same conditions.
- **7–10.** Take readings across 5–30 cm and use 1/d² as a relative light-intensity proxy. Tabulate and analyse the association with a suitable correlation test. Bubble counts estimate gas output and depend on bubble size. Lamp heating can also affect the response, so monitor temperature when comparing light levels.

**Self-written practice.** Counts level off at high light. Design a comparison to assess whether dissolved carbon dioxide availability limits the rate there. Give one other possible cause of the plateau.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Compare groups at the same high light level while changing one candidate limiting factor.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

Compare independent shoots at the same high light and temperature with the original and a higher carbon dioxide availability. Control or monitor pH, which may also change with dissolved carbon supply. Use the same observation interval and compare rates. An increase supports carbon dioxide limitation under the original conditions. Temperature or limits of the bubble-count proxy could also produce a plateau.

</details>

## RP8 · Temperature and yeast respiration {#rp8}

[OxfordAQA student method, p. 83](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=83)

**Safety.** Wear splash goggles when handling methylene blue and shaking tubes. Keep the water bath stable, and follow the school's spill and waste procedures for the dye.

- **1–5.** Set the bath to 35 °C, label five tubes, redisperse the yeast by shaking the yeast–glucose mixture and add 2 cm³ to each tube. Wait until the contents reach 35 °C and maintain the bath temperature. This makes starting sample volumes and temperatures comparable.
- **6–8.** Add 2 cm³ methylene blue to one tube, shake for 10 s, return it to the bath and note the time. Use the same initial mixing and avoid further shaking so extra aeration does not alter the reading. Time until the blue colour disappears. Reduction of the dye provides an indirect measure of metabolic activity.
- **9–10.** Repeat with the remaining four tubes to estimate within-temperature variation, then use the same sequence at other teacher-assigned temperatures to compare results. Tubes from the same mixture share its preparation history.

**Self-written practice.** Tubes at the higher temperature take longer to lose colour. The student shook only those tubes again partway through. Explain an alternative cause of the result and design a fair comparison.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Think about how further shaking changes oxygen availability.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

Extra shaking can introduce oxygen and delay persistent decolourisation. Use the same initial mixing at both temperatures and leave all tubes undisturbed afterwards. Use the same endpoint criterion and record times separately for each tube.

</details>

## RP9 · Sowing density and early growth {#rp9}

[OxfordAQA student method, pp. 85–86](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=85)

**Safety.** Use teacher-approved seeds, wash hands after handling them and ask the teacher to dispose of any mouldy dishes with the lids on to limit exposure.

- **1–4.** Mark sectors on each dish to distribute the seeds systematically. Give each dish equally wetted filter paper with no standing water to make initial moisture comparable while permitting gas exchange. Place one to five seeds per sector across dishes to change sowing density. Lid and place them together in darkness for comparable light exposure and reduced drying.
- **5–6.** Count germination at the same time daily for comparable intervals. After about three days, measure shoot and root growth to compare early growth at a common age.
- **7–8.** Record seeds sown, germinated and lengths by dish. Use germination proportions to compare unequal starting counts. The handbook asks for a statistical test. To support inference about density, replicate independently treated dishes at each density, since seeds in one dish share conditions.

**Self-written practice.** One dish has 8 of 10 seeds germinate and another has 12 of 20. Which has the higher germination proportion? Explain how to redesign the comparison to estimate variation at each density.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Use seeds sown as the denominator. Ask what received the density treatment independently.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

The first dish has 80% germination and the second has 60%. Randomly assign several independent dishes to each density and compare dish-level proportions. Seeds within one dish share an environment.

</details>

## RP10 · Root growth and gravity {#rp10}

[OxfordAQA student method and setup diagram, pp. 88–89](https://www.oxfordaqa.com/oaqaresources/science/teaching/a-level-practical-handbook-biology.pdf#page=88)

**Safety.** Use intact cases and secure upright ones in modelling clay to prevent tipping. Wash hands after handling seedlings and ask the teacher to dispose of any mouldy material.

- **1–4.** Fit and moisten blotting paper in six clear CD cases without standing water. Space four seeds in each away from edges for comparable moisture and room to grow. Number cases and label their edges Up, Down, Left and Right to keep directions traceable after rotation.
- **5–7.** Keep cases 1–2 upright as a fixed reference. Lay cases 3–4 flat so gravity acts perpendicular to the plane in which growth is observed, and raise the cases so excess water drains. Start cases 5–6 upright, rotating them 90° clockwise every two days to change gravity's direction relative to the case.
- **8–9.** Keep all cases in darkness to limit directional light effects. Check daily and add water with a dropper as needed without shifting seedlings, to maintain moisture and preserve their measured positions.
- **10–11.** Measure root angles daily using the same scale (Down 0°, Left 90°, Up 180°, Right 270°). Record angles by case, seed and day so the response can be related to the timing of rotations.

**Self-written practice.** Rotating cases are checked next to a window each day, and fixed cases are checked under dim, diffuse light. Explain a competing explanation for differences in root direction and specify a comparable observation procedure.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>

Identify what changed along with case orientation.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback</summary>

The groups also experienced different directional light. Keep both groups in darkness between observations, use matched brief observations under diffuse light, and record root direction against the marked axes and rotation times.

</details>

## Independent design and diagnosis {#independent-design}

**Self-written task.** In RP7, one student moves the lamp closer and immediately begins a 30 s bubble count. At the previous distance, they waited 5 min and counted for 1 min. Explain two changes needed to make the readings comparable. Then explain why the waiting period in RP1 serves a different purpose.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check your explanation</summary>

Allow the same 5 min after changing the light level so the new reading is less affected by the transition. Count for the same 1 min so each reading samples an equal time window. In RP1, the 10 min wait lets separately held reagents reach the bath temperature before they react.

</details>

**Self-written transfer task.** A student claims that growing plants in moving air has a lasting effect on shoot water uptake. Design a comparison that distinguishes the effect of air movement during growth from its immediate effect during measurement. Specify the experimental units, allocation, controls, raw records and one limitation.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint for the transfer task</summary>

Think of growth conditions and measurement conditions as two separate treatments.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback on the transfer task</summary>

Randomly assign independent growth chambers to moving or still air, using several chambers per condition and comparable light, humidity, temperature and water supply. Test shoots from each growth group under both measurement air speeds, allocating different shoots to the two speeds within each chamber. Use the same acclimatisation interval. Record chamber and shoot IDs, both air treatments, leaf area, capillary radius, meniscus positions and times. Compare water uptake rates between growth treatments at a common measurement speed and between speeds within growth treatments. Preserve chamber identity when summarising the results. A difference under common measurement conditions supports a persistent effect over the observed interval. Residual water status may contribute to it.

</details>

**Self-written diagnostic retry.** Six runs with the same potometer agree closely, but water escapes from a joint during every run. Explain what the agreement shows and propose a check before repeating the comparison.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint for the retry</summary>

Ask whether the same fault can affect every run in a similar way.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>Feedback on the retry</summary>

Agreement suggests repeatability under those conditions. The leak can systematically distort the meniscus movement as a measure of shoot uptake. Reseal the joint and check the apparatus for leakage using the teacher-approved setup before retaking the readings.

</details>
