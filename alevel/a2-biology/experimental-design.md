## Build a testable comparison {#design}

The **independent variable** is the treatment you deliberately change. Define the **dependent variable** as something you can record, including its unit and observation period. “Growth” is too vague: change in shoot length over seven days and dry mass after seven days answer different questions. A **control treatment** supplies a baseline; **controlled variables** are other conditions kept comparable so that they do not explain the difference.

The **experimental unit** is the smallest unit independently assigned a treatment. If a whole pot receives a watering treatment, the pot is the treatment unit. Ten seedlings in one pot provide subsamples, not ten independently treated pots. Reading one seedling three times can check measurement consistency, but does not sample three organisms.

![Teaching schematic: six separate pots, three assigned low density and three high density. Seedlings share soil within each pot; pot labels identify the independent treatment units.](/assets/images/a2-biology/bl05-experimental-units.svg)

*Original schematic, not a result: plants are not to scale. The boundary around each pot identifies a shared environment. Independent treatment replication is across pots.*

**Self-written / 自拟题 · Worked design.** To investigate density, randomly assign six identical pots to two densities, use seeds from the same batch, standardise soil and water per pot, and randomise positions. Record each pot's surviving count and individual heights; calculate a mean height for each pot, then compare the three pot means at each density. This separates variation between pots from variation within a pot. Three is a manageable starting design, not a universal adequate sample size: use pilot variability and the needed precision to plan more independent pots.

Random **sampling** helps represent a population; random **allocation** helps distribute pre-existing differences between treatments. Neither guarantees that groups are identical. Blocking can compare treatments within similar starting conditions, such as a shelf or donor plant. A blind measurement code can reduce expectation bias. Record exclusions before seeing which result they favour; investigate an outlier rather than deleting it automatically.

Use these distinctions when evaluating results:

| Term | Meaning in a specific method | What an improvement can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | Closeness to the true/reference value | Calibration can correct a balance offset; more readings alone cannot |
| Precision | Small spread in repeated measurements under stated conditions | A more readable scale may reduce reading uncertainty |
| Repeatability | Agreement with the same method, operator and equipment | Standardise timing and endpoint judgements |
| Reproducibility | Agreement when relevant conditions, such as operator or apparatus, change | Compare independent groups using a sufficiently specified method |
| Biological variation | Real differences between organisms or treatment units | Sample independent units and show the spread; do not call all variation “error” |

A useful evaluation names **the problem → its effect on the measurement or inference → a feasible change → a remaining limit**. Repeating a leaky gas collection method does not repair the leak.

## RP1 — enzyme activity and initial rate {#rp1}

**Question and principle.** How does substrate concentration affect catalase activity? Catalase speeds the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen by lowering activation energy. With a fixed amount of active enzyme, increasing substrate can increase successful binding until active sites become limiting. Measure the initial oxygen-production rate; a late total volume also depends on substrate exhaustion and assay duration. See [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/) and [gene expression](/alevel/a2-biology/gene-expression/) for protein production.

**Choose and carry out a method.** Use a teacher-prepared, filtered potato extract. For example, mix 8 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide solution, 1 cm³ buffer and 1 cm³ extract; stock peroxide concentrations 0, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00 and 1.25% give final assay concentrations 0, 0.20, 0.40, 0.80 and 1.00% on the same concentration basis. Keep total volume, extract volume, pH and temperature fixed. Equilibrate reagents in a 25°C water bath before mixing. Use a gas syringe with a freely moving plunger; start timing at mixing and record every 10 s for the first minute. Pilot the amount of enzyme so the early linear region is measurable. Run each concentration with at least three independent extract preparations; each preparation can supply the whole concentration series as a block. Randomise run order within a block.

**Record and analyse.** Record preparation ID, final concentration / %, temperature / °C, time / s and oxygen volume / cm³. Find the slope of the early linear region, in cm³ s⁻¹, for each run; then summarise independent preparations. A zero-substrate control tests whether oxygen collection depends on substrate. A reagent blank without extract checks non-enzyme gas production.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Loss of gas while fitting a bung can underestimate initial production; use a sealed mixing/start arrangement approved by the teacher. Foam height is an unreliable proxy if foam stability varies. Wear eye protection; the teacher prepares dilute peroxide, which can irritate eyes/skin. Avoid inhaling enzyme aerosols, use a stable water bath, and never seal a gas-producing reaction into a rigid vessel without a gas outlet.

## RP2 — osmosis and percentage mass change {#rp2}

**Question and principle.** At what external sucrose concentration does potato tissue show no net mass change under specified conditions? Water moves by osmosis across partially permeable membranes from higher to lower water potential. Dissolved solute makes water potential more negative; pressure also contributes in living cells. Zero net change suggests approximate balance under the assay conditions, not that water molecules stop moving or that the cell sap has the same sucrose concentration as the solution.

**Method choice.** Prepare equal-diameter cylinders of equal length with skin removed. From each of at least three potatoes, randomly allocate one cylinder to each of 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 mol dm⁻³ sucrose. First blot each cylinder with the same gentle procedure and measure its initial mass. Then fully submerge it in its assigned solution in a separate labelled tube and start timing; keep solution volume and temperature the same. After a piloted fixed period, such as 60 min, remove, blot in the same way and measure the final mass. Use a separate time-course pilot to check whether 60 min is sufficient for a stable mass: it cannot simply be assumed to be equilibrium.

**Record and analyse.** Record potato ID, concentration, initial and final mass / g and duration / min. Calculate percentage change relative to each cylinder's initial mass; plot mean change against concentration. Interpolate between points either side of zero and refine that interval. Fresh mass includes water; this is not a measure of new biomass production.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Unequal blotting adds variable surface liquid; excessive pressure may damage cells. Different potatoes vary, so preserve the donor blocks in analysis. Use a cork borer/scalpel on a board, cutting away from fingers with teacher supervision. Do not eat laboratory tissue or solutions; clean spills promptly.

## RP3 — chromatography and a conditional identity {#rp3}

**Question and principle.** Does a leaf extract contain separable pigments? A moving solvent carries molecules along paper or a thin-layer plate. Different affinities for the mobile and stationary phases produce different migration distances. Separation depends on the solvent and stationary phase as well as the pigments. A matching colour or $R_f$ supports an identification only under comparable conditions; it is not unique chemical proof. Connect pigment absorption to [photosynthesis](/alevel/a2-biology/photosynthesis/).

**Method choice.** Grind a fixed leaf mass with a small volume of teacher-selected extraction solvent. Draw a pencil baseline, apply a small extract spot and let it dry before repeated spotting. Keep the solvent below the baseline in a covered chamber; allow it to rise without disturbing the strip. Remove before the front reaches the top and immediately mark the solvent front. Run a known reference alongside the extract in the same chamber where possible; use separate extractions/strips to check reproducibility.

**Record and analyse.** Sketch the order and colour of bands. Measure from the baseline to each band centre and to the solvent front, in the same unit. Record solvent/stationary phase and extraction ID. Calculate pigment travel distance divided by solvent-front travel distance. A diffuse band gives a range of plausible centres; do not hide this by reporting many decimal places.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Large wet spots smear and reduce separation; dry several small applications. An unmarked evaporating front prevents reliable calculation. Organic solvents may be flammable and harmful by inhalation: use the school-approved small volumes and ventilation/fume cupboard, keep the chamber covered, remove ignition sources, wear eye protection and collect solvent waste separately. Do not heat solvent over a flame.

</details>

## RP4 — a root-tip squash and mitotic index {#rp4}

**Question and principle.** What fraction of cells in a specified root-tip region are in mitosis? A growing root tip contains dividing cells; more mature tissue may have a different cell-cycle distribution. Staining makes chromosomes visible and squashing separates overlapping cells. The mitotic index is the number of cells in mitosis divided by all countable cells, including interphase. It is a snapshot, not directly “divisions per minute”.

**Method choice.** Use young onion roots and the school's approved fixation/acid-softening protocol. Rinse after the prescribed treatment, isolate the terminal 2–5 mm, place it on a slide, add an approved chromosome stain and coverslip, then apply gentle vertical pressure through folded tissue. Do not slide the coverslip sideways. Locate the meristem at low power, then count with higher magnification. Sample several independent roots and non-overlapping fields using a preset route rather than selecting fields with many divisions.

**Record and analyse.** Record root/field ID and counts in interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase; record unclassifiable cells separately. Specify the classification rule before counting. Calculate an index per root and show between-root variation. A clear drawing should show observed structures, labels and a calibrated scale, without inventing detail absent at that resolution.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Overlap or poor staining makes the numerator and denominator uncertain. A longer mitotic phase can raise the index without raising cell production. Acid, some stains and hot liquids can injure eyes/skin: use teacher-prepared reagents, goggles and the approved temperature/time; handle hot tubes with a holder. Cut on a board; shield fingers from coverslip breakage and place broken glass in a sharps bin. See [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/) and [gene expression](/alevel/a2-biology/gene-expression/).

## RP5 — pulse rate during recovery {#rp5}

**Question and principle.** How does pulse rate change during recovery from a fixed, gentle activity? Activity changes muscle ATP demand and cardiovascular regulation; pulse rate estimates heart rate under suitable conditions. A count over an interval measures an average while the underlying rate may already be falling. See [homeostasis](/alevel/a2-biology/homeostasis/) and [muscles](/alevel/a2-biology/muscles/).

**Method choice.** With informed consent, let each participant sit quietly for a standard rest period and check a stable baseline. Choose a teacher-approved low-intensity activity, such as a fixed comfortable stepping cadence for one minute. Keep its duration and each participant's cadence constant. Record pulse in precisely defined recovery intervals, for example 0–15, 30–45, 60–75 and 120–135 s, using the same wrist-pulse technique or a checked sensor. Use the same posture. Participants supply independent biological observations; repeated recovery times from one participant are paired measurements, not different people.

**Record and analyse.** Use anonymous participant codes. Record baseline, actual activity duration, interval start/end and raw beat counts, then convert to beats min⁻¹. Plot a trajectory for each participant and summarise change from their own baseline. Repeated trials need full recovery and a standard rest; randomise order if comparing activities.

**Evaluate and control risk.** A 15 s count has less averaging over recovery but a one-beat counting error becomes 4 beats min⁻¹. A 60 s count has finer counting resolution but mixes early and late recovery. Participation is voluntary, with a non-exercise alternative. Screen through the school's procedure for health restrictions; avoid maximal effort and stop immediately for pain, dizziness or unusual breathlessness. Clear the floor, use a stable step and disinfect shared sensors. Do not publish names or infer medical diagnoses from classroom data.

</details>

## RP6 — water uptake with a potometer {#rp6}

**Question and principle.** How does air movement affect water uptake by a leafy shoot? Water evaporates from leaf surfaces and diffuses out, sustaining water movement through xylem. A potometer measures **water uptake**, used as an approximate proxy for transpiration when storage and other water uses are small and conditions are steady. It does not directly collect water vapour. See [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/) and [plant responses](/alevel/a2-biology/plant-responses/).

**Method choice.** Fill the apparatus with water, cut the shoot underwater and assemble underwater where appropriate to avoid air entering xylem. Seal joints and check for leaks. Introduce one bubble into the calibrated capillary by the approved method. Compare still air with measured low air speeds from a guarded fan; keep light, temperature and humidity comparable and allow acclimatisation. Measure displacement over a fixed interval and reset with the reservoir. Repeat conditions on several independent shoots, randomising order and retaining shoot IDs; leaf area can be measured for normalisation.

**Record and analyse.** Record shoot ID, air speed, leaf area, capillary internal radius, initial/final bubble position and elapsed time. Volume uptake is capillary cross-sectional area multiplied by displacement; divide by time, and by leaf area if needed. Compare within-shoot responses as paired data rather than treating every time interval as a new plant.

**Evaluate and control risk.** A leak can admit air or divert water, making the bubble a poor measure of shoot uptake; its error direction depends on the leak. Changes in stored water also weaken the transpiration proxy. Cut away from fingers, handle capillary glass carefully and let the teacher fit tight glass joints. Keep water away from electrical sockets/fans; wipe spills and secure the stand.

**Self-written / 自拟题 · RP6 decision**

A bubble moves 15 mm in 5 min along a capillary of internal radius 0.20 mm. Calculate the water-uptake rate in mm³ min⁻¹. Explain why the result should not be labelled an exact measurement of water lost by transpiration.
<!-- /task:rp6 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint — movement is a length, not a volume</summary>

Multiply the displacement by the circular cross-sectional area before dividing by time.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution — RP6 decision</summary>

$$\text{rate}=\frac{\pi(0.20)^2\times15}{5}=0.377\ \mathrm{mm^3\,min^{-1}}.$$

This is water entering the shoot. Some can change tissue water storage or be used in metabolism, and uptake may lag loss during changing conditions. Transpiration is an approximation only when these differences and leaks are negligible.

</details>

## RP7 — a limiting factor and net photosynthesis {#rp7}

**Question and principle.** How does light intensity affect oxygen production by an aquatic plant? Light supplies energy for photosynthesis; at high light another factor may limit the rate. Oxygen collected reflects net exchange because respiration also consumes oxygen. A plateau alone does not identify which factor is limiting. See [photosynthesis](/alevel/a2-biology/photosynthesis/).

**Method choice.** Use comparable healthy shoots of an approved aquatic species and a fixed bicarbonate solution. Set at least five measured light levels, with a dark baseline if appropriate. A light meter measures the exposure more directly than distance: $1/d^2$ is only an approximation for a suitable point-like source without complicating reflections. Use an LED and a controlled water bath to limit heating, maintaining temperature and solution composition. After equilibration, collect oxygen volume over a piloted fixed period, such as 5 min, with an appropriate calibrated apparatus. Repeat on independent shoots with randomised treatment order; preserve shoot IDs if each shoot receives every level.

**Record and analyse.** Record intensity with the meter's units, temperature, shoot size, elapsed time and oxygen volume. Compute net oxygen-production rates and plot against intensity. Use a suitable fitted trend rather than automatically connecting every noisy point. If counting bubbles, explain the untested assumption that their volumes are comparable; gas volume is preferable when collectable.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Some oxygen remains dissolved; early gas release may not equal early production. Allow equilibration and maintain common collection conditions. Keep electrical lamps away from water, avoid hot bulbs, handle cut plant/glass ends safely and do not release aquarium species or water into natural waterways.

## RP8 — respiration and what a gas change measures {#rp8}

**Question and principle.** How does temperature affect oxygen uptake by germinating seeds? In a sealed respirometer at stable temperature and pressure, a carbon dioxide absorbent removes produced CO₂, so gas-volume reduction can indicate oxygen uptake. Without the absorbent, net change combines oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide release. No net change is not evidence of no respiration. See [respiration](/alevel/a2-biology/respiration/) for gas balance and RQ.

**Method choice.** Use equal masses of the same species and germination stage in separate respirometers at, for example, 15, 20, 25 and 30°C after a pilot. Keep the CO₂ absorbent physically separated from living material by a secure barrier. Use a matched apparatus with inert beads of similar occupied volume to reveal physical pressure/temperature effects. Equilibrate in a water bath with the apparatus vented as specified by the school's design, then close and record capillary position at regular intervals for a short, oxygen-sufficient run. Use independently prepared seed batches per temperature. Leak-test first; do not deprive living material of oxygen in long closed runs.

**Record and analyse.** Record mass, temperature, time, internal capillary radius and displacement with an explicit direction convention. Correct comparable control drift in the same units and sign. Calculate a linear volume-change slope, then oxygen uptake per unit mass per unit time. A no-absorbent measurement can support RQ calculation only with comparable material, conditions and a non-zero oxygen-uptake estimate.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Temperature changes expand/contract gas even without respiration; thermal equilibration and a control address this. Gas leaks weaken the interpretation. CO₂ absorbents such as soda lime or alkali can be corrosive: the teacher handles small quantities, uses eye protection and follows the approved spill procedure. Keep absorbent away from organisms and avoid skin contact; secure glassware and use safe water-bath temperatures.

<!-- /task:rp8 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution — RP8 decision</summary>

The explanation requires matched conditions; the readings alone do not identify a unique respiratory substrate.

</details>

## RP9 — competition and the unit receiving density {#rp9}

**Question and principle.** Does increasing seedling density reduce growth per plant under a fixed resource supply per pot? Competition occurs when organisms use a shared limiting resource. Higher density may reduce resources available to each plant, yet total biomass per pot can still rise. These are different dependent variables. See [ecology](/alevel/a2-biology/ecology/) and [energy production](/alevel/a2-biology/energy-production/).

**Method choice.** Randomly assign at least four independent pots to each of three densities, for example 2, 6 and 12 similar germinated seedlings per pot. Use identical pots, equal soil, equal water/nutrients per pot and the same seed batch and initial stage. Equal water per plant would also change total resources per pot and answer a different question. Randomise pot positions and rotate a planned layout to reduce location effects without mixing pot identities. Follow for a fixed piloted period, such as 14 days; record initial and final counts and growth.

**Record and analyse.** Record pot ID, assigned density, survivors, individual shoot lengths and final dry mass. Dry tissue to constant mass with teacher-controlled equipment, cool consistently and reweigh. Calculate per-pot means and total dry mass, then compare independent pots. Reporting only surviving plants can hide mortality; report survival as a separate outcome and explain the denominator.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Shorter plants do not identify whether light, water or minerals caused competition. A follow-up resource intervention can discriminate mechanisms. Shared pots make seedlings dependent. Use clean commercial growing medium, cover cuts, wash hands, avoid inhaling dry compost and contain mouldy cultures for teacher disposal. Only the teacher handles hot drying trays; do not place unknown plastics in a drying oven.

## RP10 — direction of growth, not just elongation {#rp10}

**Question and principle.** Does orientation relative to gravity affect the direction of root and shoot growth? A tropism is directional growth relative to a stimulus. Unequal elongation on opposite sides produces curvature; roots and shoots can respond differently to IAA. Length alone cannot establish growth direction. See [plant responses](/alevel/a2-biology/plant-responses/).

**Method choice.** Germinate similar seedlings on moist paper. Randomly assign independent dishes to initially horizontal or vertical orientations in a dark chamber, keeping moisture and temperature comparable. Mark the initial root/shoot axis and gravity direction. Photograph each at the start and after a piloted period, such as 24 h, from the same camera direction with a scale. Brief observations must not create unequal directional illumination. Include several independent dishes per orientation; seedlings within a dish are subsamples. A slow clinostat can be a comparison if available, but changes the direction of gravity experienced over time rather than removing gravity.

**Record and analyse.** Record dish/seedling ID, organ, initial/final axis, time, length and signed angular change, defining positive/negative directions first. Compare roots separately from shoots and use dish-level summaries. A uniform elongation change is not the same as curvature; photographs should preserve the reference axis and orientation.

**Evaluate and control risk.** Unequal light or drying could confound orientation. Darkness helps isolate gravity, but does not make every other condition identical. Keep moist chambers away from electrical equipment, wash hands after handling seedlings and contain mould for teacher disposal. Secure any rotating apparatus and keep fingers away from its moving parts.

## Independent design and diagnosis {#independent-design}

<!-- task:design-transfer -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 · Independent transfer**

A student claims that plants grown in moving air develop a permanently higher rate of water uptake. Design an investigation that distinguishes a developmental effect of growth conditions from an immediate effect of air movement during measurement. Specify allocation, experimental units, measurements, controls, a record table and a limitation. Do not perform the investigation until the teacher approves the procedure.
<!-- /task:design-transfer -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint — separate growth treatment from measurement conditions</summary>

Plants can first grow in different conditions and later be measured in a common environment. Consider whether leaf area and the time allowed to acclimatise change the interpretation.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution — independent transfer</summary>

Randomly allocate comparable plants to moving/still-air growth treatments with independent treatment environments, keeping light, water supply, temperature and humidity comparable. If an entire chamber receives the treatment, replicate chambers, not merely plants inside one chamber. After the growth period, measure all shoots at the same air speed and other environmental conditions after a defined acclimatisation period. Record chamber and plant IDs, growth treatment, leaf area, capillary radius, displacement and time; calculate water uptake per time and, where appropriate, per leaf area. Compare independent treatment units and show variation. A second measurement air speed, with randomised order, could test immediate responses too.

A difference in common measurement conditions supports a persisting treatment effect over that measurement period. It does not establish permanence or identify anatomy as the cause: water status and other carry-over effects may remain. Use RP6 risk controls, inspect for leaks and avoid unsupported causal claims about a specific mechanism.

</details>

| Calls a proxy the process itself | State what is measured and the conditions needed for inference |
| Recommends an improvement without an effect | Explain which reading or alternative explanation the change addresses |

**Self-written / 自拟题 · Diagnostic retry**

An enzyme assay is repeated ten times with the same leaking gas syringe. Results have a small spread. Explain whether this establishes accuracy, and propose one check that addresses the main problem.
<!-- /task:design-retry -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint — agreement is not a reference value</summary>

The same loss mechanism can recur in every trial.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution — diagnostic retry</summary>

A small spread supports precision under those conditions, not accuracy. Repeated gas loss can consistently underestimate collection. Leak-test the syringe and joints, repair or replace the faulty part and check recovery of a known gas volume using the approved setup before repeating the biological assay.

</details>
