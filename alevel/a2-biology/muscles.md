## Structure at four scales {#structure}

A skeletal muscle attaches to bone by tendons and contains bundles of muscle fibres. Each **muscle fibre** is a long multinucleate cell with a surface membrane (sarcolemma), many mitochondria and many **myofibrils**. The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) stores Ca²⁺. Transverse tubules carry the membrane excitation into the fibre, helping coordinate release from the SR throughout its depth.

Myofibrils contain repeating **sarcomeres**, from one Z line to the next. Thin actin filaments attach to Z lines; thick myosin filaments lie centrally, with projecting heads that can bind actin. The **A band** spans the full length of thick filaments. The **I band** contains thin filaments without thick filaments; the **H zone** is the central thick-only region.

During shortening, Z lines move closer together, overlap increases, and I bands and the H zone become narrower (the H zone can disappear). A-band width and the lengths of individual actin and myosin filaments remain constant. Filament sliding can also generate tension without measurable overall shortening when an external load prevents movement.

## Why calcium permits contraction and ATP permits cycling {#mechanism}

At rest, **tropomyosin** covers myosin-binding sites on actin. A muscle action potential triggers Ca²⁺ release from the SR into the sarcoplasm. Ca²⁺ binds to **troponin**, changing its shape and shifting tropomyosin so binding sites are exposed. Calcium binds troponin, not the ATP-binding site on myosin.

The cycle can be followed from an energised myosin head carrying ADP and phosphate:

1. The head binds to exposed actin, forming an actomyosin cross-bridge.
2. Phosphate release is associated with the power stroke; the head changes angle and pulls actin towards the sarcomere centre. ADP leaves.
3. A new ATP molecule binds to myosin, lowering its affinity for actin so the head detaches.
4. ATP is hydrolysed; the released energy returns the head to its energised configuration. It can attach again farther along actin if Ca²⁺ remains high.

When excitation stops, ATP-dependent pumps return Ca²⁺ to the SR. Ca²⁺ leaves troponin and tropomyosin covers the binding sites again. Relaxation therefore requires effective calcium removal and cross-bridge release; it is not simply “ATP runs out”. ATP has distinct roles in detachment, re-energising the head and active calcium transport.

## Interpret a structural measurement {#measurement}

**Worked example · Self-written / 自拟题:** a sarcomere shortens while its A band stays unchanged. Choose the thick-filament length as the internal comparison: unchanged A-band width means that thick filaments did not shorten. Decreased Z-to-Z distance with greater overlap supports sliding. In real images, compare scale bars and section orientation before inferring a biological change.

## Whole-muscle movement and energy supply {#energy}

Muscles pull rather than actively push. In an **antagonistic pair**, contraction of one muscle moves a joint in one direction while the opposing muscle can move it back. For example, elbow flexors and extensors act on a rigid, effectively incompressible skeleton through tendons. Relaxing one muscle does not itself supply an active force to reverse the movement. Co-contraction can stabilise a joint.

The small ATP store must be replenished continuously. **Phosphocreatine** transfers a phosphate group to ADP rapidly, regenerating ATP without directly requiring oxygen. Its store is limited; during recovery, ATP from respiration helps restore it. Glycolysis provides ATP rapidly, while aerobic respiration supports sustained production when fuel and oxygen are supplied. Phosphocreatine does not pull actin directly.

| Property | Slow oxidative fibres | Fast fibres, especially fast glycolytic types |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mitochondria, capillaries, myoglobin | Usually abundant; sustained aerobic ATP supply | Often fewer in fast glycolytic fibres; fast oxidative types also exist |
| Force development and fatigue | Slower contraction, greater fatigue resistance | Rapid contraction and high power; glycolytic types fatigue sooner |
| Typical contribution | Posture and endurance, e.g. substantial slow-fibre contribution in soleus | Rapid movements and sprinting, e.g. mixed limb muscles recruited for high power |

Most muscles contain mixtures. Recruitment, fibre size, training, oxygen supply and activity duration all matter; “fast means no aerobic respiration” is false.

Treatment A keeps cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ high while ATP remains available. Treatment B removes ATP while some myosin heads are attached to actin. Compare the immediate mechanical predictions and explain why “no ATP means instant relaxation” is incorrect.
<!-- /task:BL04-M4 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>M4 · Hint</summary>

Separate whether binding sites are exposed from whether attached heads can detach.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>M4 · Explanation</summary>

A keeps binding sites accessible, so ATP-supported cycling and tension can continue, subject to load and other conditions. In B, attached heads cannot detach normally without ATP; stiffness can persist even though further cycling fails. ATP-dependent Ca²⁺ pumping also fails. Thus ATP depletion does not cause immediate relaxation.

</details>
