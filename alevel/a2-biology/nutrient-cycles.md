## Track an element through a boundary {#start}

Plants require carbon, nitrogen and other elements to make biomass. Harvesting removes atoms from a field; sunshine cannot replace nitrogen atoms. Decomposition and uptake reconnect organisms with inorganic reservoirs. The goal is to name the chemical form, the process and the conditions at each arrow, then use those mechanisms to evaluate agriculture and climate claims. Review proteins, membranes and transfers in [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/#proteins).

A crop is harvested and removed from a field. Explain why recycling sunlight is not a way to replace the nitrogen removed, and name two nitrogen-containing classes of molecule in the crop.
<!-- /task:N0 -->

<summary>Hint N0</summary>

Distinguish an energy input from an input of atoms.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer N0</summary>

Sunlight transfers energy but contains no nitrogen atoms to replace those removed. Proteins and nucleic acids contain nitrogen; nitrogen must return through material inputs such as fertiliser, fixation or organic residues.

</details>

## Decomposition connects organic material to inorganic forms {#decomposition}

Saprobiontic microorganisms, including fungi and bacteria, secrete enzymes onto dead organisms and wastes. These enzymes hydrolyse large molecules outside cells into smaller soluble molecules, which the microorganisms absorb and use. Their respiration releases CO₂, while breakdown of nitrogen-containing compounds can release ammonium. Detritivores fragment material and increase the surface available to microorganisms; fragmentation and microbial extracellular digestion are different contributions.

Decomposition depends on temperature, moisture, oxygen supply and the properties of the material. Warmth can accelerate enzyme-controlled reactions within a suitable range; too little water restricts enzyme activity and diffusion. Waterlogged material can lack oxygen, reducing aerobic decomposition and favouring different microbial pathways. “More water means faster decomposition” is not a universal rule.

Producers take up inorganic substances and incorporate them into organic molecules. Feeding, digestion and assimilation transfer elements between organisms. Matter is transformed and transferred; it does not become energy.

## Carbon: short cycles and long-term stores {#carbon}

Photosynthesis removes CO₂ from air or water and incorporates its carbon into organic compounds. Plants, animals and decomposers return CO₂ through respiration. Feeding transfers organic carbon; death and waste transfer it to detrital pathways. Some carbon enters longer-lived stores in wood, soils, sediments or fossil fuels when storage exceeds release over long periods.

Seasonal growth can increase net CO₂ uptake, while reduced photosynthesis and continuing respiration can reverse the balance. Regional seasons and vegetation differ; a seasonal fall at one monitoring site does not show that the long-term global trend has reversed. Compare equivalent months or remove a seasonal pattern before interpreting a multi-year trend.

Combustion of fossil fuels moves carbon from long-term geological stores to the atmosphere. Clearing vegetation can release stored carbon and reduce future uptake; soil disturbance can change decomposition. Replanting can increase uptake, but the rate, permanence and previous land use matter. A newly planted tree does not instantly replace all carbon lost from a mature ecosystem.

### Greenhouse gases and conditional crop predictions {#climate}

CO₂ and methane absorb outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it, changing Earth's energy balance. Increasing their concentrations can enhance the greenhouse effect. This is distinct from ozone depletion. Methane can be produced by microorganisms in anaerobic environments, including waterlogged soils and ruminant digestive systems.

Higher CO₂ may increase photosynthesis if CO₂ is limiting, but growth can still be constrained by water, nitrogen or temperature. Warming may lengthen a growing season in one setting, exceed a crop's optimum in another, or increase evaporation and drought stress. Warmer conditions may accelerate an insect's development and allow additional generations or overwinter survival within its tolerances; extreme heat can instead reduce survival.

A modelled yield change depends on assumptions about these factors, pests, adaptation and farming practice. A predicted response is not an observed future harvest. State the crop, region, timescale, baseline and uncertainty before generalising.

## Nitrogen: five processes with different directions {#nitrogen}

Most plants cannot directly use atmospheric N₂. Nitrogen fixation converts it into biologically usable reduced nitrogen. Free-living or symbiotic bacteria, including those in legume root nodules, carry out fixation; the host can provide carbohydrate and suitable conditions, while the bacteria supply fixed nitrogen compounds. The nodule's low free-oxygen conditions help protect nitrogenase, although energy for fixation is still required.

| Process | Change in chemical form | Mechanism or condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen fixation | N₂ → reduced nitrogen, incorporated as NH₄⁺/organic N | Fixing microorganisms; energy required |
| Ammonification | Organic N → NH₄⁺ | Decomposition of nitrogen-containing compounds |
| Nitrification | NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ | Oxidation by nitrifying microorganisms; aerobic conditions |
| Assimilation | NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻ → organic N | Uptake and synthesis of amino acids and other compounds; nitrate is reduced before incorporation |
| Denitrification | NO₃⁻ → gaseous nitrogen, including N₂ | Denitrifying microorganisms under oxygen-limited conditions |

![Original nitrogen-cycle teaching diagram with labelled chemical forms and directional arrows for fixation, ammonification, nitrification, assimilation and denitrification. It is a process map, not a quantitative flux chart.](/assets/images/a2-biology/bl03-nitrogen-cycle.svg)

Nitrification and nitrogen fixation are not interchangeable names: nitrification oxidises existing reduced nitrogen in soil; fixation brings atmospheric nitrogen into the biologically available pool. Denitrification returns nitrogen to the atmosphere. Plants can use nitrate or ammonium, depending on the species and conditions, but do not convert atmospheric N₂ into nitrate simply by root absorption.

**Mechanism demonstration — Self-written:** waterlogging reduces oxygen supply in soil. Aerobic nitrification can slow, while denitrification can increase as microorganisms use nitrate in oxygen-limited respiration. Nitrate can therefore become less available to crops. Low root oxygen can also reduce active ion uptake, so poor growth is not evidence for only one mechanism.

## Fertilisers, leaching and eutrophication {#fertilisers}

Harvesting crops and removing livestock export nutrients from a farm. Natural fertilisers such as manure return organic material and nutrients as decomposition proceeds; artificial fertilisers can supply specified soluble ions more directly. Compare timing, concentration, availability, soil structure, costs and losses. Manure can also pollute water; “natural” does not guarantee no environmental effect.

Nitrate is soluble and can be leached below roots or carried into water. Timing application to crop demand, avoiding saturated soil or heavy rainfall, using appropriate rates and retaining buffer vegetation can reduce losses. Fertiliser increases yield only while the supplied nutrient limits growth; excess input can add cost and pollution without proportional yield gain.

Eutrophication can follow enrichment with a limiting nutrient, often nitrogen or phosphorus. Algal growth increases, light reaching submerged plants falls, and dead material increases. Decomposers digest this material and respire, increasing oxygen demand. If oxygen consumption exceeds replacement by photosynthesis, diffusion and mixing, dissolved oxygen falls and aerobic animals may die. Algae also respire, especially when photosynthesis stops at night. Nitrate does not simply remove oxygen by reacting directly with it.

**Self-written / 自拟题 — N2**

After nutrient-rich runoff enters a lake, algae increase and fish deaths occur several days later. Write a mechanism linking nutrient input to low dissolved oxygen. Give one measurement and one comparison that would test the explanation, and explain why the time sequence alone does not prove causation.
<!-- /task:N2 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint N2</summary>

Include decomposition and respiration between organic material and oxygen loss.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer N2</summary>

Extra limiting nutrients promote algal growth; shading and later death increase dead plant/algal material. Decomposer respiration increases oxygen consumption; dissolved oxygen may fall below fish requirements. Measure dissolved oxygen repeatedly, including night/day readings, and compare similar water without the nutrient input or replicated controlled nutrient treatments. Temperature, toxins or disease might also cause deaths, so temporal order alone is insufficient.

</details>

<!-- task:N3 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — N3**

A constructed annual field budget has nitrogen inputs of 100 kg ha⁻¹ from fertiliser and 20 kg ha⁻¹ from fixation. Outputs are 80 kg ha⁻¹ in harvest, 15 kg ha⁻¹ by leaching and 10 kg ha⁻¹ by denitrification. Assume all other fluxes are negligible. Calculate the change in the field's nitrogen store. Explain why this change is not automatically equal to nitrate available to the next crop.
<!-- /task:N3 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint N3</summary>

Use input minus output, then distinguish total nitrogen from a particular chemical pool.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer N3</summary>

Change = 100 + 20 − 80 − 15 − 10 = +15 kg ha⁻¹ over the year. Some retained nitrogen may be in soil organic material, microbial biomass or ammonium; its availability depends on mineralisation, uptake and other transformations. The calculation is conditional on the stated closed account.

</details>

<!-- task:N4 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — N4**

A model predicts that a 2°C increase raises a crop's yield by 8% when water and nitrate are unlimited. A farmer concludes that warming will raise yield by 8% on every farm. Evaluate the conclusion using photosynthesis, water supply and insect life cycles. Identify what the 8% is measured relative to.
<!-- /task:N4 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint N4</summary>

Preserve the model's controlled conditions and baseline; then examine omitted constraints.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer N4</summary>

The prediction applies to the model crop and conditions, not every farm. Water shortage can close stomata and restrict CO₂ uptake; nitrogen or temperature limits may prevent the predicted increase. Insects may develop faster or survive winter better, increasing crop losses, though responses depend on species and temperature range. The 8% must be relative to the model's baseline yield before warming. Field data and realistic water/nutrient/pest scenarios are needed before a local prediction.

</details>

<!-- task:N5 -->
**Self-written / 自拟题 — N5**

Retry: a student says waterlogged soil always supplies more nitrate because bacteria grow better when wet. Give two nitrogen-cycle reasons to challenge the claim, naming the oxygen conditions and the direction of each process.
<!-- /task:N5 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint N5</summary>

Contrast nitrification with denitrification.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Answer N5</summary>

Waterlogging can lower oxygen, slowing aerobic nitrification from ammonium through nitrite to nitrate. Oxygen-limited conditions can favour denitrification from nitrate to nitrogen gases, removing nitrate. Moisture alone does not predict the direction of the net nitrate change.

</details>
