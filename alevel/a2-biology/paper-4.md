## Entry diagnosis {#diagnosis}

**Self-written task.**

> A hormone circulates in blood, but only some cells respond. Explain why. Predict what could happen if the hormone still binds to a target cell but the next intracellular step is blocked.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: separate recognition from the response pathway</summary>

Which protein recognises the hormone? Does binding guarantee that all later steps occur?

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Feedback and next step</summary>

Target cells have an appropriate receptor; cells lacking that receptor do not recognise that signal in the same way. Blocking a necessary intracellular step can reduce or prevent the downstream response even when binding occurs. The receptor is not automatically an enzyme's active site, and a response requires more than the hormone reaching the tissue. Start with [homeostasis](/alevel/a2-biology/homeostasis/) for a pathway example; repair protein specificity in the [AS bridge](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/).

</details>

## Learn to test a mechanism {#evidence}

For each mechanism, draw the normal sequence first. Then predict the result of blocking one step, changing the stimulus, or restoring a missing substance. State what the intervention actually tests. An association alone does not establish which step caused the response.

RP10 investigates the **direction** of root or shoot growth. A concentration–length experiment is useful, but does not by itself complete a directional-growth investigation. RP5 links pulse measurement to heart-rate control. Choose controls and independent experimental units using [experimental design](/alevel/a2-biology/experimental-design/).

## Independent comparison {#transfer}

**Self-written task.**

> Compare how an action potential and a plant growth response communicate information. Include the responding structures, the time course and how a larger response may be produced. Explain why a stronger stimulus does not necessarily produce a larger action-potential amplitude.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: compare the same features on each side</summary>

Use membrane ion channels versus hormone-dependent differences in growth. Separate the size of one electrical event from the number of events per second.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Feedback and retry</summary>

An action potential involves rapid changes in membrane permeability as voltage-gated channels open and close; above threshold, individual events are all-or-nothing. A stronger stimulus can increase action-potential frequency rather than amplitude. In a tropic plant response, hormone distribution and tissue sensitivity alter relative cell elongation over a longer time, producing curvature. The response depends on organ and concentration; increasing IAA is not a universal way to increase growth. Compare the same features explicitly, not two unrelated lists.

Retry: explain how a substance that blocks calcium entry at a presynaptic terminal can prevent muscle contraction even when an action potential reaches the terminal. Follow the connection through transmitter release to the muscle.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Check the blocked-calcium retry</summary>

Without the necessary calcium influx, synaptic vesicles do not release acetylcholine normally. The muscle membrane may not reach threshold, so a muscle action potential and the normal release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum do not follow. Tropomyosin then continues to restrict the actin binding sites, reducing cross-bridge cycling. The presynaptic and muscle calcium steps occur in different compartments.

</details>
