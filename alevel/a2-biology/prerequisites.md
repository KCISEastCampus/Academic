## Proteins, enzymes and rate {#proteins}

**Goal:** connect a change in protein structure to a functional effect, and distinguish a rate from a final amount.

A protein's amino acid sequence is its primary structure. Hydrogen bonding helps produce secondary structure; interactions between side chains help maintain the folded tertiary structure. Some functional proteins contain several polypeptide subunits. The sequence and folding determine which molecules can interact with the protein. Different proteins serve as enzymes, receptors, channels, carriers, antibodies or structural components; these roles are not interchangeable.

An enzyme's active site binds a suitable substrate to form an enzyme–substrate complex. Binding may induce a conformational change. The enzyme lowers the activation energy of the reaction and is not used up. Raising temperature initially increases molecular kinetic energy and successful collisions. At sufficiently high temperatures, disruption of interactions maintaining the active site reduces its functional shape; this is denaturation, not “the enzyme dies”. A pH change can alter charges and interactions. Both substrate binding and catalysis may be affected.

At fixed enzyme concentration, increasing substrate concentration can increase initial rate until active sites are occupied for much of the time. More substrate then has little effect on the limiting rate. A competitive inhibitor competes for an active site; increased substrate concentration can reduce its effect. A non-competitive inhibitor acts elsewhere and reduces functional enzyme activity; extra substrate does not simply restore all lost activity.

**Worked measurement.** A reaction produces 12 cm³ of gas in the first 20 s:

$$\text{mean rate over the interval}=\frac{12}{20}=0.60\;\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}.$$

This is an interval mean. The initial rate is the gradient at the start of the volume–time curve; a sufficiently short initial interval can estimate it. Use a tangent or early linear section when supported by the data. Do not call an endpoint volume a rate without time.

> An enzyme reaction produces 18 cm³ of gas in the first 30 s and another 6 cm³ in the next 30 s. Calculate the mean rate in each interval. Suggest a reason for the change without assuming that the enzyme has denatured.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: use the gas produced within each interval</summary>

The second interval contributes 6 cm³, not the cumulative 24 cm³. Consider substrate availability.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution, diagnosis and retry</summary>

The rates are 0.60 and 0.20 cm³ s⁻¹. Substrate depletion could reduce enzyme–substrate complex formation; product inhibition could also matter in a suitable reaction. The data alone do not establish denaturation. If you used 24/30, you mixed a cumulative reading with an interval duration.

Retry: two reactions finish with the same total gas volume. Explain why they may still have different initial rates. Return to [photosynthesis](/alevel/a2-biology/photosynthesis/), [respiration](/alevel/a2-biology/respiration/) or [gene technology](/alevel/a2-biology/gene-technology/).

</details>

## ATP and electron carriers {#energy-carriers}

ATP is continually regenerated from ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP hydrolysis can be coupled to processes such as active transport or biosynthesis; transfer of a phosphate can also change another molecule's reactivity. Energy is transferred between processes, while atoms remain in molecules. ATP is an immediate energy-transfer molecule, not a permanent supply that a cell uses only once.

Oxidation is loss of electrons, and reduction is gain of electrons. In many biological reactions, removal or addition of hydrogen provides a way to follow that transfer. When a substrate loses hydrogen to NAD, the substrate is oxidised and NAD is reduced. Reduced NAD carries reducing power to later reactions and must be reoxidised for the carrier to be reused. NADP is a related carrier used in photosynthesis; keep the named coenzyme matched to the pathway. The carrier transfers electrons rather than turning matter into energy.

> A substrate transfers hydrogen to NAD. Identify which molecule is oxidised and which is reduced. Explain why a finite pool of NAD cannot support this process indefinitely if reduced NAD is never reoxidised.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: follow the transferred electrons and the available carrier</summary>

Identify the acceptor, then consider how much oxidised NAD remains after repeated transfers.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Feedback</summary>

The substrate is oxidised; NAD is reduced. Without regeneration, oxidised NAD becomes unavailable to accept further hydrogen/electrons, so the NAD-dependent oxidation cannot continue indefinitely even when substrate remains. The cell needs a route that reoxidises the carrier. See [respiration](/alevel/a2-biology/respiration/) for aerobic and anaerobic routes.

</details>

## Membranes, transport and water potential {#membranes}

Phospholipids form a bilayer with hydrophilic heads facing water and hydrophobic tails facing inward. The interior restricts ions and many polar solutes. Membrane proteins provide selective routes; their presence, number and state affect permeability.

| Process | Direction and route | Energy relationship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple diffusion | Net movement down a concentration gradient through the bilayer, for suitable molecules | No direct ATP use |
| Facilitated diffusion | Down a concentration/electrochemical gradient through a channel or carrier | No direct ATP use |
| Active transport | Transport against the relevant gradient using a transporter | Requires an energy source; some pumps use ATP directly |
| Osmosis | Net water movement from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane | Passive movement |

Solutes lower water potential; pressure also contributes in plant cells. Water entering a plant cell increases turgor pressure, which can oppose further entry. At equilibrium there is no **net** water movement, but water molecules continue moving both ways. A cell wall resists expansion; it does not act as the selectively permeable plasma membrane.

**Worked measurement.** A tissue sample increases from 2.00 g to 2.16 g:

$$\text{percentage mass change}=\frac{2.16-2.00}{2.00}\times100=8.0\%.$$

Blot surface liquid consistently before weighing. Use initial mass as the denominator so samples with different starting masses can be compared; equal percentage changes do not imply equal absolute gains.

> A plant tissue sample falls from 2.50 g to 2.20 g in a solution. Calculate its percentage mass change. Infer the initial direction of net water movement, and state an assumption needed to relate the mass change to osmosis.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: keep the sign and name the membrane</summary>

Use final minus initial mass. Ask whether loss of tissue or inconsistent surface liquid could also change the measurement.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution, diagnosis and retry</summary>

The change is −12%. Net water moved out of the tissue, consistent with lower water potential outside initially. This interpretation assumes the mass difference mainly reflects water exchange across intact, partially permeable cell membranes, rather than tissue loss or inconsistent blotting. Negative water potential does not mean “negative water”.

Retry: if a treatment destroys membrane selectivity, explain why the same mass calculation can still be performed but its interpretation using an intact-cell osmosis model may fail. Continue to [plant responses](/alevel/a2-biology/plant-responses/) and [homeostasis](/alevel/a2-biology/homeostasis/).

</details>

## DNA, RNA and protein synthesis {#information}

DNA contains antiparallel nucleotide strands joined by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Each nucleotide contains a phosphate, a sugar and a base. Phosphodiester bonds join nucleotides within a strand; hydrogen bonds join complementary bases across strands. DNA replication separates strands and uses each as a template to make a complementary strand, giving semiconservative replication. Do not confuse copying DNA with transcribing RNA.

| Information step | Molecules and action | Location and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transcription | RNA polymerase uses one DNA template strand to assemble complementary RNA, using U rather than T | In a eukaryote's nucleus for nuclear genes; in the cytoplasm of a prokaryote |
| RNA processing | Introns are removed and exons joined in the pre-mRNA model used here | Eukaryotic nucleus; do not automatically add this step to a bacterial answer |
| Translation | A ribosome reads mRNA codons; tRNA anticodons pair with codons and bring specific amino acids; peptide bonds link the amino acids | Ribosomes in the cytoplasm or on rough ER in eukaryotes; cytoplasmic ribosomes in prokaryotes |
| Folding and use | A polypeptide folds and may associate with other subunits | The destination and further processing depend on the protein |

A DNA change may alter an mRNA codon, changing an amino acid and potentially protein structure and function. Some changes do not alter the amino acid, and not every amino acid replacement changes function. A change in gene expression changes how much product is made without necessarily changing the DNA sequence.

> A bacterium contains a gene for an enzyme. Describe the path from that gene to the enzyme, including the role of mRNA, tRNA and ribosomes. Explain why adding “mRNA leaves the nucleus” makes this answer incorrect.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: state the cellular setting before the sequence</summary>

The organism is a prokaryote. Distinguish the molecule carrying the code from the molecule carrying an amino acid.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Model English chain and retry</summary>

“RNA polymerase transcribes the gene into mRNA using complementary base pairing. Ribosomes translate the mRNA. tRNA molecules with complementary anticodons bring specific amino acids, which are joined by peptide bonds. The polypeptide folds to form the enzyme.” A bacterium has no membrane-bound nucleus, so nuclear exit is not part of this pathway.

Retry: explain how reducing the amount of this mRNA can lower enzyme concentration without changing the enzyme's amino acid sequence. Continue to [gene expression](/alevel/a2-biology/gene-expression/) and [gene technology](/alevel/a2-biology/gene-technology/).

</details>

## Cell division, alleles and biological levels {#division}

A gene is a DNA sequence associated with a functional product; an allele is a version of a gene. In a diploid nucleus, homologous chromosomes carry the same gene loci but may carry different alleles. A replicated chromosome contains sister chromatids. DNA replication doubles DNA content without immediately doubling the chromosome number counted by centromeres.

Mitosis separates sister chromatids and normally produces genetically identical nuclei, maintaining chromosome number. Meiosis includes two divisions after one DNA replication. Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I; sister chromatids separate in meiosis II. Crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes and independent segregation generate new combinations of alleles. Fertilisation restores the diploid chromosome number.

**Worked count.** In a defined sample of 200 visible root-tip cells, 32 are in mitosis:

$$\text{mitotic index}=\frac{32}{200}=0.16.$$

This is a proportion, equivalent to 16%. It estimates the fraction observed in mitosis, not the number of divisions completed per minute. The location sampled and ability to identify stages affect it. A greater index can reflect more entry into mitosis or a longer time spent there.

> A drug prevents cells from completing metaphase. Predict how the mitotic index could change. Explain why that change would not necessarily show faster cell production.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: a snapshot counts cells occupying a stage</summary>

What happens when cells enter mitosis but remain there for longer?

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Feedback and retry</summary>

Cells may accumulate in metaphase, raising the observed mitotic index while fewer cells complete division. Stage occupancy and completed divisions per unit time are different measurements.

Retry: distinguish a change in an individual's enzyme activity from a change in an allele's frequency in a population. The latter concerns allele counts across individuals and generations, not a purposeful change within an individual. Continue to [inheritance](/alevel/a2-biology/inheritance/) and [evolution](/alevel/a2-biology/evolution/).

</details>

## Cell structure and microscopy {#microscopy}

The cell surface membrane controls exchange. The nucleus contains most eukaryotic DNA; ribosomes assemble polypeptides; rough ER and Golgi are involved in making, processing and routing many secreted proteins. Mitochondria carry out aerobic stages of respiration; chloroplasts carry out photosynthesis in photosynthetic eukaryotic cells. Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and these membrane-bound organelles, but have a membrane, DNA and ribosomes. The named process must match its compartment.

Magnification is image size divided by actual size, using the same units. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two close points; enlarging an image does not recover unresolved detail.

**Worked scale-bar ratio.** A structure spans 180 image pixels and a scale bar labelled 20 µm spans 60 pixels:

$$\text{actual length}=\frac{180}{60}\times20=60\;\mu\mathrm m.$$

If the whole image is resized uniformly, both pixel lengths change by the same factor, so the answer is unchanged. Measuring displayed millimetres against a printed magnification value is unreliable when a webpage has resized the image. Use its integral scale bar or a verified print size.

> A cell spans 150 pixels and an integral scale bar labelled 10 µm spans 50 pixels. Find the actual cell length. The image is then enlarged to twice its width and height. State the new cell length in pixels and its actual length.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: track image length and biological length separately</summary>

The scale bar is enlarged with the cell. The organism itself has not grown.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and next step</summary>

Actual length is 30 µm. After enlargement the cell spans 300 pixels, but remains 30 µm long; the scale bar now spans 100 pixels. Multiplying the actual length by two confuses image enlargement with growth. Use [data skills](/alevel/a2-biology/data-skills/) for further scale and unit tasks.

</details>

## Heart, gas exchange and plant transport {#transport}

In mammals, the right side of the heart pumps to the lungs and the left side pumps to the body. Pressure differences drive blood flow; valves reduce backflow. Ventricular contraction raises pressure, closing atrioventricular valves and opening semilunar valves when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure. The sinoatrial node initiates excitation; the detailed control pathway is developed in [homeostasis](/alevel/a2-biology/homeostasis/).

At exchange surfaces, diffusion is faster with a larger area, a shorter diffusion distance and a steeper concentration gradient, with other conditions comparable. Ventilation and blood flow help maintain gas gradients in the lungs. Oxygen diffuses into blood and binds reversibly to haemoglobin; circulation carries it to tissues. Do not describe blood's bulk flow as diffusion through the whole body.

In plants, evaporation from mesophyll cell walls and diffusion of water vapour through stomata contribute to transpiration. Loss of water lowers leaf water potential and generates tension in xylem; cohesion helps maintain a continuous water column. Water is drawn from roots toward leaves. This transpiration stream does not require ATP-driven pumping of water along xylem. Root uptake of mineral ions can involve active transport, which is a different process.

Phloem transports organic solutes between sources and sinks. In the mass-flow model, loading sucrose at a source lowers water potential, water enters, and hydrostatic pressure rises. Unloading and water loss at a sink help produce a pressure difference driving bulk flow. Loading can require energy; movement through the sieve tube is driven by the pressure difference. A growing root can be a sink even though xylem water travels upward.

> A potometer shows a bubble moving faster when air movement around a leafy shoot increases. Explain the likely mechanism. State why water uptake is only an estimate of transpiration, and name one reason an air leak would invalidate the reading.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: link the leaf surface to the apparatus</summary>

Consider the humid air layer, the water-vapour gradient and the continuity of the water column.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Model answer and retry</summary>

Air movement can remove humid air near the leaf, maintaining a steeper water-vapour gradient and increasing diffusion through open stomata. Increased water loss can increase uptake. Uptake need not equal transpiration exactly because some water is retained or used in the plant, and short-term tissue water stores can change. A leak permits movement unrelated to shoot uptake or breaks the continuous water pathway, so bubble displacement no longer measures only the intended process.

Retry: calculate uptake when a capillary of radius 0.50 mm shows a displacement of 12 mm in 4 min, assuming a uniform circular bore and no leak. Explain why the bore **radius**, not its diameter, belongs in the area calculation. See [experimental design](/alevel/a2-biology/experimental-design/) for RP6.

</details>

## Use the bridge in a new explanation {#transfer}

**Independent self-written task — keep both support panels closed initially.**

> A mutation reduces the activity of a membrane protein that transports mineral ions into root cells. Explain a possible chain of effects on mineral uptake, protein synthesis and plant growth. Distinguish the direct molecular effect from later whole-plant effects, and state why the mutation need not reduce growth in every environment.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint: make each link conditional and specific</summary>

Choose a mineral needed for amino acid production. Consider alternative transport routes and resource availability.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Feedback</summary>

A sequence change may alter the transporter's structure and lower ion transport. If nitrate uptake becomes limiting, less nitrogen may be available for amino acid synthesis and hence for making proteins such as enzymes and structural components. This can reduce biosynthesis and growth. The direct effect concerns one protein's activity; whole-plant growth also depends on other transporters, external mineral supply and other limiting factors. A DNA mutation does not automatically destroy all proteins or produce the same phenotype in every environment. Return to the relevant section if a link was missing, then try the explanation for a photosynthetic protein instead.

</details>

## Check the retries {#retry-feedback}

Write the retry before opening the relevant check below.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Retry checks: rates, membranes and information</summary>

- **Same endpoint, different initial rates:** the same initial amount of limiting substrate can yield the same final amount of product even when one reaction reaches completion sooner. Compare early gradients, not only endpoints.
- **Damaged membranes:** the arithmetic still describes mass change, but loss of selectivity allows additional solute exchange and may prevent maintenance of the original water-potential difference. The intact-cell assumptions no longer hold.
- **Less mRNA:** fewer available templates can reduce translation and enzyme production; unchanged coding sequence can still specify the same amino acid sequence in each polypeptide produced. Existing protein breakdown also affects the concentration over time.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Retry checks: division, transport and the new protein</summary>

- **Biological levels:** enzyme activity can change in one individual through inhibition or expression changes without altering DNA alleles. Allele frequency is a proportion of gene copies in a population; it can change across generations through processes such as selection or drift.
- **Potometer:** the swept volume is the circular area multiplied by displacement. Using the diameter as the radius would make the area, and calculated rate, four times too large. The value is 2.36 mm³ min⁻¹; retain more digits until rounding the final result.
- **Photosynthetic-protein transfer:** reduced activity of an electron-transfer protein could reduce the formation of ATP and reduced NADP, limiting carbon fixation and organic synthesis. State the protein's role and whether that step is limiting; alternative pathways and environmental conditions can modify the whole-plant effect.

</details>
