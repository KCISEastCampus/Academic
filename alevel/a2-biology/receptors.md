## What must a response explain? {#start}

A complete explanation connects **stimulus → receptor → coordination → effector → response**. By the end, distinguish reflexes, taxis and kinesis; explain a Pacinian generator potential; and separate light sensitivity, colour discrimination and visual acuity. Review membrane transport in [prerequisites](/alevel/a2-biology/prerequisites/).

Name the structure that detects a stimulus and the structure that produces a response. In a withdrawal reflex, is the contracting muscle the detector or the effector?
<!-- /task:BL04-R0 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>R0 · Check</summary>

A receptor detects the stimulus; an effector produces the response. The contracting muscle is an effector. Its motor neurone carries the instruction; it is not itself the muscle response.

</details>

## Three neurones, a fast protective response {#responses}

A reflex is a rapid, automatic response. In a three-neurone withdrawal reflex, a receptor in the skin stimulates a **sensory neurone**, which carries impulses into the spinal cord. It synapses with a **relay neurone**, which synapses with a **motor neurone**. Motor impulses reach a skeletal muscle and cause contraction. Information can also travel to the brain, but conscious decision is not needed to initiate withdrawal. Shortening the time of contact can reduce tissue damage; a reflex does not guarantee that no damage occurs.

A **taxis** is movement of a whole organism in a direction related to a stimulus: positive towards, negative away. A **kinesis** changes speed or turning rate with stimulus intensity, without a movement direction fixed relative to the stimulus. Either can increase time spent in favourable conditions. Neither requires an animal to understand which habitat is “best”. Tropisms in [plants](/alevel/a2-biology/plant-responses/) involve growth rather than locomotion.

## A Pacinian corpuscle converts deformation into voltage {#pacinian}

A receptor is a **transducer**: it converts energy in a stimulus into an electrical change. A Pacinian corpuscle contains a sensory nerve ending surrounded by concentric connective-tissue layers. It is especially responsive to changes in pressure and vibration.

Pressure deforms the capsule and the nerve-ending membrane. Mechanically gated (stretch-mediated) sodium channels open; Na⁺ moves into the ending down its electrochemical gradient. The membrane becomes less negative: a **generator potential** develops. This local potential is graded: a greater effective deformation can open more channels and give a larger depolarisation.

If the generator potential brings the nearby impulse-triggering region to threshold, action potentials are generated. Stronger stimulation is then represented by a higher frequency of impulses, not taller individual action potentials. Sustained pressure causes the layered capsule to redistribute the force and the response adapts; changing pressure is particularly effective. The capsule's mechanical properties help determine what the ending detects.

Start with greater membrane deformation and a larger generator potential, then explain more frequent threshold triggering. Do not say “the stronger stimulus makes a larger action potential”. ## Retina: detection, colour and resolution {#retina}

**Rods** contain a very light-sensitive pigment, rhodopsin, and support vision at low illumination. Many rods converge through bipolar cells onto shared ganglion-cell pathways. Combined signals can influence the output when one receptor's signal would be insufficient. However, a shared output gives less precise information about which receptor was stimulated.

**Cones** need stronger light. Humans have three main cone types with different, overlapping wavelength sensitivities. The nervous system compares their relative responses to distinguish colours; one cone is not a label that recognises only one colour. In the fovea, densely packed cones with relatively little convergence support high acuity. Rods are more abundant away from the fovea; the optic disc has no photoreceptors.

Photoreceptors change their membrane potential and transmitter release; they do **not** themselves send action potentials along the optic nerve. Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve. Detailed light-transduction chemistry is not required here, but it would be misleading to copy the Pacinian Na⁺-entry mechanism into a rod.

**Sensitivity** is the ability to respond to low light levels. **Visual acuity** is the ability to resolve nearby points as separate. Their trade-off depends on pigments and wiring, so compare one factor at a time.

## Independent explanations and investigation {#practice}

<!-- task:BL04-R3 -->
**R3 · Self-written / 自拟题**

Small animals are released at the centre of an arena. In dry regions they move faster and turn more often; their initial movement directions are random. Eventually more animals occupy damp regions. Decide whether these observations demonstrate taxis or kinesis, and explain how the distribution can arise. State one additional observation that would support a directional response.
<!-- /task:BL04-R3 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>R3 · Hint</summary>

Ending up in one region does not prove that every movement was directed towards it.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>R3 · Explanation</summary>

The described response is kinesis: movement rate and turning vary, while initial directions are random. Faster movement in dry conditions and slower movement after entering damp conditions can increase residence time in damp regions. The eventual distribution does not itself prove taxis. Consistent movement towards a moisture source, relative to its direction and beyond a random-direction expectation, would support taxis. Turning rate alone does not establish a preferred direction.

</details>

<!-- task:BL04-R4 -->
**R4 · Self-written / 自拟题**

Plan a comparison of two-point touch discrimination on the fingertip and forearm. Explain how you would reduce expectation bias and control stimulus application. State why a difference would not, by itself, measure only the number of Pacinian corpuscles.
<!-- /task:BL04-R4 -->

<details markdown="1">
<summary>R4 · Hint</summary>

Control pressure and timing; conceal the number and separation of contact points. Distinguish an anatomical count from a perceptual threshold.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>R4 · One defensible design</summary>

With informed consent and the option to stop, use blunt clean tips and gentle, standardised pressure. Keep contact duration and orientation consistent. Have the participant look away; randomise one-point and two-point trials and separations, and use a predetermined repeated-response criterion for reliable discrimination. Allow rests, record results for both sites within each participant and include several participants.

This measures a perceptual threshold involving receptor types, density, receptive-field overlap and central processing; it does not isolate a count of Pacinian corpuscles. Do not use sharp points or interpret discomfort as the desired measurement. A school practical requires its own risk controls and supervision.

</details>

## Diagnose, then change the model {#retry}

“Stronger impulse” confuses generator potential with action potential. “More animals in damp conditions proves taxis” confuses outcome with movement mechanism. “More sensitivity means better acuity” ignores information lost by convergence.
