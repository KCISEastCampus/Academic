## M1: Electron configurations, ligands, shapes and isomerism {#complexes}

A **transition metal** is an element that forms at least one ion with an incomplete d subshell. Position in the d block is not sufficient: Zn and its common Zn²⁺ ion have complete d subshells, so Zn does not meet this definition.

Remove 4s electrons first when forming cations. Ground-state Fe is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^64s^2$ and Fe²⁺ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^6$; Cu is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^{10}4s^1$ and Cu²⁺ is $[\mathrm{Ar}]3d^9$.

A **ligand** donates a lone pair to the central metal ion to form a coordinate bond. The **co-ordination number** is the number of donor atoms/coordinate bonds to the central metal, not simply the number of ligands.

| Ligand | Donors and binding |
| --- | --- |
| H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻ | Monodentate: one coordinate bond per ligand |
| $\ce{NH2CH2CH2NH2}$ (en) | Bidentate: each of the two N atoms donates a lone pair |
| $\ce{C2O4^{2-}}$ (ethanedioate) | Bidentate: two O atoms bind to the same metal |
| EDTA⁴⁻ | Multidentate: commonly six donor atoms surround the metal ion |

| Common coordination number | Shape | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Linear, 180° | $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral, approximately 109.5° | $\ce{[FeCl4]^-}$ |
| 4 | Square planar, 90° | $\ce{Pt(NH3)2Cl2}$ |
| 6 | Octahedral, 90°/180° | $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^{2+}}$ (may be distorted) |

**Geometric isomerism:** in square-planar $\ce{Pt(NH3)2Cl2}$, adjacent Cl ligands give cis and opposite Cl ligands give trans. Draw the metal and four positions first. Rotating the entire drawing does not create an isomer.

cis-$\ce{Pt(NH3)2Cl2}$ is **cisplatin**. Octahedral $\ce{[Co(NH3)4Cl2]+}$ also has cis/trans forms: the Cl ligands subtend 90° and 180° respectively. Establish the shape before comparing the relative positions of identical ligands.

**Optical isomerism:** some octahedral tris-bidentate complexes have non-superimposable mirror images. Use plain, solid-wedge and dashed-wedge bonds to show spatial directions, and give the overall charge outside square brackets. Not every octahedral complex has optical isomers.

### Example 1 (self-written): ligand count is not coordination number {#example-1}

**Question:** Co²⁺ forms a complex with three neutral en ligands. Find its charge, coordination number and shape, and describe a pair of optical-isomer drawings.

**Method and steps:** each en donates one lone pair from each of two N atoms, giving six donors in total. Neutral en does not alter the overall charge. **Answer:** $\ce{[Co(en)3]^{2+}}$ is six-coordinate, octahedral and has a pair of non-superimposable mirror images.

**Drawing model:** treat the six donor positions as the octahedral +x, −x, +y, −y, +z and −z directions. In the first drawing, the three en ligands join (+x,+y), (−x,+z) and (−y,−z). Reflection in the yz plane gives pairs (−x,+y), (+x,+z) and (−y,−z). Connect each pair with one complete N–CH₂–CH₂–N ligand; use solid and dashed wedges to show directions towards and away from the viewer.

**Independent check:** each drawing has three complete en ligands, six Co–N coordinate bonds and charge +2 outside brackets. Each bidentate ligand joins adjacent rather than opposite positions. The mirror images have opposite handedness; rotating the first drawing does not produce the second.

## M2: Colour, energy gaps and colorimetry {#colour}

Ligands split the d orbitals into groups of different energy. Photons at certain visible wavelengths are absorbed, with energy $\Delta E=h\nu=hc/\lambda$, exciting electrons to higher-energy d orbitals. The observed colour comes from **unabsorbed transmitted/reflected light**, not from electrons “emitting that colour” in this question.

$$\Delta E=\frac{hc}{\lambda}.$$

Use wavelength in m; multiply by $10^9$ at the end to express it in nm. J per photon is not kJ per mol: do not multiply by the Avogadro constant unless molar energy is requested. Changing the metal, oxidation state, ligand or coordination environment can change the energy gap and hence colour.

### Example 2 (self-written): explain colour and check units {#example-2}

**Question:** a complex absorbs a visible photon with energy $4.00\times10^{-19}$ J. Use $h=6.63\times10^{-34}$ J s and $c=3.00\times10^8$ m s⁻¹ to find wavelength, and explain why its observed colour is not simply this wavelength.

**Method and steps:** use the energy gap to calculate absorbed wavelength, first in m and then in nm.

$$\lambda=\frac{hc}{\Delta E}=\frac{6.63\times10^{-34}\times3.00\times10^8}{4.00\times10^{-19}}=4.97\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}=497\,\mathrm{nm}.$$

**Answer:** absorption excites d electrons to higher levels; the remaining unabsorbed transmitted/reflected light gives the observed colour. The explanation is not “emission of 497 nm light”. **Independent check:** J s multiplied by m s⁻¹ and divided by J gives m; 497 nm is visible. Convert back to m and substitute into the energy equation to recover approximately the original photon energy.

## M3: Ligand substitution, chelation and biological contexts {#ligand-substitution}

**Ligand substitution** replaces ligands, usually without changing the metal oxidation state. For example:

$$\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^{2+} + 4Cl^- <=> [CuCl4]^{2-} + 6H2O}.$$

The aqua complex is predominantly six-coordinate octahedral; the chloride complex is four-coordinate tetrahedral, with a different colour. Adding much Cl⁻ favours substitution; dilution lowers Cl⁻ concentration and favours reversal. A colour change alone does not establish redox.

**Chelate effect:** when one multidentate ligand replaces several monodentate ligands, released particles often increase entropy and favour a more stable complex. EDTA⁴⁻ replacing six water ligands changes the particle count from 2 to 7. Relate the argument to the actual equation and $\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$; do not assert that $\Delta H$ is zero for every substitution.

### Example 3 (self-written): explain chelation using particle count {#example-3}

**Question:** three en ligands replace six water ligands. Explain greater stability when the enthalpy change from exchanging bonds is small. **Method:** write the substitution, then use entropy and Gibbs energy.

$$\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^{2+} + 3en <=> [Co(en)3]^{2+} + 6H2O}.$$

1. There are 4 particles on the left and 7 on the right, so $\Delta S>0$.
2. If the six broken and six formed metal–ligand bonds have similar energies, $\Delta H$ is small.
3. The $-T\Delta S$ term is negative, so $\Delta G$ can be negative, favouring the new complex.

**Answer and independent check:** the explanation uses the stated enthalpy approximation and increased entropy, not an unconditional claim that multidentate ligands form stronger bonds. Co and en counts match, six waters are released, total charge is +2 on both sides and coordination number remains six.

Other substitutions include $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^{2+} + 4Cl^- <=> [CoCl4]^{2-} + 6H2O}$ (pink to blue) and $\ce{[Fe(H2O)6]^{3+} + 4Cl^- <=> [FeCl4]^- + 6H2O}$. Larger Cl⁻ ligands may change coordination number from 6 to 4. NH₃ and H₂O are similar in size, so substitution usually retains coordination number and may be partial: the tetraammine-diaqua copper complex remains six-coordinate.

## M4: Variable oxidation states and redox titration {#redox-titration}

Transition-metal 3d and 4s electrons have similar energies, allowing different oxidation states. Aqueous vanadium species have different colours: V(V), $\ce{VO2+}$, is yellow; V(IV), $\ce{VO^{2+}}$, is blue; V³⁺ is green; V²⁺ is violet. Use [potential comparisons](/alevel/a2-chemistry/electrode-potentials/#redox-direction) to decide how far a reducing agent can reduce the system.

Balance half-equations first and obtain the mole ratio from the overall equation. Distinguish **moles in the whole flask** from **moles in the aliquot**. Acidified KMnO₄ normally needs no separate indicator: the endpoint is the persistent pale pink of the first slight excess of MnO₄⁻.

### Example 4 (self-written): the Fe²⁺ titration mole chain {#example-4}

**Question:** 20.0 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ MnO₄⁻ completely oxidises 25.0 cm³ of Fe²⁺ in sufficient acid. Find the Fe²⁺ concentration. **Method:** use the overall reaction ratio, not the ethanedioate ratio.

$$\ce{MnO4^- + 8H+ + 5Fe^{2+} -> Mn^{2+} + 4H2O + 5Fe^{3+}}.$$

1. MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5. The 2 : 5 ratio for ethanedioate belongs to a different reaction.
2. Convert both volumes from cm³ to dm³, calculate moles of MnO₄⁻ and multiply by 5.
3. Divide by the Fe²⁺ sample volume:

$$c(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}})=\frac{5(0.0200\times0.0200)}{0.0250}=0.0800\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}.$$

**Answer and independent check:** 0.0800 mol dm⁻³. Work backwards: the 25.0 cm³ sample contains 0.00200 mol Fe²⁺; dividing by 5 gives 0.000400 mol MnO₄⁻, equal to titrant concentration times volume. Total charge is +17 on each side and Mn, Fe, O and H are balanced.

Reduction potentials depend on pH and ligands. For example, changing acidity changes the reduction tendency of the MnO₄⁻ half-equation containing H⁺. **Tollens’ reagent** contains linear $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$; aldehydes reduce it to silver, giving a silver mirror, whereas ketones normally do not. An alkaline overall equation is $\ce{RCHO + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 3OH^- -> RCOO^- + 2Ag + 4NH3 + 2H2O}$.

## M5: Homogeneous, heterogeneous and autocatalysis {#catalysis}

A catalyst provides a lower-activation-energy route and is regenerated in the overall reaction. It speeds approach to equilibrium in both directions without changing equilibrium position. Transition metals can use different oxidation states in reaction cycles.

A **heterogeneous catalyst** is in a different phase from the reactants. Surface adsorption brings reactants together and facilitates reaction, then products desorb. Impurities may occupy active sites and poison the catalyst. A **homogeneous catalyst** is in the same phase as the reactants, typically cycling through intermediates/oxidation states.

The Haber process uses solid Fe to catalyse $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$. Dispersing catalyst on a support increases effective surface area and reduces use of expensive material. Removing impurities reduces poisoning and replacement costs.

<span id="fe-均相催化的两步"></span>

### Example 5 (self-written): two steps in Fe²⁺ homogeneous catalysis {#example-5}

**Question:** $\ce{S2O8^{2-}}$ and I⁻ are both negative ions, so they repel. Show how Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ provides an alternative route and check that the catalyst is regenerated. **Method:** add the two stages and cancel species present on both sides:

$$\ce{S2O8^{2-} + 2Fe^{2+} -> 2SO4^{2-} + 2Fe^{3+}}.$$

$$\ce{2Fe^{3+} + 2I^- -> 2Fe^{2+} + I2}.$$

**Steps and answer:** Fe²⁺ is oxidised in the first step; Fe³⁺ is reduced in the second, regenerating Fe²⁺. All species are aqueous, so catalysis is homogeneous. Add and cancel to obtain $\ce{S2O8^{2-} + 2I^- -> 2SO4^{2-} + I2}$. **Independent check:** both sides have S 2, O 8, I 2 and total charge −4; neither Fe²⁺ nor Fe³⁺ remains in the net equation. Unlike a reactant, the catalyst is recovered.
