OxfordAQA 9665 · A2 Further Pure Mathematics · FP2 / FM03. Start with roots and polynomials, then follow the route below. If returning for revision, go directly to the [mixed tasks](#mixed-review).

## Start Here

1. **[Roots and Polynomials](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/roots-and-polynomials/)** — Use roots and coefficients, find complex roots and decide how many roots are real.
2. **[Proof by Induction](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/proof-by-induction/)** — Prove statements about series, sequences, divisibility, powers and derivatives.
3. **[Finite Series](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/finite-series/)** — Use partial fractions and differencing to find exact sums.
4. **[Series and Limits](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/series-and-limits/)** — Expand functions, find limits and evaluate improper integrals.
5. **[De Moivre's Theorem](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/de-moivres-theorem/)** — Find complex powers and roots, derive trigonometric identities and use them in integration.
6. **[Polar Coordinates](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/polar-coordinates/)** — Convert coordinates, sketch curves and calculate areas with the correct angular limits.
7. **[Calculus of Inverse Trigonometric Functions](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/inverse-trigonometric-functions/)** — Choose principal values, differentiate inverse functions and find exact integrals.
8. **[Arc Length and Surface Area](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/arc-length-and-surface-area/)** — Find curve lengths and curved surface areas from Cartesian or parametric equations.
9. **[Hyperbolic Functions](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/hyperbolic-functions/)** — Use exponential definitions, prove identities and apply inverse functions in calculus.
10. **[First-order Differential Equations](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/first-order-differential-equations/)** — Use an integrating factor or a complementary function and particular integral, then apply the given condition.
11. **[Second-order Differential Equations](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/second-order-differential-equations/)** — Use the auxiliary equation and a particular integral, then apply initial or boundary conditions.
12. **[Vector Products and Coplanarity](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/vector-products-and-coplanarity/)** — Connect perpendicular directions, areas, volumes and coplanarity.
13. **[Lines and Planes](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/lines-and-planes/)** — Change representations, find intersections and justify angles, distances and reflections.
14. **[Matrix Algebra](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/matrix-algebra/)** — Use three-dimensional transformations, determinants, inverses and invariant objects.
15. **[Linear Equations and Three Planes](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/linear-equations/)** — Classify all solutions, keep exceptional parameters and explain the common geometry.
16. **[Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors/)** — Derive characteristic equations, handle repeated real eigenvalues and verify nonzero eigenvectors.

Follow the lessons in order, or choose the topic you need. Together the lessons cover all fourteen syllabus topics FP2.1–FP2.14; some topics need more than one lesson.

## Course Outline

The lessons follow the A2 Pure part of *International A Level Further Mathematics*. Chapter 18 and Chapter 25 each cover two syllabus topics.

- **Algebra, proof and series:** roots and polynomials; proof by induction; finite series; series and limits.
- **Complex numbers and polar coordinates:** De Moivre's theorem; polar coordinates.
- **Calculus:** inverse trigonometric functions; arc length and surface area; hyperbolic functions; first-order and second-order differential equations.
- **Vectors and matrices:** three-dimensional geometry; solutions of linear equations; matrix algebra.

### Task A {#mixed-a}

> Find all complex roots of $z^3=8$, plot them on an Argand diagram, and explain why their sum and the sum of their squares are both zero. Give a second check using coefficients of a polynomial.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write the modulus and all distinct arguments first. For the coefficient check, use both the sum and pairwise products of the roots.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

The roots are

$$
z_k=2\left(\cos\frac{2k\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{2k\pi}{3}\right),\quad k=0,1,2,
$$

so plot $(2,0)$, $(-1,\sqrt3)$ and $(-1,-\sqrt3)$, an equilateral triangle centred at the origin. The real and imaginary components sum to zero. Squaring doubles each argument and multiplies each modulus by itself: the three squared roots again have arguments $0,2\pi/3,4\pi/3$, so their sum is zero.

For the separate coefficient check, let the roots be $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. From $z^3+0z^2+0z-8$,

$$
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=0,\quad
\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=0,
$$

$$
\alpha^2+\beta^2+\gamma^2
=(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)^2-2(\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha)=0.
$$

If a root is missing or duplicated, revisit [complex roots](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/de-moivres-theorem/#roots); if the coefficient reasoning fails, revisit [roots and coefficients](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/roots-and-polynomials/).

</details>

</section>

### Task B {#mixed-b}

> A function satisfies $y'+2y=\sinh x$ and $y(0)=0$. Find $y$ explicitly and $y(\ln2)$ exactly. Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}y/x^2$ without using a decimal approximation.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Express the forcing function using exponentials. After solving, retain terms through the quadratic term when examining the limit.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

The equation is linear and already normalised. Using $\sinh x=(e^x-e^{-x})/2$, the integrating factor is $e^{\int2\,dx}=e^{2x}$, giving

$$
(e^{2x}y)'=\frac12(e^{3x}-e^x).
$$

Integrate both sides, including the coefficient from each exponential derivative:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{2x}y&=\frac12\int e^{3x}\,dx-\frac12\int e^x\,dx\\
&=\frac12\left(\frac{e^{3x}}3\right)-\frac12e^x+C,\\
y&=\frac16e^x-\frac12e^{-x}+Ce^{-2x}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At $x=0$, $0=1/6-1/2+C$, so $C=1/3$. Hence

$$
y(\ln2)=\frac{2}{6}-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{12}=\frac16.
$$

Using the exponential expansions as $x\to0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
y&=\frac16\left(1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}+O(x^3)\right)
-\frac12\left(1-x+\frac{x^2}{2}+O(x^3)\right)\\
&\quad+\frac13\left(1-2x+2x^2+O(x^3)\right)\\
&=\frac12x^2+O(x^3),\qquad
\lim_{x\to0}\frac{y}{x^2}=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

Check by differentiation in the original equation and by $y(0)=0$. A sign error in the exponential definition sends you back to [hyperbolic definitions](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/hyperbolic-functions/); an integration error to [a complete integrating-factor example](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/first-order-differential-equations/#example-1-normalise-and-solve); an uncancelled constant or linear term to [series and limits](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/series-and-limits/).

</details>

</section>

### Task C {#mixed-c}

> Let the matrix be

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}.$$

> Solve $A\mathbf{x}=(3,2,3)^{\mathsf T}$. Find every real eigenvalue and its full eigenspace. Decide whether $(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ is an eigenvector and justify your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The triangular structure helps with both calculations. A repeated root of the characteristic equation does not prescribe the number of independent eigenvectors.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

From the last two rows, $z=1$ and $y=1$; the first then gives $2x+1=3$, so $\mathbf{x}=(1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}$. The nonzero determinant $12$ also confirms uniqueness.

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2(3-\lambda)=0.
$$

For $\lambda=2$, the equations reduce to $y=0,z=0$; the eigenspace is $\{t(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}:t\in\mathbb R\}$. For $\lambda=3$, they reduce to $y=x,y=0$; its eigenspace is $\{t(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}:t\in\mathbb R\}$. In either space only nonzero vectors are eigenvectors.

$$
A(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}=(3,2,0)^{\mathsf T}\ne\lambda(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}
\quad\text{for every scalar }\lambda.
$$

The first coordinate would require $\lambda=3$, the second $\lambda=2$, a contradiction. Recover [matrix-vector multiplication](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/matrix-algebra/), [solving systems](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/linear-equations/), or [repeated eigenvalues](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors/) according to the failed step.

</details>

</section>

### Review A {#later-a}

> Sketch the complete curve $r=2\cos\theta$, identify its Cartesian form, and find its enclosed area exactly. Explain why your angular interval traces the curve once.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Multiply by the radius to identify the Cartesian equation. An interval with nonnegative radius can cover the complete curve.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

Since $r^2=2r\cos\theta$, the Cartesian form is $x^2+y^2=2x$, or $(x-1)^2+y^2=1$. Sketch a circle centred at $(1,0)$ with radius $1$, passing through the origin and $(2,0)$, tangent to the y-axis at the origin. The interval $-\pi/2\le\theta\le\pi/2$ has nonnegative radius: the lower and upper semicircles join at $(2,0)$, with only the pole repeated at the endpoints.

Using $2\cos^2\theta=1+\cos2\theta$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A&=\frac12\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(2\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta
=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}(1+\cos2\theta)\,d\theta\\
&=\left[\theta+\frac12\sin2\theta\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\\
&=\left(\frac\pi2+\frac12\sin\pi\right)
-\left(-\frac\pi2+\frac12\sin(-\pi)\right)=\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

This agrees with the Cartesian circle area. Recover [tracing and angular bounds](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/polar-coordinates/) or [multiple-angle identities](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/de-moivres-theorem/) if those steps failed.

</details>

</section>

### Review B {#later-b}

> A curve is given by $x=t$, $y=t^2/2$, $0\le t\le1$. Find its length exactly, explaining any substitution and its limits.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write the parametric length integral first. A hyperbolic substitution can turn the square root into a positive hyperbolic function.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

Here $dx/dt=1$, $dy/dt=t$, so $L=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt$. Set $t=\sinh u$, $dt=\cosh u\,du$; since $\cosh u>0$, $\sqrt{1+\sinh^2u}=\cosh u$. The endpoints become $0$ and $a=\operatorname{arsinh}1=\ln(1+\sqrt2)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
L&=\int_0^a\cosh^2u\,du
=\frac12\int_0^a(1+\cosh2u)\,du\\
&=\left[\frac u2+\frac14\sinh2u\right]_0^a\\
&=\frac a2+\frac14(2\sinh a\cosh a)-0
=\frac{\ln(1+\sqrt2)+\sqrt2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We used $\sinh a=1$ and $\cosh a=\sqrt2$. Since $1\le\sqrt{1+t^2}\le\sqrt2$ on the interval, the length lies between $1$ and $\sqrt2$, consistent with the result. Recover the [parametric length model](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/arc-length-and-surface-area/) or [hyperbolic substitution](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/hyperbolic-functions/) according to the error.

</details>

</section>

### Review C {#later-c}

> The transformation $T$ has matrix

$$M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

> Find its fixed points and its real eigenspaces. Describe the transformation geometrically and justify your description using a normal vector.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare a general point with its image, then test a vector perpendicular to the fixed set.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and recovery</summary>

Since $M(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}=(y,x,z)^{\mathsf T}$, the fixed points form the plane $x=y$. Its normal is $\mathbf n=(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, and $M\mathbf n=-\mathbf n$.

$$
\det(M-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)(\lambda^2-1).
$$

The eigenspace for $1$ is $\{(s,s,t)^{\mathsf T}:s,t\in\mathbb R\}$; the eigenspace for $-1$ is $\{s(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}:s\in\mathbb R\}$. Every vector splits into a part in the fixed plane and a part parallel to its normal:

$$
(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}
=\left(\frac{x+y}2,\frac{x+y}2,z\right)^{\mathsf T}
+\frac{x-y}2(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The first part stays fixed and the second reverses, so this is reflection in the plane $x=y$. Recover [plane normals](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/lines-and-planes/) or [invariant objects and transformations](/alevel/a2-further-mathematics/matrix-algebra/) if the geometric justification is missing.

</details>

</section>
