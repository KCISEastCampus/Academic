<h2 id="start-here">To control a product, control reaction and separation</h2>

Oxidation of a primary alcohol can give an aldehyde or a carboxylic acid. Why does changing the apparatus change what you collect? And how can acid remove water from an alcohol without being used up? This AS §3.3.5.1–2 lesson includes RP4 preparation and interpretation. You need [functional groups and electron-pair arrows](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-foundations/) and the idea that heating a mixture does not necessarily break every covalent bond. All tasks, examples and datasets are self-written/constructed, with no official marks. Paper preparation and constructed results do not count as carrying out a supervised practical.

<h2 id="classification">Count carbon neighbours of the OH-bearing carbon</h2>

An alcohol contains OH bonded to a saturated carbon. Find that carbon before classifying: **primary (1°)** has one carbon neighbour, **secondary (2°)** two and **tertiary (3°)** three. CH3CH2CH2OH is primary, CH3CH(OH)CH3 secondary and (CH3)3COH tertiary. The number of OH groups is a different question. Methanol has no carbon neighbour but shows primary-type oxidation chemistry; do not force its zero neighbours into the one-neighbour definition.

For oxidation, expand the bonds at the OH-bearing carbon. A primary alcohol has two C-bound H there, a secondary one H and a tertiary none. The O–H hydrogen is present in all three and is not what distinguishes their oxidation behaviour.

<h2 id="oxidation">The carbon-bound hydrogen makes the difference</h2>

Warm with **acidified potassium dichromate(VI), K2Cr2O7/H2SO4**. Primary and secondary alcohols reduce orange Cr(VI) to green Cr(III) while the organic molecule is oxidised. For this reaction, increasing C–O bonding while removing H gives the carbonyl C=O. A tertiary alcohol cannot make that carbonyl while retaining all three C–C bonds, so it is not readily oxidised under these conditions. This does not mean a tertiary alcohol can never react or burn.

[O] is a stoichiometric shorthand for oxidising capacity, not a free oxygen atom or a chromium mechanism. No complete dichromate oxidation mechanism is required here.

$$\ce{RCH2OH + [O] -> RCHO + H2O}$$

$$\ce{RCHO + [O] -> RCOOH}$$

$$\ce{R2CHOH + [O] -> R2CO + H2O}$$

A primary alcohol first gives an **aldehyde** (terminal –CHO), then a **carboxylic acid** (–COOH). A secondary alcohol gives a **ketone** (C=O between carbon groups). Count O and H in each step; adding the first two gives RCH2OH + 2[O] → RCOOH + H2O.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

Butan-2-ol has one H on C2. Removing that H and the O–H H gives CH3COCH2CH3, butan-2-one, plus water using one [O]. Butan-1-ol gives CH3CH2CH2CHO first, with the same four-carbon skeleton; a second [O] gives CH3CH2CH2COOH. An extra carbon in your product signals a naming/counting error, not oxidation.

</div>

<h2 id="control-product">Distil an aldehyde; reflux to continue oxidation</h2>

For a primary alcohol → aldehyde, limit oxidant and **distil the aldehyde as it forms** so it leaves the oxidising mixture before substantial further oxidation. For → acid, use excess oxidant and **reflux**: vapour condenses and returns, maintaining contact with oxidant while heating. Reflux is not a reaction type or a guarantee of a pure product. The cold receiver used for ethanal is important because ethanal boils at about 21 °C; a warm open receiver can lose it. Cooling reduces loss but the apparatus must remain vented, never sealed.

<h2 id="rp4">RP4 — collect a volatile product from a reaction</h2>

The official handbook illustrates ethanol oxidation and collection of ethanal. We consistently choose the specification’s acidified **potassium** dichromate(VI); the handbook alternates potassium/sodium wording, which is not a reason to splice recipes. This is preparation for a teacher-approved practical, not a home protocol or proof of hands-on completion.

![Distillation schematic with reaction flask in water bath, anti-bumping granules, thermometer bulb at vapour take-off, sloping condenser lower water inlet and upper outlet, cold vented receiver.](/assets/img/chemistry-as-alcohols-distillation.svg){: width="680" height="480" style="min-width:680px" }

</div>

Put anti-bumping granules in before heating: their surfaces help bubbles form steadily, reducing bumping. The thermometer bulb belongs in the vapour path at the side-arm entrance, not submerged in the reaction. Cold water enters the condenser jacket at its lower end and leaves the upper end, filling the jacket. Use a cold, vented receiver to reduce volatile ethanal loss. An open path prevents pressure accumulation. A vertical reflux condenser would return product instead of collecting it.

Volatile organics are flammable: use controlled water-bath/electric heating, no flame. Acid and Cr(VI) are hazardous; teacher-approved small scale, eye protection, ventilation, controlled addition and segregated hazardous waste address splashes, exposure and the exothermic reaction. Do not interpret old handbook disposal wording as permission to pour chromium residues away. Never distil a flask dry.

<h2 id="tests">Separate the observation from the functional-group inference</h2>

To distinguish a simple aldehyde from a simple ketone, warm fresh separate aliquots with **Tollens’ reagent** (ammoniacal Ag(I)): an aldehyde reduces it to silver, often a silver mirror or grey silver deposit; a ketone gives no such change under these conditions. With freshly mixed **Fehling’s A and B**, warm in a water bath: an aliphatic aldehyde gives a brick-red Cu2O precipitate from the blue mixture; a simple ketone is negative. The aldehyde is oxidised during these tests.

Fresh Tollens’ must be prepared and disposed of promptly under the centre’s procedure, never stored or allowed to dry because hazardous residues may form. Use small-scale supervised tests, eye protection and no flames. “No change” requires working reagent, adequate time/temperature and controls; it is not an unconditional proof of a ketone.

<h2 id="dehydration">Acid turns a poor leaving group into water</h2>

Heat an alcohol with a suitable acid catalyst such as concentrated H2SO4 (or H3PO4) to eliminate water and form an alkene. This is **dehydration**, a type of elimination; the original alcohol OH does not simply leave as OH− in acid. For a scientifically consistent secondary-alcohol model, use propan-2-ol. In water-containing acid write the catalyst as H3O+ so its regeneration can be checked explicitly.

1. Alcohol O lone pair → a H of H3O+; that hydronium O–H bond → its O. This gives (CH3)2CH–OH2+ and H2O: the alcohol oxygen has three bonds and + charge.
2. The C–O bond pair → O. H2O leaves and (CH3)2CH+ remains. The carbon is secondary and has three bonds.
3. A water O lone pair → a neighbouring carbon’s H; that C–H bond pair → the C–C bond, making C=C. Propene and H3O+ form. The positive catalyst is regenerated; no electron pair vanishes.

Net: CH3CH(OH)CH3 → CH3CH=CH2 + H2O. Charges +1 persist through each stage including catalyst. Other nonreacting lone pairs are omitted in the drawing. Primary ethanol does not need a stable free primary carbocation: its loss of water and β-H can occur together after protonation. Do not copy the secondary-cation model indiscriminately onto every alcohol.

<h2 id="carbon-source">An alcohol can connect renewable carbon to a polymer</h2>

Fermentation of plant-derived sugars supplies ethanol without using petroleum as the carbon feedstock: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2. Acid-catalysed dehydration gives ethene, C2H5OH → C2H4 + H2O; ethene can form poly(ethene). This connects alcohol chemistry with polymer monomers. The same ethene molecule has the same chemistry whatever its source. Renewable feedstock does not guarantee carbon neutrality: cultivation, land use, fertiliser, fermentation yield, distillation/dehydration energy, transport and disposal all affect lifecycle emissions and cost.
