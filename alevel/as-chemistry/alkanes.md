<h2 id="start-here">Separate a mixture, then change selected molecules</h2>

Crude oil is useful because its molecules can be separated and transformed. Which stage only separates molecules, which breaks bonds, and which causes pollution? This AS §3.3.2.1–4 lesson connects all three to evidence and equations. You need [bond counting and radicals](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-foundations/); the boiling bridge below supplies the physical model. All tasks and data are self-written/constructed, with no official marks. Try unaided, then use the separately closed help.

<h3 id="starting-check">What does saturated mean?</h3>
<!-- task:starting-check -->
Compare CH3CH2CH3, CH2=CHCH3 and cyclohexane. Which are hydrocarbons, which are saturated, and which fit the acyclic alkane formula? Explain without counting only hydrogen atoms.
<!-- /task:starting-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Hydrocarbon refers to elements present; saturated refers to C–C bond types.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
All are hydrocarbons (only C and H). Propane and cyclohexane are saturated: all C–C bonds single. Only propane belongs to acyclic CnH2n+2 alkanes; the ring gives C6H12. Propene has C=C and is unsaturated. Do not label any low-H formula an alkene without inspecting its connections.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="fractionation">A boiling range is a property of a mixture</h2>

**Alkanes** are saturated hydrocarbons; for the acyclic series CnH2n+2, each added CH2 increases electron number and usually contact area. Stronger London dispersion between larger molecules requires more energy to separate them, so boiling points tend to rise. Branching can reduce contact and lower boiling point among isomers. Melting depends strongly on packing and is not a universal monotonic branching rule.

Crude oil is a mixture mainly of alkanes. Heat it to vaporise much of it and feed a **fractionating column**, hot at the bottom, cooler at the top. Vapours rise, repeatedly condense and evaporate; high-boiling material condenses lower, lower-boiling material reaches higher. A fraction is a mixture with a boiling range, not a pure substance with one formula. Boiling overcomes intermolecular attractions; it does not normally break the molecules’ C–C bonds.

<h2 id="cracking">Cracking changes the supply of useful molecules</h2>

**Cracking** breaks C–C bonds of larger hydrocarbons, making smaller molecules. An atom-balanced example is C10H22 → C8H18 + C2H4. It is one possible product set, not the sole industrial reaction. Heat supplies energy and a product mixture must be separated.

**Thermal cracking:** high temperature and high pressure, producing a high proportion of alkenes useful for chemical manufacture. **Catalytic cracking:** high temperature, slightly raised pressure and a zeolite catalyst, producing motor-fuel components and aromatic hydrocarbons. The catalyst supplies another reaction pathway; it is not a reactant consumed to supply carbon. No cracking mechanism or single memorised temperature/pressure is required here.

A refinery may have excess heavy fractions and demand for small fuels or polymer feedstocks. Converting lower-value surplus into higher-demand products can increase revenue, but energy, catalysts, separation, plant cost, emissions and changing market demand matter. “Smaller is always more profitable” is not a chemical law.

<h2 id="combustion">Oxygen supply changes combustion products</h2>

Combustion transfers chemical energy to the surroundings and makes alkanes useful fuels. With sufficient oxygen, carbon gives CO2 and hydrogen gives H2O. For propane, balance C, then H, then O: C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O. Water may be vapour in hot exhaust and liquid after cooling; state the context. Limited oxygen can give toxic CO and solid carbon (soot), often together with CO2 and unburnt fuel. A chosen balanced equation models one product set, not every engine exhaust.

<h2 id="pollution">Match each pollutant to its source and treatment</h2>

High engine temperatures allow atmospheric N2 and O2 to react: N2 + O2 → 2NO; further oxidation forms NO2. NOx contributes to respiratory harm and photochemical pollution. CO impairs oxygen transport in blood; soot harms lungs; unburnt hydrocarbons contribute to photochemical smog. Sulfur impurities burn to SO2, an acidic gas linked to acid deposition. CO2 contributes to enhanced greenhouse warming even when combustion is complete.

A hot **catalytic converter** uses catalysts such as Pt/Rh to remove several gaseous pollutants: 2CO + 2NO → 2CO2 + N2; CO and unburnt hydrocarbons can also be oxidised. It does not remove all particulate matter, or eliminate CO2 emissions, and needs a suitable operating temperature.

In flue-gas desulfurisation, basic CaO or CaCO3 reacts with acidic SO2: SO2 + CaO → CaSO3; SO2 + CaCO3 → CaSO3 + CO2. Sulfite may then oxidise: 2CaSO3 + O2 → 2CaSO4. Capturing sulfur reduces acidic emissions but creates solid waste/usable by-products and has energy/material costs.

<h2 id="radical-chain">A chain continues because a radical is regenerated</h2>

Methane reacts with chlorine under UV light by **free-radical substitution**. UV causes Cl–Cl homolysis in initiation, not ion formation. Propagation consumes one radical and makes another, so many molecules react after one initiating event. Termination joins two radicals with no radical product. In bright light this mixture is hazardous; the equations explain chemistry, not a classroom mixing instruction.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

**Initiation:** Cl2 → 2Cl· (UV). **Propagation:** Cl· + CH4 → HCl + CH3·; CH3· + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl·. Add the two propagation equations and cancel the radicals: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl. **Termination possibilities:** Cl· + Cl· → Cl2; CH3· + Cl· → CH3Cl; CH3· + CH3· → C2H6.

Termination can make the same CH3Cl as propagation, but it consumes two radicals instead of regenerating one. Further H substitutions produce CH2Cl2, CHCl3 and CCl4, so the method gives a mixture; it is not a guarantee of pure chloromethane.

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="chain-check">Write the chain for a changed alkane</h3>
<!-- task:chain-check -->
Construct a UV chlorination mechanism for ethane giving chloroethane: initiation, both propagation steps and all three radical-pair termination types. State which radical is regenerated, and explain why producing C4H10 is evidence of a termination route. Curly arrows are not required.
<!-- /task:chain-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Use C2H5· after removal of one H from ethane; conserve every atom in each step.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Cl2 → 2Cl·; Cl· + C2H6 → HCl + C2H5·; C2H5· + Cl2 → C2H5Cl + Cl·. Terminations: 2Cl· → Cl2; C2H5· + Cl· → C2H5Cl; 2C2H5· → C4H10. Cl· is regenerated in propagation. Two ethyl radicals join to butane with no radical left. Missing dots hide the mechanism even if the net formula balances.
</details>
</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="retry-chain">Diagnose a false propagation step</h3>
<!-- task:retry-chain -->
A proposed step is CH3· + Cl· → CH3Cl, labelled propagation. Correct the label and write a genuine methane propagation step producing CH3Cl. Explain why replacing all radical dots with minus signs fails charge and electron accounting.
<!-- /task:retry-chain -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Look for a radical on the product side.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
It is termination. Propagation: CH3· + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl·. Dots mean neutral species with unpaired electrons; minus signs mean an extra electron and negative charge. CH3− + Cl− → CH3Cl would lose charge −2 without any charged product.
</details>
</div>
