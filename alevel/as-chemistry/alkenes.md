<h2 id="start-here">Why does the double bond attract an electrophile?</h2>

An alkene is an unsaturated hydrocarbon containing C=C. This lesson covers AS §3.3.4.1–3: electrophilic addition, competing product routes and addition polymers. §3.3.4.4 is taught in [epoxyethane](/alevel/as-chemistry/epoxyethane/). Before starting, use [organic foundations](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-foundations/) to check carbon valence, E/Z and curly-arrow language. All tasks, examples and datasets are self-written/constructed, with no official marks. Record whether you used the closed hints.

<h2 id="double-bond">An electron-rich region can donate a pair</h2>

C=C contains two shared pairs. A useful teaching model is one σ bond along the internuclear line and one π bond with electron density above and below the plane. The π pair is accessible to an **electrophile**, a species that accepts a pair to form a covalent bond. The electrophile need not be an isolated positive ion: the δ+ H in HBr is an example. Addition uses the π pair while retaining the C–C σ bond; two new bonds form across the former double bond. The carbon skeleton is not split as in cracking.

<h2 id="electrophilic-addition">HBr — two steps and a reason for the major route</h2>

For propene, first draw the C=C pair → H of H–Br and H–Br bond → Br. If H attaches to the terminal carbon, the middle carbon becomes CH3–C+(H)–CH3, a secondary carbocation with three bonds and an empty orbital. Then a Br− lone pair → C+ gives 2-bromopropane. Total charge at the ion stage is +1 plus −1, still zero.

The other orientation puts the positive charge on the terminal carbon, CH3CH2CH2+, a primary carbocation; Br− capture gives 1-bromopropane. Alkyl groups donate electron density and stabilise a nearby positive carbon: tertiary > secondary > primary. Formation of the more stable intermediate generally favours the 2-bromo route under these ordinary ionic-addition conditions. This predicts major/minor products, not an exact ratio or absolute exclusivity. We do not introduce radical/peroxide HBr addition here.

<h2 id="three-reagents">The attacking pair and medium determine the second stage</h2>

<h3 id="sulfuric-acid">Cold concentrated H2SO4 adds across C=C</h3>

Treat H2SO4 as H–O–SO3H for the changing bonds. With ethene under cool, concentrated-acid addition conditions, C=C pair → H and O–H bond → O form CH3CH2+ and HSO4−. A lone pair on the negatively charged O of HSO4− attacks C+, making CH3CH2–O–SO3H, ethyl hydrogensulfate. The new bond is C–O, not C–S. With unsymmetrical alkenes compare the possible carbocations as above. On subsequent dilution/heating, hydrolysis can give the alcohol and regenerate sulfuric acid; that is a later operation, not the product to insert into the cold-acid addition mechanism.

<h3 id="bromine">Br2 — distinguish a reaction model from the water test</h3>

An alkene’s electron density induces a dipole in Br2: the nearer Br is δ+. In a dry, inert nonaqueous medium, addition gives a vicinal dibromo compound (one Br on each former double-bond carbon). A scientifically fuller pair-arrow model uses a **bromonium ion**: C=C pair → near Br, Br–Br bond → far Br, and a near-Br lone pair → the other alkene C close a three-member bridge with positive charge on Br. Then Br− donates to a carbon while its C–bridging-Br bond pair returns to Br, opening the bridge. No stable five-bond carbon or free open carbocation is required. The bridge is a model that preserves correct electron accounting, not an extra stereochemistry syllabus; you are not asked for advanced anti-addition analysis.

For the **bromine-water test**, shake a small separate sample with bromine water, at room temperature without UV: orange/brown colour disappears rapidly with an alkene under the test conditions. Record the colour change before inferring unsaturation. Water can compete as a nucleophile and form bromohydrin products, so this qualitative water test is not a preparation guaranteeing a pure dibromo product. Some other substances also decolourise bromine; a positive result is strongest in a defined candidate set, not proof of a unique unknown in all chemistry. Halogen reagents require supervised small-scale use and suitable ventilation.

<h2 id="polymers">Repeat units preserve every side group</h2>

In **addition polymerisation**, many alkene molecules join without loss of a small molecule. Replace C=C by C–C and extend one bond from each former double-bond C to the next unit. Put brackets across these two backbone bonds and n outside; do not put brackets around a side group alone. The general representation n CH2=CHR → [–CH2–CH(R)–]n conserves all atoms. Poly(propene), poly(chloroethene) (PVC) and poly(tetrafluoroethene) are named from the monomer. Their repeat units are –CH2–CH(CH3)–, –CH2–CHCl– and –CF2–CF2– respectively.

<h2 id="materials">Unreactive chains can still have different useful properties</h2>

Polyalkenes have strong, largely nonpolar saturated C–C/C–H backbones and no remaining monomer C=C for ordinary addition. This helps explain chemical unreactivity under many everyday conditions; it does not mean they cannot burn or degrade. Dispersion forces act between chains, with many contact points giving a substantial combined attraction. In PVC the C–Cl groups also introduce permanent dipoles; chains are not normally held together by newly invented covalent crosslinks.

Rigid PVC suits pipes/window frames. A **plasticiser** fits between chains, changes their packing and reduces effective attractions so chains slide more easily: flexible PVC suits cable insulation. It does not soften PVC by cutting every C–C backbone. Evidence linking formulation, flexibility and durability allowed materials science to develop better products; conclusions require controlled measurements, including ageing and additive loss. Waste persistence, recycling difficulty and possible plasticiser migration must be weighed against long service life and avoided replacement, not settled by the word “plastic”.
