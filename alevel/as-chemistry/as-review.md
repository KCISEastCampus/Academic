<h2 id="start-here">Choose an explanation before choosing an equation</h2>

These **self-written mixed tasks** connect CH01 and CH02 learning. They are not an official paper, predicted paper or mark scheme. Complete the [course route](/alevel/as-chemistry/) first, then attempt with every hint/solution closed. State your chosen system, model and conditions before calculations. Work in English; a connected scientific explanation matters more than a keyword list. Use a data booklet where instructed.

Record first attempt, help used, error cause and a fresh retry. Return after another topic to the two new [later tasks](#later-check); an immediate corrected answer is not evidence of retention. No task here certifies apparatus skills.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="mixed-a">A sample and a gas</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-a -->
Element X is in Period 3 and has a large jump between its third and fourth ionisation energies. Identify its outer-shell configuration and a plausible element. A 0.540 g alloy contains 80.0% X by mass; the remainder is inert to the excess dilute acid used. X forms X3+(aq), and H+(aq) forms H2(g). Construct the balanced ionic equation and determine the hydrogen volume at 100kPa and 300 K, using Ar(X)=27.0 and R=8.31 J mol−1 K−1. State assumptions needed to compare this dry ideal-gas prediction with an actual wet collected volume. Explain in English why the solid X can conduct, without claiming its ions migrate through the solid.
<!-- /task:mixed-a -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Connect the jump to outer electrons, then determine how many electrons each metal atom supplies.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Three outer electrons in Period 3 give 3s² 3p¹, Al. 2Al+6H⁺→2Al³⁺+3H₂. Mass Al=.540×.800=.432 g; n(Al) = .0160 mol; n(H₂) = .0240 mol. V=nRT/p=.0240×8.31×300/100000=.00059832m³=**598cm³**(3sf). Assumptions: complete accessible reaction/no other H₂ source, correct gas temperature/pressure, dry gas or water-vapour correction, negligible leaks/dissolution and adequate ideality. In the metal, delocalised electrons carry current; ion cores do not migrate through a solid. If element inference fails, return to [ionisation evidence](/alevel/as-chemistry/atomic-structure/#ionisation-evidence); if volume fails, use [gas evidence](/alevel/as-chemistry/formulae-and-gases/). Retry with a new system in later task A.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="mixed-b">A laboratory report</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-b -->
An unknown aqueous sample may contain carbonate, sulfate and chloride, separately or together, above the validated detection limits. Design a sequence using separate fresh portions to investigate all three. Explain acid choices, reagent order, visible observations, a gas confirmation, controls and one relevant risk control. Write the relevant ionic equations. A classmate uses HCl before AgNO3, gets a white solid and reports chloride in the original bottle. Evaluate the conclusion and explain why repeating that same procedure does not repair the problem.
<!-- /task:mixed-b -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Decide which added reagent could itself supply the ion being investigated.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Carbonate: fresh portion+dilute acid→effervescence; pass evolved gas through limewater, milky CO₂ confirmation (avoid prolonged excess gas): CO₃²⁻+2H⁺→CO₂+H₂O; CO₂+Ca(OH)₂→CaCO₃+H₂O. Sulfate: another portion, dilute HCl first/remove carbonate, then BaCl₂→white BaSO₄; Ba²⁺+SO₄²⁻→BaSO₄. Chloride: fresh portion, dilute HNO₃ first, then AgNO₃→white AgCl, soluble in dilute NH₃; Ag⁺+Cl⁻→AgCl. Use reagent blanks/known positive references with identical conditions. HCl adds chloride, so that result cannot establish original chloride; repeats reproduce the contamination. Eye protection and small supervised additions address splashes; segregate Ba/Ag waste. If you reused a portion, recover [test decisions](/alevel/as-chemistry/qualitative-analysis/), then use later task B.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="mixed-c">Two energy reports</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-c -->
A rapid aqueous reaction in an open insulated cup has limiting reaction amount 0.0200 mol, total solution mass 75.0 g and corrected temperature rise 4.0 K. Use specific heat capacity 4.18 J g−1 K−1; neglect cup absorption and dilution heat. Determine the enthalpy per mole of the reaction as written and justify its sign and constant-pressure interpretation. Explain the likely direction of error if heat loss from the warm liquid were uncorrected. Separately, given C(graphite)+O2(g)→CO2(g), ΔH=−394 kJ mol−1, and CO(g)+1/2O2(g)→CO2(g), ΔH=−283 kJ mol−1 at the same conditions, construct the equation and enthalpy for forming one mole CO(g). Why cannot these thermochemical numbers be replaced uncritically with a mean gas-phase bond-enthalpy estimate?
<!-- /task:mixed-c -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Track the direction and amount basis of energy; choose which comparison equation must be reversed.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Liquid q=75.0×4.18×4.0=1254 J, reaction q=−1.254 kJ, ΔH=−1.254/.0200=−62.7≈**−63 kJ mol−1**(rise 2sf). An open cup approximates constant pressure; after thermal corrections and with no significant non-pV work, reaction heat corresponds to ΔH. Loss reduces rise, making result less negative. Add first equation to reverse second: C(graphite)+½O₂(g)→CO(g), −394+283=**−111 kJ mol−1**. CO₂ cancels. Mean bond enthalpies are gas-phase averages, not exact state/environment-specific data; graphite is not gaseous atomic C. If you subtracted reaction heats without reversing an equation, use [Hess cycles](/alevel/as-chemistry/hess-and-bond-enthalpies/); sign/mass errors return to [calorimetry](/alevel/as-chemistry/energetics-and-calorimetry/). Later task A changes the energy direction.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="mixed-d">A curve and a sealed vessel</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-d -->
A constructed gas-production record gives times 0,10,20,30,40 s and gas volumes 0,18,30,38,40 cm3. Find average volume-production rate over 0–20 s. A tangent at 20 s passes through(15 s,25 cm3) and(25 s,35 cm3): find instantaneous rate there. Distinguish either rate from the final gas amount. Sketch equal-particle-number Maxwell–Boltzmann curves for T2 > T1, mark Ea and explain a temperature increase and a catalyst separately. In another sealed 2.00 dm3 vessel at constant temperature, H2(g)+I2(g)⇌2HI(g) reaches equilibrium: initially 0.100 mol each H2/I2, no HI; equilibrium HI is 0.120 mol. Calculate Kc and its units. The forward reaction is endothermic: predict the effect of higher temperature on Kc and compare adding a catalyst. Explain why equal equilibrium concentrations are unnecessary.
<!-- /task:mixed-d -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Keep slope, final extent, equilibrium composition and equilibrium constant as distinct quantities.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Average30/20=**1.50cm³s−1**; tangent(35−25)/(25−15)=**1.00cm³s−1**; final 40cm³ is an amount-related observation, not a rate. Equal MB areas, higher T lower broader peak with a greater high-energy fraction; Ea unchanged by warming. Catalyst provides lower Ea pathway at same T distribution, not hotter molecules. HI = .120 means .0600 mol of each H₂/I₂ consumed; equilibrium .0400 mol each, so concentrations .0200,.0200,.0600 mol dm−3. Kc=.0600²/(.0200×.0200)=**9.00**, units cancel under this concentration convention. HigherT favours endothermic direction, increasing Kc; catalyst accelerates approach without changing Kc/composition. Dynamic forward/reverse rates equal, not concentrations. If you used initial quantities in Kc, recover [equilibrium amounts](/alevel/as-chemistry/equilibrium/); if warming moved Ea, recover [energy distributions](/alevel/as-chemistry/kinetics/). Retry the changed equilibrium in later task B.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="mixed-e">Combine a formula, a spectrum and a reaction</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-e -->
An intact singly charged molecular ion has m/z 60.0573. Candidate formulae are C3H8O and C2H4O2. Use relative isotope masses C = 12.0000, H = 1.0078, O = 15.9949. A validated IR record has broad O–H absorption 3200–3550 cm−1 and no C=O band. On oxidation with warm acidified dichromate, the isolated organic product has molecular formula C3H6O, a C=O band and gives negative Tollens under a valid test; remaining candidates are propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol and methoxyethane. Identify the starting structure with a chain of evidence and state what exact mass alone cannot decide. Write its oxidation using[O], choose a halogenoalkane precursor and conditions, then draw the complete mechanism for dehydrating the alcohol to an alkene. Include one reason negative test evidence needs a positive reference. Do not use NMR.
<!-- /task:mixed-e -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Use each observation to narrow a different structural question; mass formula is not connectivity.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
C₃H₈O=36+8.0624+15.9949=**60.0573**; C₂H₄O₂=24+4.0312+31.9898=60.0210. IR supports alcohol, excluding ether; oxidation to a non-Tollens carbonyl supports secondary propan-2-ol→propanone (not propan-1-ol→aldehyde/acid under altered conditions). Exact mass alone cannot distinguish C₃H₈O isomers; supplied candidates/valid oxidation evidence matter. CH₃CH(OH)CH₃+[O]→CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O. Precursor2-bromopropane, warm aqueous NaOH favours substitution. Dehydration: heat with acid catalyst; O lone pair→H⁺ gives O⁺H₂, C–O bond→O loses H₂O to secondary carbocation; water/base lone pair→neighbour H, C–H pair→C–C forms propene and regenerates H⁺. Every intermediate charge/arrow origin required. A failed reagent may produce false negative; known aldehyde positive reference tests method validity. Tollens must be freshly prepared/handled under approved supervision, never stored. Model/check failures return to [analysis](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-analysis/), [conditions](/alevel/as-chemistry/halogenoalkanes/) or [alcohol mechanisms](/alevel/as-chemistry/alcohols/); later taskB changes the compound.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="mixed-f">Design two products and assess a claim</h3>
<!-- task:mixed-f -->
Ethene is used industrially to make epoxyethane. Give the oxidant, catalyst and balanced equation. Draw acid-catalysed ring opening with water and separately methanol, including complete electron-pair arrows, charges, deprotonation and catalyst regeneration. Name/draw the water product and explain a use. A company says “the ring-opening products are useful, so production has no environmental or safety cost”: evaluate with a specific risk and a benefit. Separately draw E and Z forms of CH3CH=C(Cl)CH2CH3 using CIP priorities, and explain why ethene itself has no E/Z pair.
<!-- /task:mixed-f -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Track which ring bond opens and which reagent supplies the new O group; do not attack a protonated ring using an incompatible unexplained hydroxide model.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
2C₂H₄+O₂→2C₂H₄O with silver catalyst at elevated temperature. Protonate ring O by its lone pair→H⁺; water or methanol O lone pair→ring C, that C–O ring bond→ring O. Deprotonate the newly attached oxonium O to regenerate H⁺. Water gives HOCH₂CH₂OH, ethane-1,2-diol (antifreeze/feedstock); methanol gives HOCH₂CH₂OCH₃, not a three-carbon contiguous chain. Useful downstream surfactants/antifreeze do not remove toxic/carcinogenic, flammability/explosion and energy/emission costs of epoxyethane; enclosed controlled manufacture is essential. CIP: left CH₃>H, right Cl>CH₂CH₃ by directly attached atomic number; Z high priority same side, E opposite. Ethene has identical H substituents on each C, so fails distinct-substituent criterion. Mechanism error→[epoxyethane](/alevel/as-chemistry/epoxyethane/); priority error→[organic representations](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-foundations/). After correction, redraw ring opening with ethanol before checking the model again.
</details>
</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="later-a">Later task A</h3>
<!-- task:later-a -->
A constructed elemental spectrum has mononuclear 1+ peaks at 63 and 65 with signals 3:1. Find Ar and explain why there need be no peak there. A 0.3980 g hydrated salt loses 0.1800 g water on complete heating without decomposition and leaves 0.2180 g anhydrous salt of molar mass 218.0g mol−1. Find waters per formula unit using 18.0g mol−1. Explain why residual water would affect this inference. Separately, the same final solution state is reached by dissolving anhydrous salt (ΔH1) or its hydrate (ΔH2=+12 kJ mol−1). Hydration anhydrous+nH2O(l)→hydrate has ΔH=−45 kJ mol−1. Construct the Hess relation and find ΔH1; do not infer an actual salt identity from these constructed numbers.
<!-- /task:later-a -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Match a weighted mixture to isotope data and matching endpoints to an energy cycle.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Ar=(63×3+65)/4=**63.5**,a mixture mean not a new isotope. n(water)=.1800/18=.0100 mol; n(salt)=.2180/218=.00100 mol; n=**10**. Residual water gives too little mass loss and too much apparent anhydrous mass, thus inferred hydration number too small. Direct dissolution equals hydration then hydrated dissolution: ΔH₁=−45+12=**−33 kJ mol−1**. Equal final solution state and solvent amount accounting are essential. If any fail, redo [weighting](/alevel/as-chemistry/atomic-structure/#isotope-evidence),[formula ratios](/alevel/as-chemistry/formulae-and-gases/) or [Hess](/alevel/as-chemistry/hess-and-bond-enthalpies/) then change the peak ratio or cycle direction and retry with the answer closed.
</details>
</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="later-b">Later task B</h3>
<!-- task:later-b -->
For a homogeneous 2.00 dm3 equilibrium A(g)⇌2B(g), initial A is 0.100 mol and B is 0; equilibrium B is 0.120 mol. Determine Kc including units and predict the composition shift after compression at the same temperature. Does Kc change? In a separate organic problem an unknown C2H6O has a broad O–H IR band; controlled oxidation with removal of the volatile product gives a Tollens-positive carbonyl. Identify starting compound and carbonyl, write the [O] equation, choose distillation or reflux and justify. In a third fresh aqueous sample, warming with NaOH releases a gas that turns damp red litmus blue. State the supported ion inference, write its ionic equation and explain why the paper must not touch the alkaline liquid. Write one connected English explanation of an evidence limitation across these three investigations.
<!-- /task:later-b -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Use equilibrium concentrations rather than initial amounts, and distinguish vapour/gas evidence from a liquid contaminating a test.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
n(B) = .120 mol means .0600 mol A reacted, remaining A=.0400 mol. [A]=.0200, [B]=.0600 mol dm−3; Kc=.0600²/.0200=**.180 mol dm−3**. Compression favours fewer gas moles, left; constant T leaves Kc unchanged. C₂H₆O with OH supports ethanol, controlled oxidation/distillation gives ethanal:CH₃CH₂OH+[O]→CH₃CHO+H₂O; remove the product to limit further oxidation; reflux favours retention/further reaction. Positive Tollens requires validated fresh reagent/reference and candidate context. Gas supports NH₄⁺:NH₄⁺+OH⁻→NH₃+H₂O; alkaline liquid on paper could turn it blue without gas. Example: “Constant temperature fixes Kc even though compression changes equilibrium amounts. A positive chemical test supports an inference only when the method and controls are valid; direct alkali contamination would not demonstrate ammonia.” Repair [Kc](/alevel/as-chemistry/equilibrium/),[preparation](/alevel/as-chemistry/alcohols/) or [test inference](/alevel/as-chemistry/qualitative-analysis/) according to the failed step, then attempt a fresh problem from that page.
</details>
</div>
