<h2 id="start-here">Which electrons hold the structure together?</h2>

A formula counts atoms; it does not yet tell you whether the material contains molecules, a lattice of ions or delocalised electrons. Here you will build bonding representations, predict three-dimensional shape and decide whether bond dipoles cancel. Start with [electron counts](/alevel/as-chemistry/atomic-structure/#electron-configurations) and charge conservation. All questions and diagrams are self-written; no official marks are assigned.

<h2 id="ionic">Electron transfer makes ions; attraction holds a lattice</h2>

A Group 1 atom commonly loses one outer electron to make M⁺, Group 2 loses two to make M²⁺; Group 17 commonly gains one to make X⁻, Group 16 gains two to make X²⁻. These are simple-ion predictions, not a rule that every element has only one oxidation state. Ionic bonding is electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions throughout a giant lattice. Transfer explains formation, whereas attraction explains the bond. Each Na⁺ in NaCl is attracted to surrounding Cl⁻, not exclusively to its original partner.

Common compound ions must remain intact when balancing charge: sulfate SO₄²⁻, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, ammonium NH₄⁺. For example Mg²⁺ needs two NO₃⁻: Mg(NO₃)₂, with charge +2−2=0. The brackets multiply the whole ion. The formula is the simplest ion ratio, not a separate three-ion molecule.

<h2 id="sharing">Shared pairs, lone pairs and a donated pair</h2>

A covalent bond is the attraction between two nuclei and a shared electron pair. A single line is one shared pair, a double bond two and a triple bond three. Nonbonding pairs are lone pairs. For H₂O, oxygen contributes six outer electrons and the two H atoms one each: eight electrons form two O–H pairs and two oxygen lone pairs. Dot/cross symbols identify origins only; all electrons are identical. Hydrogen needs a two-electron shell, not eight. Expanded outer-shell descriptions are used for specified heavier-atom examples below; an octet is not a universal counting law.

A coordinate (dative covalent) bond forms when both electrons of the shared pair come from one donor. NH₃ has three N–H bonds and one lone pair. H⁺ has no electron; donation of the N pair makes NH₄⁺. Draw a straight bond arrow N→H to record the donor-to-acceptor origin. Once formed, the four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are equivalent: one does not remain an intrinsically weaker special bond. A curved electron-pair mechanism arrow instead records a change in electron location, and an equation arrow connects reactants to products. These three arrow jobs are different.

<h2 id="metallic">Mobile electrons belong to the whole metal</h2>

Metallic bonding is electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ion cores and delocalised electrons. In Mg the outer electrons are no longer attached to one chosen atom. The bulk solid is neutral; do not label every particle Mg²⁺ while omitting the balancing electrons. Mobile electrons carry current in solid and molten metal. A solid ionic lattice contains charged ions too, but they cannot migrate through it; mere presence of charge is insufficient for conductivity.

<h2 id="shape">Count repulsion regions before naming the shape</h2>

Electron-pair repulsion predicts an arrangement that minimises repulsion around a central atom. Find its bonds and remaining lone pairs, then count regions: a multiple bond points in one direction and counts as one region for shape. The region arrangement includes lone pairs; the **molecular shape** names atom positions only. Lone pair–lone pair repulsion > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair in this model, so lone pairs generally compress adjacent bond angles.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Self-written example.** NH₃ has three bonding pairs and one lone pair on N: four regions, arranged approximately tetrahedrally. Only three regions contain H, so the molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal, H–N–H about 107°, below the tetrahedral 109.5°. H₂O has two bonding and two lone pairs: bent, about 104.5°. Neither molecule is flat trigonal just because a paper drawing is flat. Draw a solid wedge towards you and a dashed bond away; a normal line lies in the page.
</div>

| Regions around centre | No lone pairs: example, shape, angles | With lone pairs: examples and molecular shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | CO₂: linear, 180° | Each C=O is one region |
| 3 | BF₃: trigonal planar, 120° | A lone pair with two bonded atoms gives bent, <120° |
| 4 | CH₄: tetrahedral, 109.5° | NH₃ pyramidal ~107°; H₂O bent ~104.5° |
| 5 | PCl₅: trigonal bipyramidal, 90°/120°/180° | SF₄ seesaw; ClF₃ T-shaped; XeF₂ linear |
| 6 | SF₆: octahedral, 90°/180° | IF₅ square pyramidal; XeF₄ square planar |

Five-region arrangements have three equatorial directions 120° apart and two axial directions perpendicular to that plane. A lone pair prefers an equatorial site: fewer close 90° interactions. In six-region arrangements, two lone pairs occupy opposite positions, giving square planar XeF₄. Lone pairs distort ideal angles; quote approximate 90°/180° for these higher-region shapes unless more accurate data are supplied, rather than inventing a precise universal compression. For an ion, add an electron per negative charge and remove one per positive charge in the initial count.

The schematic below expands the two region arrangements: use wedges/dashes to preserve depth. Removing an atom position and replacing it with a lone pair changes the molecular shape; keep the region arrangement as the starting scaffold.

<h2 id="polarity">A polar bond does not decide the whole molecule</h2>

**Electronegativity** is an atom’s ability to attract the electron pair in a covalent bond. More electronegative O attracts an O–H bond pair: Oδ−–Hδ+. This is partial, not full ionic charge. A permanent molecular dipole is the vector result of all bond dipoles and the distribution of charge.

CO₂ has two polar C=O bonds in opposite directions; equal dipoles cancel, so no permanent dipole. Bent H₂O has polar bonds that do not cancel. Symmetric tetrahedral CCl₄ also cancels, whereas CHCl₃ has unequal bond surroundings and a permanent dipole. Sketch the shape before adding arrows towards the more electronegative atom. The arrow for a bond dipole describes charge separation, not transfer of a whole electron or a reaction. Nonpolar molecules still have temporary induced dipoles, explored [next](/alevel/as-chemistry/structure-and-forces/).

<h3 id="polarity-check">Test a molecular claim</h3>
<!-- task:polarity-check -->
Define electronegativity. Draw shape and partial charges for BF3, NH3 and CH2Cl2, then decide which have permanent dipoles. F and Cl attract a bond pair more than their bonded B or C; N attracts more than H. Explain why “different elements always make a polar molecule” fails and why “nonpolar means no attractions” also fails.
<!-- /task:polarity-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Add directions, not a count of polar bonds.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Electronegativity is ability to attract a covalent bonding pair. BF₃ is trigonal planar; Bδ⁺/Fδ⁻ with three equal symmetric dipoles cancelling. NH₃ is pyramidal, Nδ⁻/Hδ⁺, nonzero permanent dipole. CH₂Cl₂ is tetrahedral with each C–Cl bond labelled Cδ⁺–Clδ⁻ (these are bond-specific partial charges), and C–Cl dipoles towards Cl; the two C–Cl directions do not oppose exactly, so a permanent dipole remains. Mixed elements can have cancelling dipoles, and all molecules have dispersion forces. If you flattened tetrahedral CH₂Cl₂ to cancel its bonds, repair [the shape](#shape) before retrying CO₂.
</details>
</div>
