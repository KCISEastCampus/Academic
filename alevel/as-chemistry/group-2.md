<h2 id="start-here">When does a white solid identify an ion?</h2>

A white solid can be a reaction product, an undissolved starting material or contamination. Its colour alone does not identify it. Here you will connect the outer electrons of Mg–Ba to their reactions, then choose tests whose observations support a particular conclusion.

You need atomic nuclei and electron shells, ion charges, acid–base reactions and balancing. Use [amounts and equations](/alevel/as-chemistry/amount-and-equations/) to repair formula ratios and [solutions](/alevel/as-chemistry/solutions-and-titration/#concentration) for concentration. The electron, bonding and redox ideas needed below are explained here. This covers OxfordAQA 9620 AS §3.2.2 and the Group 2/sulfate part of Required practical 3; it is not the whole practical. All tasks and sample records are self-written/constructed, with no official mark allocations. Paper decisions do not certify practical competence.

<h2 id="electrons-and-reactivity">The same outer pattern, a different attraction</h2>

Mg, Ca, Sr and Ba each have two outer electrons, in an **ns²** arrangement: Mg [Ne]3s², Ca [Ar]4s², Sr [Kr]5s², Ba [Xe]6s². Brackets represent filled inner shells. Losing the two outer electrons gives M²⁺; an atom does not start with a 2+ charge. In reactions where the metal loses electrons it is **oxidised**, and it acts as a **reducing agent** by supplying electrons to something else.

Down the group there are more occupied shells. The outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons. Although proton number increases, the increased distance and shielding outweigh that increase for the outer electron: its attraction to the nucleus is weaker. **First ionisation energy** therefore decreases. This is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms:

$$\mathrm{M(g)\rightarrow M^+(g)+e^-}$$

Do not substitute a solid metal or M²⁺ into that definition. Removing both outer electrons involves two successive ionisations. Easier electron loss helps explain the increasing reactivity with water from Mg to Ba; ionisation energy alone is not a reaction-rate equation. Surface coating, temperature and exposed area also affect an observation.

<h3 id="melting">Melting is a different process from electron removal</h3>

A metal is a giant structure of positive ion cores attracted to delocalised electrons. Melting disrupts the ordered metallic structure; it does not turn every atom into a gaseous ion. For Ca→Sr→Ba the ion cores are larger with the same outer-electron contribution per atom, so attraction to the delocalised electrons generally weakens and less energy is needed to melt the metal.

| Element | First ionisation energy / kJ mol−1 | Melting point / K |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mg | 738 | 922 |
| Ca | 590 | 1112 |
| Sr | 550 | 1042 |
| Ba | 503 | 998 |

These approximate reference values are from Lister/Renshaw (2016), p.160, table 1; they are not class measurements. **Mg is an exception to the simple melting-point trend**: Ca has a higher melting point than Mg. Crystal structure also matters. The simple size argument explains the Ca–Ba trend but does not predict the entire Mg–Ba melting sequence; do not invent a smooth decrease or claim melting breaks covalent molecules.

<h2 id="water-and-oxygen">State the conditions before choosing the product</h2>

Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water. Hydrogen may be difficult to see over a short time; the surface oxide and poorly soluble hydroxide layer hinder contact. “No visible change in one minute” does not establish that no reaction is possible. Its cold-water reaction is represented by:

$$\mathrm{Mg(s)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow Mg(OH)_2(s)+H_2(g)}$$

Heated magnesium reacts much more readily with **steam**, giving the oxide instead:

$$\mathrm{Mg(s)+H_2O(g)\rightarrow MgO(s)+H_2(g)}$$

Ca, Sr and Ba react with cold water increasingly vigorously, forming hydroxide and hydrogen. For calcium in excess water:

$$\mathrm{Ca(s)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow Ca(OH)_2(aq)+H_2(g)}$$

Some Ca(OH)2 can form a white suspension when its solubility is exceeded; `(aq)` describes the dissolved product, not a promise that the mixture stays clear at every concentration. Sr/Ba hydroxides are more soluble. Effervescence is an observation; “hydrogen evolved” is an inference supported by the reaction and, if needed, a teacher-controlled small gas test. Do not bring a flame near a reacting metal to find out.

As an oxygen-reaction comparison, heated magnesium burns with an intense white light and forms a white oxide in oxygen:

$$\mathrm{2Mg(s)+O_2(g)\rightarrow 2MgO(s)}$$

Mg changes from oxidation state 0 to +2 and oxygen from 0 to −2: total electron loss and gain are both four per balanced equation. This is a redox reaction, not a reaction producing hydrogen. Burning in air can produce other products as well; do not assume a pure oxide yield. Some heavier Group 2 metals also form peroxides under suitable oxygen conditions, so this Mg example is not a universal “only MO” rule. Oxygen chemistry is supporting context here, not an additional list of AS §3.2.2 requirements.

<h3 id="titanium">Worked example: why magnesium extracts titanium</h3>

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Self-written explanation — an industrial reaction, not a school procedure.** Titanium(IV) chloride is reduced using magnesium at high temperature under an inert atmosphere. We choose an electron-transfer explanation because titanium changes oxidation state:

$$\mathrm{TiCl_4+2Mg\rightarrow Ti+2MgCl_2}$$

Ti is +4 in TiCl4 and 0 in Ti, so it gains four electrons. Each Mg changes from 0 to +2 and supplies two; therefore two Mg atoms are needed for every Ti atom. Chlorine remains −1. Mg is the reducing agent and is itself oxidised; it is not a catalyst. Atom check: one Ti, four Cl and two Mg on each side. We omit state symbols here because this overall industrial equation does not specify the exact operating temperature or phase arrangement. The electron accounting, not a guess based on a white product, explains the 2:1 ratio.
</div>

<h2 id="dissolving-and-reacting">A soluble hydroxide is not a more reactive metal</h2>

An ionic solid is an extended lattice, not a collection of separate M(OH)2 molecules. **Dissolving** transfers ions from the solid into water. For the fraction of magnesium hydroxide that dissolves:

$$\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2(s)\rightleftharpoons Mg^{2+}(aq)+2OH^-(aq)}$$

Most remains solid because it is sparingly soluble. Down Mg→Ca→Sr→Ba, hydroxide solubility **increases**. Mg(OH)2 forms a suspension; Ca(OH)2 is sparingly soluble and its clear saturated solution is limewater; Sr/Ba hydroxides are more soluble. More dissolving can supply more OH− to a saturated solution at a given temperature. Comparing arbitrary unsaturated solutions at unknown concentrations does not establish this trend.

For **sulfates the direction reverses**: MgSO4 is soluble, CaSO4 sparingly soluble, SrSO4 very poorly soluble and BaSO4 effectively insoluble for these tests. A white solid can appear when aqueous Ba²⁺ and sulfate meet. This is not evidence that barium metal was present.

Both trends involve the balance between holding ions in a solid and stabilising them in water. Changing the anion changes that balance; outer-electron loss alone cannot predict solubility. You need the observed relative trends at AS, not an A2 lattice/hydration-energy calculation or a solubility-product calculation.

**Oxides require another distinction.** CaO reacts with water, releasing heat, to form a different compound:

$$\mathrm{CaO(s)+H_2O(l)\rightarrow Ca(OH)_2(s)}$$

This equation represents slaking with limited water. In excess water some hydroxide dissolves; excess solid may remain. MgO reacts much less readily with water and remains largely as solid in a short cold-water comparison. Loss of a starting oxide is not simply dissolution of unchanged MO into free oxide ions in water. Do not label these observations a measured monotonic “oxide solubility” trend.

<h3 id="uses">Choose a compound for what it does</h3>

Sparingly soluble Mg(OH)2 is used in antacid suspensions. The dissolved OH− reacts with acid; as it is removed, more solid can dissolve and neutralise further acid. Low solubility does not mean the solid is unable to neutralise acid:

$$\mathrm{Mg(OH)_2(s)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow Mg^{2+}(aq)+2H_2O(l)}$$

Ca(OH)2 neutralises acidity in agricultural soil, raising soil pH; the amount must suit the soil rather than be added without limit. Soluble Ba compounds must not be substituted. In medical X-ray contrast preparations, BaSO4 combines strong X-ray absorption by barium with extremely low solubility, limiting release of toxic Ba²⁺. This use concerns a professionally prepared material, not ingesting a laboratory precipitate. MgSO4, by contrast, is soluble and has medicinal uses such as a laxative; it is not an insoluble contrast agent.

CaO or CaCO3 removes acidic SO2 from flue gases through chemical reaction. Simple overall equations for the sulfite stage are:

$$\mathrm{CaO(s)+SO_2(g)\rightarrow CaSO_3(s)}$$

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)+SO_2(g)\rightarrow CaSO_3(s)+CO_2(g)}$$

In an oxidising scrubber the sulfite can be converted to sulfate: $\mathrm{2CaSO_3+O_2\rightarrow2CaSO_4}$. Real wet processes involve suspensions and can produce hydrated gypsum. The first equations explain acid-gas removal; they do not say that all industrial solid is anhydrous sulfite. A benefit is reduced SO2 release; CaCO3 consumption also releases CO2, so removing one pollutant is not proof of zero environmental cost.

<h2 id="sulfate-evidence">Why acid comes before barium chloride</h2>

BaCl2 provides aqueous Ba²⁺. If sulfate is present at a detectable concentration, a white BaSO4 precipitate forms:

$$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}(aq)+SO_4^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)}$$

There is one Ba, one S and four O on either side; net charge is zero on both sides. Na⁺ or Mg²⁺ from the sample and Cl− from the reagent are spectators and are omitted. A net ionic equation identifies the ions forming the new solid, not every ion in the tube.

Carbonate can also form a white barium precipitate:

$$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}(aq)+CO_3^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow BaCO_3(s)}$$

So adding BaCl2 first makes a white solid ambiguous. On a fresh sample, add **dilute hydrochloric acid first**, mixing carefully until carbonate has reacted and the sample remains acidic. Allow any effervescence to finish before adding BaCl2:

$$\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}(aq)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow CO_2(g)+H_2O(l)}$$

Acid also neutralises OH− if present: $\mathrm{H^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)\rightarrow H_2O(l)}$. The point of acidification is to remove an interfering species, not to make BaSO4 white. Dilute HCl does not introduce sulfate. **Do not use H2SO4 to prepare a sample for a sulfate test**: it supplies the very ion being investigated. Dilute nitric acid can also avoid adding sulfate in this test, but follow the given method; acid choices must be reconsidered for a different test. HCl would introduce chloride into a later chloride test, so use separate fresh portions for separate analyses.

Add small portions rather than flooding the tube: the sequence and first visible change must remain observable, the volume must stay safe, and unnecessary dilution can hide a weak precipitate. Enough acid is required to remove the possible carbonate, not a fixed universal “two drops”. Enough BaCl2 must then be added to give a detectable test with the validated method. More reagent is not automatically better, and precipitate colour/intensity alone cannot quantify sulfate.

<h3 id="worked-test">Worked example: a mixture can give two observations</h3>

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Self-written, constructed case.** A solution may contain sulfate, carbonate, both or neither; these are the only interfering anions allowed in this model. We take a fresh portion because an earlier addition could change its composition. Dilute HCl gives effervescence; after enough acid has reacted and the liquid remains acidic, BaCl2 gives a white precipitate. A reagent blank stays clear and a sulfate reference gives a white precipitate under the same method.

Record “effervescence with HCl” and “white precipitate after subsequent BaCl2” in the observation column. In this restricted model the first supports carbonate, through acid releasing CO2; the second supports sulfate because the carbonate interference has been removed. The two net equations are the carbonate/acid and barium/sulfate equations above. A separate teacher-approved limewater test can support the gas identity if needed: CO2(g) + Ca(OH)2(aq) → CaCO3(s) + H2O(l), initially turning limewater milky. Prolonged excess CO2 can clear it, so the initial change matters.

The positive reference checks that the reagents/method can reveal sulfate; the blank checks contamination. Neither allows us to claim every possible unknown chemical has been excluded. “Sulfate detected under these conditions” is stronger evidence than “a white solid was seen”, and more defensible than “the sample is pure sodium sulfate”.
</div>

<h2 id="safe-comparison">Compare cations without changing the question</h2>

For a Group 2 salt, adding OH− can form M(OH)2; adding sulfate can form MSO4. Mg and Ba give a useful contrast: Mg(OH)2 precipitates readily in a suitable NaOH test but MgSO4 remains dissolved; a matched dilute Ba²⁺ sample can remain clear with NaOH yet precipitate with sulfate. Use reference samples under the same conditions. Do not identify Ca versus Sr merely by guessing which white precipitate looks “slighter”. Concentration, amount added, mixing and observation time can alter the apparent result.

Use **separate fresh portions** for the hydroxide and sulfate comparisons. First observe a small addition, then add more dropwise to the prescribed excess, recording whether the solid persists. This distinguishes initial formation from later behaviour; excess reagent does not mean unlimited concentrated alkali or that every solid must dissolve. Group 2 hydroxide precipitates in these tests are not expected to show the amphoteric redissolution used for some other metals. That wider complex-ion chemistry is outside this lesson.

These are planning tasks. In supervised work, wear splash eye protection, use the teacher-approved dilute reagents and small quantities, keep tubes below half full and pointed away from people, and never taste or directly sniff unknowns. Soluble barium salts are toxic; use dedicated droppers, avoid contact and collect barium-containing waste as instructed, not in the sink. Dilute acids/alkalis can damage eyes: add dropwise, mix gently and report splashes immediately. Metal/water reactions release heat and flammable hydrogen; Sr/Ba demonstrations and burning Mg require separate teacher control, shielding/eye precautions and ignition control. Do not look directly at burning Mg. These controls address hazards; repeating a test does not remove them.

Neither establishes original sulfate: bubbles support carbonate in the restricted model, while the precipitate could use sulfate supplied by H2SO4. Start again with fresh R and dilute HCl; after enough acid has removed carbonate, allow effervescence to stop and add BaCl2. A blank treated identically should stay clear, and a known sulfate reference should precipitate. With both salts: effervescence at the HCl stage, then white precipitate after BaCl2. CO3²−(aq) + 2H+(aq) → CO2(g) + H2O(l); Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²−(aq) → BaSO4(s). HCl added later does not remove the sulfate introduced by H2SO4, so the original treated portion cannot answer the question. If you identified the bubbles as sulfate evidence, close this answer and label the species responsible for each change again.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="retry-evidence">Change the control, change the judgement</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
**Self-written, constructed case.**
<!-- task:retry-evidence -->
A properly acidified unknown stays clear after BaCl2 addition, but so does a known detectable sulfate reference; the blank is clear. A student repeats all three using the same bottles and gets the same results. What can be concluded about the unknown, and what should be checked next? Separately, a Mg(OH)2 suspension loses solid when dilute acid is added. Explain why this does not reverse the pure-water hydroxide solubility trend, and give the net ionic equation.
<!-- /task:retry-evidence -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
A clear negative control is only half the evidence that a method is functioning. In the second case the solvent conditions have changed chemically.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
The failed positive reference means the test has not demonstrated detection ability; even “not detected by a functioning test” is unjustified for this run. Check bottle identity, reagent addition and reference preparation/concentration with fresh validated materials before retesting. Reproducible failure is still failure. Acid consumes hydroxide and allows further solid to react: Mg(OH)2(s) + 2H+(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2H2O(l). This is not a measurement of solubility in pure water. A good repair addresses both the invalid control and the changed chemical conditions, rather than merely saying “repeat”.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="delayed-check">Return later without the method headings</h2>

After another chemistry topic, close this page and attempt the following in English. Record your first answer, any help used, the error and the new attempt. This is a self-check opportunity, not evidence that retention has already been tested.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
**Self-written, constructed case.**
<!-- task:delayed-check -->
A laboratory receives a colourless solution S that may contain sulfate and/or carbonate, and a separate request to reduce acidity in agricultural soil. Choose one coherent sequence to investigate S, explaining how you avoid a misleading white solid, and give an observation/inference pair plus the net ionic equation for the final test. Choose between Ca(OH)2 and Ba(OH)2 for the soil request and explain its chemical action and one risk of excessive treatment. Finally justify the prediction that Ba loses an outer electron more easily than Mg while explaining why that prediction alone cannot rank the water solubilities of their sulfates. State what a white reagent blank would do to your conclusion about S.
<!-- /task:delayed-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Keep the unknown solution, the soil and the atomic model as three different systems. Choose conditions for each before connecting the evidence.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Fresh S → dilute HCl with mixing until carbonate is removed and the sample remains acidic → let any effervescence finish → BaCl2; compare an identically treated clear blank and a precipitating sulfate reference. A white precipitate after this sequence is an observation supporting detectable sulfate in the restricted model; Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²−(aq) → BaSO4(s). A white blank blocks attribution to S until the fault is resolved. Choose Ca(OH)2 to neutralise soil acidity and raise pH; over-treatment can make pH unsuitable. Ba(OH)2 is not a safe substitute because soluble Ba compounds are toxic. Ba's greater outer-electron distance and inner-shell shielding outweigh its higher nuclear charge, lowering first ionisation energy. Sulfate solubility concerns a different balance between solid and hydrated ions, not loss of electrons by a metal atom. If one part fails, return to that explanation and use its retry before attempting this combined task again.
</details>
