<h2 id="start-here">One starting molecule, several possible products</h2>

A halogenoalkane contains a halogen bonded to a saturated carbon. Replacing that halogen can make an alcohol, a nitrile or an amine; removing a neighbouring hydrogen as well can make an alkene. The starting structure alone does not decide which product is favoured. We need the reagent, solvent and temperature, then a model that accounts for every changing bond and charge.

You will learn to choose conditions, draw electron-pair movements and judge evidence about reaction rates. You need to read simple structural formulae and conserve atoms and charge; the bridge below supplies the new electron-pair language. For revision, attempt [the mixed task](#mixed-check) first and return to the explanation where needed. This page covers OxfordAQA 9620 AS §3.3.3.1–2, not the later A2 chemistry of amines and nitriles.

**All examples, tasks and checking points are self-written (constructed), with no official marks.** Diagrams are original schematics. Record whether you used a hint; a response after opening help is useful practice but is not evidence of unaided performance.

<h2 id="electron-pair-bridge">Follow electrons, rather than moving atom labels</h2>

In these examples a carbon normally has four bonds, neutral oxygen two bonds and two lone pairs, and neutral nitrogen three bonds and one lone pair. A **lone pair** is a pair of outer-shell electrons not used in a bond. OH− has one O–H bond and three oxygen lone pairs; NH3 has three N–H bonds and a nitrogen lone pair. Only the reacting lone pair is drawn in the figures; other nonreacting lone pairs are omitted. A line representing a covalent bond already represents a pair: do not draw an extra pair of dots on the same bond.

Halogens attract the C–X bonding electrons more strongly than carbon does. The bond is polar: $\ce{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$. A partial charge, δ, describes unequal sharing within an otherwise neutral molecule. It does **not** mean that the starting carbon is a free C+ ion. A **nucleophile** donates an electron pair to form a covalent bond. OH− and CN− are negative; NH3 is neutral but still has a pair available to donate.

A full-headed **curly arrow** shows the movement of one electron pair. Its tail must start at a visible lone pair or bond, and its head must reach the atom or bond that receives that pair. A straight reaction arrow only connects reactants and products; it cannot replace these electron arrows. In substitution, the nucleophile supplies a pair to the electron-deficient carbon, and the C–X bond pair leaves with X. This is **heterolytic** bond breaking: the leaving group is X−, not a radical or X+.

CH3CH2– means the same two-carbon fragment wherever it appears; R stands for an alkyl fragment that remains attached. In an elimination picture, expand the particular C–H bond that changes even if other groups are condensed. A **neighbouring carbon** means one connected directly by a C–C bond to the carbon bearing X. For recovery use [organic foundations](/alevel/as-chemistry/organic-foundations/) and [bonding and shape](/alevel/as-chemistry/bonding-and-shape/); the bridge here supplies what this lesson needs.

<h2 id="substitution">Replace the halogen with an electron-pair donor</h2>

Warm a halogenoalkane with aqueous sodium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide to favour substitution to an alcohol. Heating under reflux lets a mixture react while a condenser returns vapour, reducing loss of volatile material; reflux is not a reaction type. Na+ or K+ is a spectator, so the mechanism shows OH− separately, not a covalent K–O–H molecule. A water-rich medium matters; aqueous reactions can use some ethanol to help the organic compound mix with water.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

Choose the carbon bonded to Br because its C–Br bond is polar and that bond must be replaced. Draw an oxygen lone pair on OH−. Draw its arrow to this carbon and a second arrow from the C–Br bond to Br. These two movements describe bond formation and breaking together in the introductory substitution model; do not add an isolated five-bond carbon as a stable intermediate.

<h2 id="other-nucleophiles">Changing the donor changes the new functional group</h2>

The carbon still loses X, but the donor atom determines its new bond. A **nitrile** contains –C≡N; a primary **amine** contains –NH2 attached to one carbon group. These structures are introduced here so that you can identify products without importing their later reactions.

<h3 id="cyanide">Cyanide adds a carbon to the chain</h3>

Heat with KCN or NaCN in aqueous ethanol, commonly under reflux. In this reaction CN− donates through its **carbon** lone pair, forming a C–C bond. The triple bond inside CN is retained. Bromoethane therefore becomes CH3CH2C≡N, **propanenitrile**: count the nitrile carbon as part of the three-carbon chain. Writing CH3CH2N≡C would change the connectivity.

<div class="table-responsive" role="region" aria-label="Worked cyanide mechanism, scroll horizontally" tabindex="0" style="overflow-x:auto" markdown="1">

![Carbon-end lone pair of CN minus attacks the carbon bonded to Br; the C–Br bond pair goes to Br. Bromoethane becomes three-carbon propanenitrile plus Br minus.](/assets/img/chemistry-as-halogenoalkanes-cyanide.svg){: width="680" height="340" style="min-width:680px" }

</div>

$$\ce{CH3CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CN + Br-}$$

Charge stays −1; the additional carbon comes from CN−. A single negative sign represents the ion's overall charge, not a requirement to place an arrow at that sign. Start the arrow at the carbon lone pair. Nitrile hydrolysis and reduction are not part of this lesson.

<h3 id="ammonia">Neutral ammonia first makes a charged intermediate</h3>

Heat with **excess concentrated ammonia in ethanol, under pressure in suitable apparatus**. Ammonia is volatile; pressure-rated equipment is needed, not an improvised sealed test tube. The N lone pair makes the new C–N bond. Nitrogen then has four bonds and carries +1: the intermediate is an **alkylammonium ion**, here CH3CH2NH3+. A second NH3 accepts a proton: its lone pair points to an N-bound H, and that N–H bond pair returns to the original N. The amine regains a lone pair. This is why neutral NH3 cannot simply be substituted as if it were OH−.

<div class="table-responsive" role="region" aria-label="Worked ammonia mechanism in two stages, scroll horizontally" tabindex="0" style="overflow-x:auto" markdown="1">

![First the nitrogen lone pair of neutral NH3 attacks the Br-bearing carbon and the C–Br pair goes to Br. Then a second NH3 accepts H from positively charged four-bond nitrogen while that N–H pair returns to N, giving ethylamine, NH4 plus and Br minus.](/assets/img/chemistry-as-halogenoalkanes-ammonia.svg){: width="680" height="610" style="min-width:680px" }

</div>

$$\ce{CH3CH2Br + NH3 -> CH3CH2NH3+ + Br-}$$

$$\ce{CH3CH2NH3+ + NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4+}$$

Overall, $\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4+ + Br-}$. The first step has net charge zero on both sides; the second has +1 on both sides. The overall net charge is zero. Excess NH3 favours formation of the primary amine over further substitution by the amine; it does not guarantee a pure product. Further alkylation mechanisms are a later A2 topic.

These are reaction choices to explain on paper, not unsupervised recipes. Cyanides are acutely toxic and must not contact acid, which can release HCN; their use requires a specialist approved method. Volatile halogenoalkanes and ammonia need appropriate ventilation; ethanol is flammable, so avoid flames; hydroxide is corrosive. Actual work requires teacher approval, eye protection, suitable apparatus and controlled waste collection. No practical work has been performed by completing this page.

<h2 id="competing-pathways">The same OH− can have two roles</h2>

When OH− donates a pair to **carbon**, it acts as a nucleophile and replaces X. When it accepts a **proton** from the neighbouring carbon, it acts as a base. Loss of H and X from adjacent carbons allows a C=C bond to form: **elimination**. These reactions compete; changing conditions changes the tendency, rather than switching off every competing reaction.

| Conditions with NaOH/KOH | Favoured route | Role of OH− and expected functional group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water-rich aqueous solution; warm if needed | Substitution | Nucleophile attacks carbon; alcohol |
| Hot, concentrated solution in ethanol, with little water | Elimination | Base removes neighbouring H+; alkene |

Temperature also changes how fast reactions occur. Heating alone does not identify the pathway: aqueous substitution is often heated too. Small amounts of ethanol can serve as a co-solvent in an aqueous preparation. **Ethanol alone is not evidence for elimination**: ethanolic ammonia and cyanide conditions have already given substitution. Always name the reagent, solvent and relevant heating condition together. The structure matters as well: inspect available neighbouring C–H bonds, rather than assuming every halogenoalkane can eliminate.

<h2 id="rate-evidence">Explain a rate trend, then ask what was measured</h2>

For comparable chloro-, bromo- and iodoalkanes with the **same carbon skeleton under the same conditions**, hydrolysis generally becomes faster in the order RCl < RBr < RI. C–I is weaker than C–Br, which is weaker than C–Cl: breaking C–X is easier in the iodoalkane. The strong C–F bond makes fluoroalkanes much less reactive under ordinary hydrolysis conditions. Carbon–halogen bond enthalpy is important, but is not numerically identical to the activation energy of a solution reaction. Do not use it alone to rank unrelated skeletons or solvent conditions.

Polarity explains why the carbon can be attacked; it does not give the observed halogen rate order. Predicting “C–Cl is more polar, so RCl must react faster than RI” ignores the strength of the bond that must break. This compares intramolecular C–X bonds, not intermolecular attractions or boiling points.

One qualitative evidence route uses aqueous silver nitrate and an ethanol–water medium. Water carries out hydrolysis; ethanol helps the poorly water-soluble organic compound mix. Released halide ions give $\ce{Ag+(aq) + X-(aq) -> AgX(s)}$: AgCl is white, AgBr cream and AgI yellow. The **precipitate** is an observation of released halide; silver nitrate does not directly measure alcohol concentration. This comparison mixture is different from the hot ethanolic KOH preparation above. Do not add KOH to this silver-ion comparison, since silver oxide precipitation would interfere.

Pre-equilibrate mixtures in a controlled water bath, start timing on mixing, and keep substrate concentration/amount, total volume, solvent ratio, silver nitrate concentration, mixing and observation criterion consistent. A shorter time to a defined turbidity is a qualitative indicator of faster halide release. Different precipitates have different solubilities and visibility; a visible-onset time does not measure an exact common conversion or a rate constant. For numerical rates, a calibrated concentration–time measurement would be needed. Use repeats to assess spread and a blank to check reagent contamination. A water bath removes the ignition hazard of flames near ethanol, but does not replace ventilation or the teacher's chemical risk assessment; collect silver-containing waste separately.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Choose what must happen to the carbon framework first. Then check the destination of the reagent's pair and the charge on nitrogen.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
For but-1-ene, use hot concentrated ethanolic KOH with little water: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2CH=CH2 + H2O + Br-}$. OH− lone pair → H on the carbon next to CH2Br; that C–H pair → the intervening C–C bond; C–Br pair → Br. For butan-1-amine use excess concentrated ethanolic NH3 and heat under pressure in suitable apparatus: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + NH4+ + Br-}$. Four-bond N must carry + in the alkylammonium intermediate. The departing proton's N–H bond pair must return to N; a second NH3 accepts that proton using its lone pair.

One possible English explanation: “Hot ethanolic hydroxide acts as a base and removes a proton from the neighbouring carbon. The C–H electron pair forms a C=C bond while bromide leaves. Excess ethanolic ammonia instead supplies a nitrogen lone pair to the carbon bearing bromine. The positively charged alkylammonium intermediate loses a proton to form the amine. These conditions favour the target routes but do not guarantee pure products.” Other scientifically consistent wording is acceptable. If only a reagent list was given, add the causal electron-pair explanation; if conditions or arrows were wrong, return to [competition](#competing-pathways) or [ammonia](#ammonia), then redo a retry before this task.
</details>
