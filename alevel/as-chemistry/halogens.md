<h2 id="start-here">Which particle gains electrons, and which loses them?</h2>

Chlorine can oxidise bromide, yet bromide can reduce concentrated sulfuric acid. The names sound similar but the particles and directions of electron transfer differ. This lesson connects molecular properties, displacement, halide tests and controlled uses of chlorine.

Prerequisites: [electron transfer and half-equations](/alevel/as-chemistry/redox-equations/) and [intermolecular attractions](/alevel/as-chemistry/structure-and-forces/). OxfordAQA 9620 AS §3.2.3 includes the solid **sodium** halides with concentrated sulfuric acid, silver-nitrate/ammonia identification, two chlorine–water reactions and chlorine with cold dilute aqueous NaOH. All tasks and observation scenarios here are self-written/constructed; no official marks. Experimental descriptions explain supervised evidence, not home procedures. Fluorine is unsuitable for school experiments; halogens, concentrated acid, ammonia and potentially toxic gases need teacher control and appropriate ventilation. Never identify gases by deliberately smelling them.

<h2 id="molecular-trends">Boiling separates molecules; electronegativity concerns a bond</h2>

Halogens are non-metals with seven outer electrons; their elemental molecules are X2, whereas a halide ion is X−. Chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid, iodine a dark solid with purple vapour under suitable conditions. Their dilute aqueous colours differ: bromine is orange/yellow-brown, iodine brown; colour also depends on concentration and solvent, so record the medium.

Down the group, molecules have more electrons and more polarisable electron clouds. Stronger London dispersion attractions **between molecules** require more energy to overcome, so boiling points increase. The covalent X–X bonds mostly remain intact on boiling. A higher boiling point does not prove that X–X bonds are stronger.

**Electronegativity** is an atom's ability to attract the bonding electron pair in a covalent bond. Down the group, the bonding region is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons; these effects outweigh increasing nuclear charge, so electronegativity decreases. This concerns attraction within a bond, not boiling or the energy of an isolated ion.

<h2 id="electron-transfer">Elements and ions have opposite redox trends</h2>

A halogen acting as an oxidising agent **gains** electrons and is reduced:

$$\mathrm{X_2+2e^-\rightarrow2X^-}$$

Oxidising ability decreases down the group. For the aqueous comparisons here, chlorine oxidises bromide and iodide; bromine oxidises iodide but not chloride; iodine does not oxidise chloride or bromide. The shell/distance/shielding model helps explain decreasing attraction for an incoming electron, but it is not a complete quantitative aqueous-energy calculation. Fluorine reacts with water, so it is not a routine member of an aqueous school displacement matrix.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Worked example — self-written.** Predict chlorine added to sodium bromide solution. Choose the stronger oxidising halogen, Cl2, to accept electrons; bromide supplies them:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+2e^-\rightarrow2Cl^-(aq)}$$

$$\mathrm{2Br^-(aq)\rightarrow Br_2(aq)+2e^-}$$

Adding cancels two electrons:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+2Br^-(aq)\rightarrow2Cl^-(aq)+Br_2(aq)}$$

Atoms and net charge −2 match. Sodium is a spectator. Formation of an orange/yellow-brown aqueous colour supports bromine, compared with reagent/solvent controls; it is not a direct observation of electrons. The reverse combination, bromine plus chloride, has no displacement under the same model.
</div>

A halide acting as a reducing agent instead **loses** electrons: $\mathrm{2X^-\rightarrow X_2+2e^-}$. Reducing ability increases down the group: I− is more readily oxidised than Br−, which is more readily oxidised than Cl−. Its outer electrons are further away/more shielded and more easily lost. Do not transfer the elemental oxidising order unchanged to the ions.

</details>

I− is the stronger reducing ion because it loses electrons more readily; elemental Cl2 is the stronger oxidising halogen. </details>

<h2 id="concentrated-acid">One mixture can have acid–base and redox stages</h2>

The specified comparison is **solid NaCl, NaBr or NaI with concentrated H2SO4**, not dilute acidified aqueous silver nitrate. These reactions can release corrosive/toxic gases; the following are explanations of teacher-controlled evidence, not instructions to generate them. Do not infer gas identity by smell.

Initially, acid liberates a hydrogen halide; for each X = Cl, Br, I:

$$\mathrm{NaX(s)+H_2SO_4(l)\rightarrow NaHSO_4(s)+HX(g)}$$

This proton-transfer stage changes no oxidation states. NaCl gives steamy fumes of HCl; chloride is not a sufficiently strong reducing agent to reduce the sulfuric acid in this comparison. With NaBr, HBr formed in the first stage can also reduce sulfur from +6 to +4, producing SO2 while bromide is oxidised to bromine:

$$\mathrm{2HBr+H_2SO_4\rightarrow Br_2+SO_2+2H_2O}$$

Steamy hydrogen-bromide fumes and orange/brown bromine vapour may both be present; SO2 is colourless. This is additional to the acid–base stage, not an alternative that makes that stage vanish. These overall redox equations omit phases because the hot concentrated reaction mixture and vapour are not all a single aqueous solution.

Iodide is stronger still. Different pathways can reduce sulfur to +4 in SO2, 0 in sulfur, or −2 in H2S. They can occur together; do not demand a single pure gas:

$$\mathrm{2HI+H_2SO_4\rightarrow I_2+SO_2+2H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{6HI+H_2SO_4\rightarrow3I_2+S+4H_2O}$$

$$\mathrm{8HI+H_2SO_4\rightarrow4I_2+H_2S+4H_2O}$$

Iodine can appear dark solid/purple vapour and sulfur a yellow solid; H2S and SO2 are colourless gases requiring controlled evidence rather than colour alone. For the H2S pathway sulfur gains eight electrons (+6→−2), so eight iodide ions must each lose one. This explains the coefficient 8 rather than memorising a row of products. For sulfur the gain is six, and for SO2 it is two. H2SO4 is both the acid in the first stage and an oxidising agent in the subsequent redox stage.

</details>

Br− additionally reduces sulfur to SO2: 2HBr + H2SO4 → Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O. I− can give SO2, S and H2S; the S and H2S equations are 6HI + H2SO4 → 3I2 + S + 4H2O and 8HI + H2SO4 → 4I2 + H2S + 4H2O. Acid fumes can occur without redox, and colourless H2S cannot be identified from white fumes; no smelling test is appropriate. </details>

<h2 id="halide-evidence">Three reagents do three different jobs</h2>

Take a fresh aqueous portion. Add **dilute HNO3 first**, then **AgNO3 solution**. Nitric acid removes carbonate and hydroxide interference without introducing chloride. AgNO3 supplies Ag+ to form a silver halide:

$$\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)+X^-(aq)\rightarrow AgX(s)}$$

Acid consumes CO3²− to CO2 and water and neutralises OH−. Otherwise Ag2CO3 or Ag2O can produce misleading solids. HCl would introduce the ion used to make white AgCl; HNO3 alone is not the precipitating reagent. A procedure that omits AgNO3 is missing the source of silver ions.

| Halide | Precipitate after acid then AgNO3 | Dilute NH3 added to precipitate | Concentrated NH3 comparison |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cl− | white AgCl | dissolves | dissolves |
| Br− | cream AgBr | remains substantially | dissolves |
| I− | yellow AgI | remains | remains |

Add ammonia to the **precipitate-containing mixture**, mixing as directed, not to a fresh sample that never received silver nitrate. Compare validated parallel portions or add the prescribed more concentrated ammonia to the remaining solid. Ammonia binds silver ions in solution, allowing some silver halides to dissolve; different concentrations discriminate AgCl/AgBr/AgI. You need the observations and reagent reasons, not a full A2 complex-ion derivation. Use appropriate excess from the approved method; a tiny ineffective drop does not establish insolubility. Colours alone may be hard to distinguish, so dissolution provides a second line of evidence. Fluoride is outside this three-halide identification: AgF is soluble, so a negative precipitation test does not identify F− by itself.

<h2 id="chlorine-water">Chlorine has more than one reaction with water</h2>

A useful molecular account of chlorine in water is:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons HCl(aq)+HClO(aq)}$$

HCl supplies H+ and Cl−; HClO is chloric(I) acid, also written HOCl. Some HClO forms H+ and chlorate(I), ClO−. Thus the chlorine(+1) species can be represented as HClO/ClO− depending on conditions; no pH calculation is required here. The combined ionic bookkeeping for chloride and chlorate(I) formation is:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons2H^+(aq)+Cl^-(aq)+ClO^-(aq)}$$

Chlorine goes from 0 to both −1 and +1: **disproportionation**, in which the same element is oxidised and reduced. HClO/ClO− provides oxidising action that kills microorganisms and bleaches coloured substances; bleaching is not simply dilution.

On exposure to sunlight, chlorine water can lose its colour while oxygen is formed. The overall reaction is:

$$\mathrm{2Cl_2(aq)+2H_2O(l)\rightarrow4H^+(aq)+4Cl^-(aq)+O_2(g)}$$

This is equivalent to 2Cl2 + 2H2O → 4HCl + O2. In this overall reaction chlorine is reduced from 0 to −1 and oxygen oxidised from −2 to 0. Do not label the overall equation disproportionation merely because the reactant is chlorine water. Storage/light conditions affect what is observed.

With **cold, dilute, aqueous NaOH**:

$$\mathrm{Cl_2(g)+2NaOH(aq)\rightarrow NaCl(aq)+NaClO(aq)+H_2O(l)}$$

The solution contains sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, used as bleach/disinfectant. It is a disproportionation with chlorine −1 and +1. Do not substitute different products from a hot concentrated alkali process for the specified conditions. Never mix a chlorate(I)/hypochlorite bleach with acid: acidification can release toxic chlorine. This is a risk control, not an invitation to demonstrate it.

<h3 id="water-treatment">A useful toxic chemical still needs control</h3>

Water treatment uses controlled chlorination to reduce disease-causing microorganisms; leaving water untreated carries risks as well. Chlorine itself is toxic, excessive dosing is harmful, and reactions with other substances can form unwanted by-products. The justified judgement is that the health benefits of appropriately controlled treatment outweigh its harmful effects, with dose/residual monitoring and safe handling. It is not “any amount is safe” or “a toxic substance can never benefit health”. These are qualitative course principles, not instructions for choosing a drinking-water dose.

<h2 id="retry-halogen">Repair the model, then change the evidence</h2>

Close earlier answers. All retries are self-written.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:retry-halogen -->
A student says “Cl2 is a stronger oxidising agent than Br2, so Cl− must also be the stronger reducing agent, and Cl2 must boil higher.” Correct both deductions, naming electron gain/loss and the attraction relevant to boiling. Use this to predict Br2 added to chloride and chlorine added to bromide, with a net equation where appropriate.
<!-- /task:retry-halogen -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
The electron process for an ion is reversed; boiling uses a different model altogether.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Br− is the stronger reducing ion because it loses electrons more readily; Cl2 is the stronger electron-accepting oxidant. Br2 has stronger intermolecular dispersion attractions and boils higher. Br2 does not displace chloride, but Cl2(aq) + 2Br−(aq) → 2Cl−(aq) + Br2(aq). A complete repair must use three different explanations rather than one “reactivity” order for every property.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="retry-acid">Check a different sulfur product</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:retry-acid -->
A student claims every gas from solid NaCl with concentrated H2SO4 proves redox. Correct the claim with an equation and oxidation states. In a separate iodide mixture, sulfur is reduced from +6 to 0. How many iodide ions supply the needed electrons per sulfur atom? Construct the overall HI/H2SO4 equation and explain why colourless-gas observations cannot by themselves distinguish SO2 from H2S in an iodide mixture.
<!-- /task:retry-acid -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
First ask whether any oxidation number changes, then balance the six-electron transfer for sulfur.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
NaCl + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HCl changes no oxidation states: Cl remains −1, S remains +6. Gas liberation can be acid–base. Sulfur +6→0 gains six electrons, supplied by six I−: 6HI + H2SO4 → 3I2 + S + 4H2O. Both SO2 and H2S are colourless; appropriate controlled evidence is required, not intentional smelling. If coefficients fail, rebuild atoms, charge/electrons and water separately.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="retry-test">Change the cause of a white solid</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:retry-test -->
A sample known to contain only nitrate salts is treated with HCl and then AgNO3, giving a white solid that dissolves in dilute NH3. What does this show about the original sample's chloride content? Plan a valid repeat and a blank. Explain why “the solid dissolves in ammonia” cannot rescue the original inference.
<!-- /task:retry-test -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
A valid identification of the solid is not necessarily a valid identification of its source.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
HCl introduced chloride; the solid can be AgCl made from that reagent, so it does not establish chloride in the original sample. Repeat on a fresh portion with dilute HNO3 then AgNO3; treat deionised water identically as a blank and include a known chloride reference. The original dissolution behaviour is consistent with AgCl but cannot undo contamination. This is why source and sequence matter as well as colour.
</details>
