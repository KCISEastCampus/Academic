<h2 id="start-here">How can different drawings describe the same molecule?</h2>

This lesson supplies the language used in every later organic page: read a structure, name it, distinguish an isomer, and show where electrons move. It covers AS §3.3.1.1–3. Start with counting bonds, not memorising reaction maps. Carbon normally makes four bonds, hydrogen and halogens one, neutral oxygen two and neutral nitrogen three. A double bond counts as two bonds. Lone pairs are nonbonding electron pairs.

All examples and tasks are self-written/constructed, with no official marks. Try before opening help and record which hints you used. A later [mixed task](#later-check) checks connections without naming the method.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<h3 id="bond-start">Start with a bond count</h3>
<!-- task:bond-start -->
For CH3CH2OH, state the atoms directly bonded to each carbon and to oxygen. Draw every C–H and O–H bond. Why is CH3CH3OH, with all these groups joined in sequence, not a valid neutral structure?
<!-- /task:bond-start -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Expand every subscript into separate bonds.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
The first C bonds to three H and the second C; the second to two H, C and O; O bonds to C and H and has two lone pairs. Joining CH3–CH3–OH gives the second carbon three H bonds plus C and O: five bonds. Repair it to CH3–CH2–OH. If you counted groups instead of bonds, expand the drawing before proceeding.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="representations">Six representations, six different amounts of information</h2>

A **molecular formula** gives actual atom numbers, for example C2H6O. The **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio: C2H6O here, but CH2 for C2H4. A **general formula** describes a family, for example CnH2n+2 for acyclic alkanes. It is not a molecular identity. A **structural formula** such as CH3CH2OH shows the sequence of groups. A **displayed formula** draws every atom and bond. A **skeletal formula** draws the carbon framework: every unlabelled end and corner is C, its attached H atoms are omitted and inferred to make four bonds; heteroatoms and their attached H are written.

The diagram connects structural, displayed and skeletal drawings of propan-1-ol. Count three carbons before adding H. The line ending at the written O is a C–O bond: the O is not an extra carbon. Rotating a drawing or drawing a chain bent does not change its connections.

<h3 id="representation-check">Translate without losing atoms</h3>
<!-- task:representation-check -->
Draw displayed and skeletal formulae for CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3. Give its molecular and empirical formulae. State which of these representations distinguishes it from CH3CH2CH2CH2OH, and why the general formula CnH2n+2O cannot identify either molecule.
<!-- /task:representation-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
OH is on the second carbon. Reduce the atom ratio only if all counts have a common factor.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
The structure is a four-carbon chain with OH on C2: C4H10O, also empirical C4H10O. The displayed form has CH3 ends, CH(OH), and CH2; the skeletal form is a four-carbon zigzag with OH at its second vertex. Structural/displayed/skeletal formulae distinguish the OH positions; molecular, empirical and general formulae do not. A family formula does not specify connectivity. If you obtained C4H9O, you lost the O–H hydrogen.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="naming">Names encode connections, not the shape of the drawing</h2>

A **functional group** is the atom or group responsible for characteristic reactions. A **homologous series** has the same functional group and general formula; successive members differ by CH2. Similar reactions follow from the group, while physical properties change gradually with size and intermolecular forces. Having the same general formula alone is insufficient: alkenes and cycloalkanes can both have CnH2n but different chemistry.

Use the longest suitable chain containing the principal group and C=C; number to give the principal group the lower locant, then unsaturation/substituents as applicable. The roots meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex- mean 1–6 C. The AS naming boundary is at most six carbons in each chain or ring; cyclo- indicates a ring. A ring corner also represents C. List substituents alphabetically (ignore di-/tri- for this order), repeat every locant and use commas between numbers and hyphens between numbers and words.

| Group or family | Pattern / name example |
| --- | --- |
| alkane / alkene | C–C / C=C; hexane / hex-2-ene |
| halogenoalkane | C–X; 1-bromopropane |
| alcohol | C–OH; butan-2-ol |
| aldehyde | –CHO, terminal carbon included; propanal |
| ketone | –CO– between carbons; butan-2-one |
| carboxylic acid | –COOH, its carbon is C1; propanoic acid |
| nitrile | –C≡N, its carbon included as C1; propanenitrile |
| amine | –NH2; propan-1-amine |

These last groups are needed to name AS products and analyse them; their later A2 reaction catalogues are not required here. For the ether comparisons below, R–O–R′ has two carbon groups attached to oxygen and no O–H. Name the shorter group plus O as an alkoxy substituent on the other chain: CH3–O–CH3 is methoxymethane; CH3CH2–O–CH2CH3 is ethoxyethane. Methoxy means CH3O–, ethoxy CH3CH2O–. This naming bridge does not add an ether-reaction unit.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

For CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH2OH, choose the four-carbon chain containing OH, not a three-carbon fragment that looks straight. Number from OH: 3-methylbutan-1-ol. Check by rebuilding from the name: four chain carbons plus one branch, OH on C1. Cyclohexane is a six-carbon ring with two H on each carbon, C6H12; it is saturated but is not an acyclic alkane.

</div>

<h2 id="isomers">Same formula does not mean same connections</h2>

**Structural isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae: chain isomers have different carbon frameworks (butane / 2-methylpropane); position isomers have the same group at different positions (butan-1-ol / butan-2-ol); functional-group isomers have different groups (ethanol / methoxymethane, both C2H6O). The ether example is a structural comparison, not an extra AS ether-reaction syllabus. Always verify the molecular formula before assigning a category.

<h2 id="ez">E/Z needs two different groups on each double-bond carbon</h2>

**Stereoisomers** have the same structural formula (same connections) but different spatial arrangements. Around C=C the attached atoms lie approximately in a plane and rotation is restricted: rotating one end would disrupt the additional bonding. If either C has two identical substituents, no E/Z pair exists.

For each C separately, apply **CIP priority**: higher atomic number of the directly attached atom wins (Br > Cl > O > C > H). If tied, compare the next attached atoms in descending atomic-number order until the first difference. Thus CH2OH outranks CH2CH3: both start with C, but (O,H,H) outranks (C,H,H). The higher-priority group on each end on the same side gives **Z**, opposite sides **E**. Do not compare the mass or drawn size of whole groups; “two identical groups on the same side” is not the general rule.

Use the unassigned right-hand structure in the figure. On its left C, CH2OH is above and CH2CH3 below; on its right C, CH3 is above and H below. Assign E or Z with a complete priority explanation, then draw its partner. Decide whether CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3 can have E/Z isomers.

First check eligibility; then compare each end independently.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Left: CH2OH wins at the next atom layer, O over C. Right: CH3 wins over H. Both winners are above: Z. Swap the right CH3 and H to draw E without changing connections. CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3 has two H on one end, so no E/Z. If you compared left CH2OH directly with right CH3, restart: priorities are local to each C.
</details>

<h2 id="mechanism-language">A mechanism accounts for electrons as well as atoms</h2>

A **radical** has an unpaired electron, shown by a dot: Cl·. **Homolysis** divides a bonding pair equally, Cl2 → 2Cl· under UV. Radical mechanisms need balanced step equations and radical dots; curly arrows are not required for these at AS. **Heterolysis** gives both electrons to one atom, producing ions.

For electron-pair mechanisms use a full-headed curly arrow from an existing bond or lone pair to the atom/bond receiving that pair. A nucleophile donates a pair; an electrophile accepts a pair. A straight → links stages, ⇌ shows equilibrium, and a straight donor-to-acceptor coordinate-bond arrow describes a bond; none substitutes for a curly electron-movement arrow. Partial δ charges describe unequal sharing; a formal + or − labels a charged species.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

In CH3Br + OH− → CH3OH + Br−, an O lone pair supplies the C–O bond and the C–Br bond pair goes to Br. Draw both arrows; carbon never becomes a stable five-bond product. Net charge is −1 on both sides. Contrast Cl2 → 2Cl·: no Cl− is made by this homolysis. Full worked diagrams and neutral NH3 donation follow in [halogenoalkanes](/alevel/as-chemistry/halogenoalkanes/).

</div>

<h3 id="arrow-check">Repair electron bookkeeping</h3>
<!-- task:arrow-check -->
A drawing for CH3CH2Br + OH− starts an arrow at carbon toward O, and another at Br toward the C–Br bond; the products are CH3CH2OH + Br·. Redraw the correct mechanism with lone pairs and charges. Write a balanced homolysis equation for Br2 under UV and explain why it is different.
<!-- /task:arrow-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
An arrow tail identifies electrons already present, not an atom that needs electrons.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Draw O lone pair → Br-bearing C; C–Br bond → Br. Product Br− gives conserved −1 total charge. UV: Br2 → 2Br·, one electron per atom. A radical dot is not a minus sign; the first process is heterolytic substitution, the second homolysis. Return to the paragraph above if you drew an arrow from a positive centre.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="retry-arrows">Retry with a neutral donor</h3>
<!-- task:retry-arrows -->
H2O accepts H+ using an O lone pair. Draw this electron-pair step, show all O–H bonds in the product, and give its charge. Does H+ supply the new bond pair?
<!-- /task:retry-arrows -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Count oxygen bonds before and after donating its pair.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
O lone pair → H+, giving H3O+ with three O–H bonds and one lone pair. Total charge +1 is conserved; H+ accepts the pair, it supplies no electrons. This is the charge-counting bridge used later in acid-catalysed opening and dehydration.
</details>
