<h2 id="start-here">Why do three trends have different shapes?</h2>

Across Na–Ar, first ionisation energy generally rises, atomic size generally falls, but melting point rises and then drops sharply. “More protons” cannot by itself explain all three. We will identify the particle and process in each comparison, then choose an appropriate model.

Use [electron configurations](/alevel/as-chemistry/atomic-structure/) and [structures and attractions](/alevel/as-chemistry/structure-and-forces/) as prerequisites. This lesson covers OxfordAQA 9620 AS §3.2.1: classification into s/p/d/f blocks and properties of the Period 3 elements. Period 3 oxide/chloride chemistry is a separate A2 topic. All tasks and checking criteria are self-written, with no official marks.

<h2 id="classification">Read position from electron structure</h2>

Elements are ordered by **proton number**, not by relative atomic mass. A neutral atom's proton number also gives its electron count. The **period** tells you the highest occupied main shell. The **block** identifies the subshell being filled across that region: s on the left, p on the right, d in the middle and f in the two usually detached lower rows. The detached rows are part of periods 6 and 7, not extra periods.

For example, Mg, Z = 12, is [Ne]3s²: its highest occupied main shell is 3, it is in period 3 and the s block. Al, Z = 13, is [Ne]3s²3p¹, in period 3 and the p block. Fe, [Ar]3d⁶4s², is in period 4 and the d block; choosing only the highest n and seeing 4s² would incorrectly classify it as s block. Helium has 1s² and is an electronic s-block exception located with the noble gases because of its filled shell and chemical properties. You do not need to memorise all f-block configurations to identify that region.

A has 17 electrons, so it is chlorine: period 3, p block. B is period 4, s block; C is period 4, d block because the series fills 3d despite occupied 4s. <h2 id="radius">A period adds protons without adding an outer shell</h2>

Na–Ar electrons enter main shell 3, while the number of protons increases. Inner-shell shielding remains broadly similar; electrons in the same outer shell do not shield the added nuclear charge completely. The stronger net attraction draws the outer electron distribution closer, so comparable atomic radii generally decrease across the period. Do not say shielding is exactly zero or that a new shell appears at each element.

An atomic radius is a model-dependent measure, not the edge of a hard ball. Covalent radius commonly uses half the internuclear distance in an appropriate bond; metallic and van der Waals radii use different contacts. Argon has no ordinary comparable covalent radius in this series. A listed van der Waals radius for Ar must not be joined blindly to Na–Cl covalent radii and interpreted as a sudden reversal of the same measurement. The atomic attraction model extends across Na–Ar, but a numerical comparison must retain its definition.

<h2 id="ionisation">Explain the overall rise and the two falls</h2>

First ionisation removes an electron from a gaseous atom, forming a gaseous 1+ ion:

$$\mathrm{X(g)\rightarrow X^+(g)+e^-}$$

With the same main shell and broadly similar inner shielding, stronger nuclear attraction generally makes electron removal harder from Na to Ar. It is not sufficient to write “more electrons means more energy”: we must consider where the electron is and how it interacts with other electrons.

![Constructed rounded first-ionisation-energy record across Na–Ar. The general rise has a decrease from Mg to Al and from P to S; all element labels and energy values are shown. Lines guide the eye between discrete elements and do not represent intermediate fractional elements.](/assets/img/chemistry-as-periodicity-ionisation.svg){: width="680" height="360" }

The plotted training values, in kJ mol−1, are constructed rounded records: Na 500, Mg 740, Al 580, Si 790, P 1010, S 1000, Cl 1250, Ar 1520. They show the chemical pattern, not a new experimental dataset or recommended reference precision. A line only guides the eye between discrete elements.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Worked explanation — self-written.** Why is Al below Mg although Al has more protons? Mg is [Ne]3s²; Al is [Ne]3s²3p¹. Al loses its 3p electron, which is higher in energy and more shielded than a 3s electron. It is easier to remove despite the extra proton. This compares different subshells, not an extra main shell: both use n = 3. An explanation that Al has a fourth occupied shell contradicts its configuration.

P/S is a different turning point. P has 3p³: three singly occupied p orbitals. S has 3p⁴: one orbital contains a pair. Repulsion between paired electrons makes one electron easier to remove from S. Both removed electrons are 3p, so copying the Mg/Al “new subshell” explanation is wrong.
</div>

**Self-written; use the plotted constructed record.**
<!-- task:trend-check -->
Sketch the three 3p orbitals for P and S using arrows to represent electrons. Explain why S has a lower first ionisation energy than P despite its higher proton number. Then explain why Cl is above S and why the general atomic-radius trend across Na–Cl is downward. A student calls both Mg→Al and P→S “starting a new shell”. Repair both statements using the relevant configurations.
<!-- /task:trend-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Place one electron in each equal-energy p orbital before pairing; the two dips require different explanations.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
P: [↑][↑][↑]; S: [↑↓][↑][↑], with equivalent choices of which orbital contains the pair. Pair repulsion makes an electron from S easier to remove; the P/S comparison does not start 3p anew. Cl also has paired p electrons, but its greater nuclear charge with broadly similar shielding makes removal harder than for S. Across Na–Cl stronger attraction draws the same main shell closer. Mg→Al changes the removed electron from 3s to higher-energy, more shielded 3p; P→S changes pairing within 3p. Neither adds a new main shell. If “more protons” was your entire explanation, use [the retry](#retry-periodicity) to include the competing electron effect.
</details>

<h2 id="melting">First identify what must separate</h2>

Melting is not ionising isolated atoms. The structure and the attractions disrupted on melting change across the period:

| Element / specified form | Structure | Approximate melting point / K |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Na | giant metallic | 371 |
| Mg | giant metallic | 922 |
| Al | giant metallic | 933 |
| Si | giant covalent | 1683 |
| P, white P4 | simple molecular | 317 |
| S, monoclinic S8 | simple molecular | 392 |
| Cl, Cl2 | simple molecular | 172 |
| Ar | monatomic | 84 |

Reference approximations and specified forms are from Lister/Renshaw (2016), p.150. Different allotropes have different structures; “all phosphorus melts at 317 K” would overstate this table.

From Na to Al, more outer electrons per atom contribute to delocalisation and the positive ion cores become smaller/more highly charged. Their attraction to delocalised electrons is stronger overall, so more energy is needed to melt the structure. Do not describe it as attraction between neutral molecules. Silicon instead has a network of covalent bonds; substantial covalent bonding must be disrupted to melt it, requiring much more energy.

For white phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine, melting separates molecules while most P–P, S–S and Cl–Cl bonds within molecules remain intact. The attractions between these non-polar molecules are London dispersion forces. Larger, more polarisable S8 molecules have stronger dispersion attractions than P4 and Cl2; S8 therefore melts above them in this comparison. Argon consists of separate atoms with weak dispersion attractions, giving the lowest melting point here. Molecular shape/packing also matters, so electron count is a reasoned comparison, not an exact formula for melting point.

Distinguish metallic attraction, a covalent network and attractions between intact molecules or atoms.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Na→Al has more delocalised electrons per atom and smaller/more highly charged positive ion cores, strengthening metallic attraction. Si needs substantial covalent-network disruption. S8 has more electrons and a more polarisable cloud than Cl2, giving stronger dispersion forces between molecules. Calling the S8/Cl2 melting difference stronger internal covalent bonds is wrong; those bonds largely remain on melting. Ar has single atoms and weak dispersion attractions. Different phosphorus allotropes have different structures, so the numerical value applies only to the stated form. If you used the same “bond” for every case, attempt [the structure retry](#retry-structure).
</details>

<h2 id="retry-periodicity">Change the claim, rebuild the explanation</h2>

Close the earlier solutions. These retries and the mixed task are self-written.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:retry-periodicity -->
A student says: “Al is in period 4 because 3p is an extra shell; S has lower first ionisation energy than P because S begins that 3p subshell. Atomic radius increases Na→Cl because there are more electrons.” Correct all three statements with configurations and a causal explanation. Then classify [Kr]5s1 by period/block and explain why its outer electron need not be held as strongly as an electron in shell 4 merely because its nucleus contains more protons.
<!-- /task:retry-periodicity -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Check n separately from s/p, then compare distance, shielding and nuclear attraction.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Al remains in period 3: 3s and 3p share n = 3. P is 3p³ and S 3p⁴; pairing, not starting 3p, creates the P/S dip. Across Na–Cl electrons enter the same main shell while increasing nuclear charge and similar inner shielding strengthen attraction, reducing comparable radii. [Kr]5s¹ is period 5, s block. Compared with a suitable element above it, its new outer shell is further away and more shielded, which can outweigh increased nuclear charge. A complete correction must name the changing electron arrangement, not just reverse each arrow.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="retry-structure">Use a property to test a structural claim</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:retry-structure -->
A student draws separate Si4 molecules to explain elemental silicon's high melting point, and says white P4 melts by breaking every P–P bond. Repair the two models and explain the Si/P4 melting difference. Would replacing white phosphorus with a different allotrope leave that argument's numerical melting value automatically valid? Explain.
<!-- /task:retry-structure -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Decide whether the basic entity is a finite molecule or an extended network before naming the disrupted attraction.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Elemental silicon is a giant covalent network, not separate Si4 molecules. White phosphorus has P4 molecules; melting largely overcomes attractions between those molecules. Network disruption costs much more energy than the molecular separation, explaining the large difference. A different allotrope needs its own structure/value; 317 K cannot be transferred merely because it is also phosphorus. Rebuild a small network and two separate P4 units on paper if your explanation still treats both as molecular.
</details>
</div>
