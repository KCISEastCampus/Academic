<h2 id="start-here">What would make an unknown-ion identification convincing?</h2>

A white solid is an observation; “sulfate is present” is an inference that needs the reagent sequence and competing explanations. This lesson prepares you to select tests, keep evidence independent and decide when a result is inconclusive.

Prerequisites: [ionic equations](/alevel/as-chemistry/redox-equations/), [Group 2 solubility](/alevel/as-chemistry/group-2/#dissolving-and-reacting) and [halide tests](/alevel/as-chemistry/halogens/#halide-evidence). OxfordAQA 9620 AS Required Practical 3 covers Group 2 and ammonium cations, and halide, hydroxide, carbonate and sulfate anions. Here Group 2 comparisons include Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+ and Ba2+. Every task, unknown and observation record below is **self-written/constructed**, with no official marks. These are supervised practical-planning and interpretation activities; completing the page does not complete the hands-on RP3 requirement.

Begin by writing two separate columns: **what was done/seen** and **what that supports**. “Colourless gas evolved” belongs in the first; “carbon dioxide” needs a further test. Use small teacher-specified quantities, eye protection and labelled fresh portions. The teacher must approve reagents, ventilation and disposal before practical work.

<h2 id="ammonium-and-alkalinity">A gas test separates ammonium evidence from alkalinity</h2>

A solution that turns red litmus blue is alkaline: it contains hydroxide ions. That does not uniquely identify the original solute. Sodium hydroxide, aqueous ammonia and carbonate solutions can all be alkaline. Indicator evidence alone cannot distinguish them.

To test ammonium, add aqueous NaOH to a **fresh portion**, then warm gently in a hot-water bath under supervision. Hydroxide removes a proton from ammonium:

$$\mathrm{NH_4^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)\rightarrow NH_3(g)+H_2O(l)}$$

Hold damp red litmus in the gas above the liquid, away from splashes or alkaline droplets. A blue change there supports ammonia evolved from ammonium. Damp paper allows ammonia to dissolve and produce an alkaline solution. Paper touching the NaOH mixture could turn blue without any ammonia, a false positive for ammonium. Never deliberately smell a gas; use ventilation and gentle heating, not boiling or a sealed tube. Compare with a reagent blank and a known ammonium reference treated identically.

<h2 id="carbonate">Identify the gas before assigning carbonate</h2>

On a fresh portion, dilute acid supplies H+ to carbonate. The observable effervescence is explained by carbon dioxide production:

$$\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}(aq)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow CO_2(g)+H_2O(l)}$$

Pass a small amount of evolved gas into fresh limewater using an open, teacher-approved delivery arrangement. Initially it becomes milky as calcium carbonate forms:

$$\mathrm{CO_2(g)+Ca(OH)_2(aq)\rightarrow CaCO_3(s)+H_2O(l)}$$

Gas alone is not a specific carbonate result. Pair acid effervescence with the limewater observation and a working reference. Do not continue passing gas indefinitely: excess CO2 can dissolve the precipitate as soluble hydrogencarbonate forms:

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)+CO_2(g)+H_2O(l)\rightarrow Ca^{2+}(aq)+2HCO_3^-(aq)}$$

Record the **sequence** “clear → milky → clear”, not only the final appearance. The later clearing does not erase the earlier positive CO2 evidence. Never seal gas generation. Before stopping gas generation or allowing the apparatus to cool, remove the delivery tube from the limewater to prevent suck-back, following the teacher's apparatus procedure.

<h2 id="separate-portions">Choose the acid for the test, then protect the next test</h2>

Split the original sample before adding reagents. A used portion now contains the original ions, added ions and reaction products. Reusing it can create the result you later claim to discover.

| Question | Sequence on a fresh portion | Why this order matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sulfate? | Add dilute HCl first; allow carbonate effervescence to finish, then add aqueous BaCl2 | Acid removes carbonate that could also give a white barium precipitate; HCl supplies no sulfate. Never acidify this test with H2SO4. |
| Chloride, bromide or iodide? | Add dilute HNO3 first, then AgNO3; examine precipitate with dilute, then if necessary concentrated aqueous NH3 under supervision | Acid removes carbonate interference; nitric acid supplies no chloride. HCl would manufacture a chloride result. Ammonia is applied to the silver precipitate, not used instead of AgNO3. |
| Initial alkalinity? | Indicator on an untouched portion | Added acid consumes OH−, so an acid-treated sample no longer represents the initial pH. |
| Ammonium? | NaOH and gentle warming on a different untouched portion | Residual acid would consume the added OH− and could suppress NH3 release. |

For sulfate the key formation equation is:

$$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}(aq)+SO_4^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)}$$

Without prior acidification, carbonate can instead produce BaCO3(s): $\mathrm{Ba^{2+}(aq)+CO_3^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow BaCO_3(s)}$. Acid removes this interference by the carbonate equation above. A white solid after the correct acid/BaCl2 sequence supports sulfate in this RP3 context; a white solid from an arbitrary mixture does not.

For halides, $\mathrm{Ag^+(aq)+X^-(aq)\rightarrow AgX(s)}$. AgCl is white and dissolves in dilute ammonia; AgBr is cream, remains in dilute ammonia but dissolves in concentrated ammonia; AgI is yellow and remains in both. Compare clean reference tubes against a white background: colour alone can be ambiguous. Acidification must be sufficient to remove carbonate; add ammonia cautiously to the precipitate mixture in the teacher-specified amount because acid present consumes some ammonia. Keep the initial colour record before testing solubility. See [why these observations distinguish halides](/alevel/as-chemistry/halogens/#halide-evidence).

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Worked unknown — self-written, constructed evidence.** W is known to be exactly one of NH4Cl, Na2CO3 or MgSO4 in aqueous solution. Start with the candidate set, not a guess from appearance. Fresh portion A effervesces with dilute acid; the gas turns limewater milky. Portions B/C, acidified appropriately, give no sulfate/halide precipitate; blank and positive references behave correctly. The acid/CO2 pair selects carbonate, hence Na2CO3 **within the stated candidate set**. It does not directly detect Na+: sodium follows from the restriction. Without that restriction, another soluble carbonate could fit. A single “no precipitate” would not be enough to identify sodium carbonate.
</div>

<!-- /task:unknown-salt -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
A positive ammonium test selects one branch. Which anion test separates the two remaining candidates?
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
One route: fresh A + NaOH, gently warm, gas turns damp red litmus blue → NH4Br within the list; NH4+ + OH− → NH3 + H2O. With valid controls, no gas detected leaves MgCl2/Na2SO4. Fresh B + dilute HCl, then BaCl2: white precipitate → Na2SO4; no precipitate under a working method → MgCl2 within this list. Confirm bromide on fresh C: dilute HNO3 then AgNO3 gives cream AgBr, insoluble in dilute NH3 but soluble in concentrated NH3; Ag+(aq) + Br−(aq) → AgBr(s). A valid alternative is to begin with sulfate, then distinguish the remaining two by ammonium. Without the candidate restriction, these tests establish detected ions but do not uniquely establish Mg2+, Na+ or a pure single salt. Write full gas and precipitate observations, not only salt names. If you contaminated branches, use [the sequence retry](#retry-sequence).
</details>

<h3 id="sequence-check">Repair a contaminated sequence</h3>
**Self-written.**
<!-- task:sequence-check -->
A student adds HCl, then AgNO3 to an unknown and sees white solid. The same tube is then used for an OH− indicator test. Another student acidifies a sulfate-test portion with H2SO4. Explain each failure, replace each sequence using fresh portions, and write the net ionic equations for the intended chloride and sulfate precipitates. Why is “add any acid after the precipitate appears” a weaker plan than correct acidification first?
<!-- /task:sequence-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Track both the anion introduced by the acid and the H+ left in the liquid.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
HCl introduces Cl−, so AgCl could come from reagent contamination. The used acidic tube cannot show the original OH−/pH. H2SO4 introduces the ion the sulfate test seeks. Replace with fresh A + HNO3 then AgNO3 for halides, fresh B + HCl then BaCl2 for sulfate, and untouched C for indicator. Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq) → AgCl(s); Ba2+(aq) + SO4²−(aq) → BaSO4(s). Correct prior acidification removes carbonate before precipitation and gives an interpretable initial observation. Acid added later may dissolve carbonate but cannot undo introduced chloride/sulfate or recover the original pH; “any acid” also ignores reagent identity. If you reasoned only about H+, track the acid's other ion in [the retry](#retry-sequence).
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="cation-evidence">Group 2 comparisons need controlled conditions</h2>

Precipitation requires the dissolved ions to form enough of a sparingly soluble solid under the actual concentrations. Compare equal sample volumes and concentrations, identical reagent concentration/volume, mixing, temperature and observation time. Add a small prescribed amount first, record it, then the prescribed excess and record again. Adding more liquid both supplies reagent and dilutes the mixture; a change is not automatically a new ion identification.

Group 2 hydroxides become more soluble down Mg→Ca→Sr→Ba; sulfates become less soluble. With suitable matched concentrations, Mg2+ gives a white hydroxide precipitate with NaOH; Ca/Sr hydroxide formation depends on the concentrations, and Ba(OH)2 is relatively soluble. MgSO4 is soluble; CaSO4 is sparingly soluble, SrSO4 less soluble and BaSO4 very insoluble. These trends guide comparison with known references, not a universal promise that every tube has a distinctive amount of solid.

$$\mathrm{Mg^{2+}(aq)+2OH^-(aq)\rightarrow Mg(OH)_2(s)}$$

$$\mathrm{Ba^{2+}(aq)+SO_4^{2-}(aq)\rightarrow BaSO_4(s)}$$

Hydroxide precipitates here are not expected to dissolve simply because NaOH is in excess under the prescribed school-test conditions. Test sulfate precipitation of the **cation** using a soluble sulfate reagent on another portion, not BaCl2 (which tests a sulfate **anion**). Soluble sulfate can itself be a contaminant in any later anion test. Keep these branches independent.

<h3 id="cation-check">What does a white precipitate actually distinguish?</h3>
**Self-written, constructed observations.**
<!-- task:cation-check -->

Specify separate portions, reagents, variables and the small-addition/excess records. State the two solubility trends, explain why matching concentrations is necessary, and write the Mg(OH)2 and BaSO4 net ionic equations. <!-- /task:cation-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Distinguish a trend in solubility from four guaranteed unique visible outcomes. Does excess alkali necessarily dissolve these precipitates?
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Use NaOH on one portion of each reference and a soluble sulfate such as Na2SO4 on another. Match sample/reagent concentrations and volumes, temperature, mixing and observation time. Record a small prescribed addition, then prescribed excess separately; do not expect these hydroxides to dissolve in excess NaOH. Hydroxide solubility increases and sulfate solubility decreases down the group. Mg2+(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s); Ba2+(aq) + SO4²−(aq) → BaSO4(s). Several ions can give white solids; concentration can change visible precipitation, so intensity alone cannot identify Ca/Sr. Compare independent tests against matched known references and report overlap as inconclusive; obtain a teacher-approved additional discriminating test or a justified candidate restriction (for example Mg/Ba only with suitable reference-validated concentrations). Do not invent a unique Ca/Sr diagnosis from a cloudiness ranking.
</details>

<h2 id="controls">A blank and a positive reference answer different questions</h2>

A **reagent blank** replaces sample with deionised water and follows the complete sequence. A positive blank suggests contamination or a non-specific procedural effect; it undermines a claimed sample positive. A **positive reference** contains a known target ion at an appropriate concentration and follows the same procedure. If it fails, sample negatives cannot establish absence: reagent, sequence, heating or detection may have failed. A successful reference does not rescue a contaminated blank, nor can a clean blank rescue a failed reference.

Repeating a contaminated method produces repeatable wrong evidence. First diagnose the failed control, use clean glassware/fresh reagents, then rerun controls and new sample portions. A valid negative means “not detected under these conditions and sensitivity”, not “absent at every possible concentration”. Dilution can reduce a precipitate below visibility. Repeats help assess consistency/random variation; they do not remove a systematic reagent contamination.

<h3 id="evidence-check">Decide whether a result may be used</h3>
**Self-written, constructed records; all sulfate tests use HCl then BaCl2.**
<!-- task:evidence-check -->
Run A: blank white, sulfate reference white, sample white. Run B with fresh reagents: blank clear, sulfate reference clear, sample clear. Run C after the method is repaired: blank clear, reference white, sample clear in three repeats. For each run state whether sulfate is established, not detected by a working method, or unresolved; justify from the controls. Propose a specific recovery for A and B. Explain why the three clear sample results in C do not prove zero sulfate and why majority voting across A/B/C is invalid.
<!-- /task:evidence-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Check contamination before accepting a positive; check detection before accepting a negative.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
A unresolved: positive blank means the sample solid may be contamination; replace contaminated reagents/clean glassware and rerun blank/reference/sample. B unresolved: failed reference means the method has not demonstrated detection; check reagent identity/concentration, order and delivery, then rerun all with a fresh reference. C supports sulfate not detected under the validated conditions; the concentration could still be below visibility. Three repeats show consistency, not unlimited sensitivity or removal of a shared bias. Runs with failed controls are not equally valid votes. If you treated every clear tube as proof of absence, use [the evidence retry](#retry-evidence).
</details>

<h2 id="practical-plan">Plan records and risk controls before opening reagents</h2>

Make labelled portions for initial indicator, ammonium, carbonate, sulfate, halides and any Group 2 cation comparisons; retain original sample for reruns. For each, record sample/reagent identity, concentration and volume supplied by the teacher, order, heating if any, initial/final appearance and time. Keep observation and inference columns separate. Do not write “sulfate” where the observation should be “white precipitate after HCl then BaCl2”.

Use small prescribed quantities and eye protection against acid/alkali splashes; add slowly and wash splashes according to the laboratory procedure. Soluble barium salts are toxic; avoid ingestion/contact and collect barium and silver waste in labelled school-approved containers rather than pouring it away. Concentrated ammonia is irritating: use teacher-controlled ventilation, keep containers closed and never smell-test. Use a gentle water bath for ammonium and an unsealed, approved gas route for carbonate. These controls address particular exposures; a generic “be careful” is not a plan.

<h2 id="retry-gas">Retry — a blue paper with no ammonium</h2>
**Self-written, changed conditions.**
<!-- task:retry-gas -->
A known NaOH solution gives a blue change when damp red litmus touches droplets at the tube mouth during warming. A student reports NH4+. Explain the alternative cause, change the paper position/heating, and state the blank/reference outcomes required before accepting a gas-test positive.
<!-- /task:retry-gas -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
The gas path and the splash path must be distinguishable.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Alkaline droplets explain the colour without ammonia. Keep damp red litmus above liquid and away from splashes; warm gently in a water bath without boiling. The same procedure with a blank must not change the paper and a known ammonium reference must produce a gas-caused blue change. The known NaOH sample alone is not evidence of NH4+. Revisit [liquid versus gas evidence](#ammonium-and-alkalinity) if you still rely on pH alone.
</details>

<h2 id="retry-carbonate">Retry — a clear final limewater tube</h2>
**Self-written, changed conditions.**
<!-- task:retry-carbonate -->
After acid is added to a sample, gas makes limewater milky and continued gas flow makes it clear. A student deletes the positive result. Correct the record, write both limewater equations, and propose a repeat that preserves the diagnostic observation without pressurising the apparatus.
<!-- /task:retry-carbonate -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Distinguish initial precipitate formation from its reaction with excess gas.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Keep the clear→milky→clear sequence: initial milkiness supports CO2, later clearing is consistent with excess CO2. CO2 + Ca(OH)2 → CaCO3 + H2O; CaCO3 + CO2 + H2O → Ca²+ + 2HCO3−, with states as above. Repeat using fresh limewater, a small controlled gas supply and an open approved delivery arrangement; observe and record the first change promptly. Final clarity alone is an incomplete record, not a negative CO2 test. Return to [the carbonate explanation](#carbonate) if only the final state was considered.
</details>

<h2 id="retry-sequence">Retry — the wrong acid was used</h2>
**Self-written, changed conditions.**
<!-- task:retry-sequence -->
A sample is acidified with H2SO4 before BaCl2, giving white solid. The student proposes adding HNO3 to the same tube to fix it, then using it for an ammonium test. Explain why neither proposal recovers valid evidence. Design clean sulfate and ammonium retests and state the carbonate-interference and residual-acid reasons.
<!-- /task:retry-sequence -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Changing the H+ source does not remove sulfate already introduced.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
H2SO4 supplied sulfate, so the white BaSO4 may be reagent-derived; more acid cannot identify its source. Use fresh sample + dilute HCl, wait for carbonate gas to finish, then BaCl2 with controls. For ammonium use another fresh sample + NaOH, gentle warming and damp red litmus in evolved gas. Carbonate must be removed because BaCO3 is also white; residual acid in a reused tube consumes OH− and may suppress NH3 evolution. Revisit [separate portions](#separate-portions), then explain both failures without looking back.
</details>

<h2 id="retry-evidence">Retry — repeated does not mean valid</h2>
**Self-written, changed conditions.**
<!-- task:retry-evidence -->
Three halide tests all give white precipitate, but their HNO3/AgNO3 reagent blanks also give white precipitate. Another class has clean blanks but its known chloride reference gives no precipitate. Compare the two failures and propose distinct recovery actions. What cautious conclusion could follow a repaired, reference-validated negative test of a highly diluted unknown?
<!-- /task:retry-evidence -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
One failure creates false positives; the other undermines negative detection.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
The first result is unresolved because contamination can create every positive; replace contaminated reagents/clean equipment and rerun complete sequences. The second is unresolved because detection has failed; investigate reagent identity/concentration, order and delivery before rerunning controls and sample. A repaired negative means no halide detected at that dilution/method sensitivity, not no halide at all. Repetition does not repair shared contamination or an insensitive method. Revisit [controls](#controls) if your conclusion depended only on the number of repeats.
</details>

<h2 id="delayed-check">Return later — interpret a mixed evidence set</h2>
**Self-written, constructed record. Attempt after studying another topic, without the test table.**
<!-- task:delayed-check -->
V is an aqueous mixture within the RP3 candidate-ion set listed on this page; other sources of ammonia/CO2 and other test interferences have been excluded. Fresh A turns red litmus blue. Fresh B with NaOH and gentle warming evolves a gas turning damp red litmus blue above the liquid. Fresh C gives acid effervescence and initial limewater milkiness. Fresh D with HCl then BaCl2 is clear. Fresh E with HNO3 then AgNO3 gives a cream precipitate, remaining in dilute NH3 and dissolving in concentrated NH3. All relevant blanks are negative and positive references work. State supported ions and limits, write the NH4+, carbonate, and bromide net ionic equations, and explain why alkalinity does not independently prove a hydroxide salt. Does D establish that sulfate concentration is exactly zero? Propose an additional controlled comparison if a Group 2 cation is suspected, including what you would do if Ca/Sr records overlap.
<!-- /task:delayed-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Report detected species, not a guessed formula for a mixture.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and check</summary>
Evidence supports NH4+, carbonate and Br−; A establishes alkalinity/presence of OH− in the solution, not a uniquely added hydroxide salt, since carbonate/ammonia can also cause alkalinity. NH4+ + OH− → NH3 + H2O; CO3²− + 2H+ → CO2 + H2O; Ag+ + Br− → AgBr, with states as above. Sulfate is not detected under working conditions, not proved zero. Use fresh matched portions for NaOH and soluble-sulfate tests beside known Mg/Ca/Sr/Ba references with matched concentrations, volumes, temperature and timing. Overlapping Ca/Sr observations remain inconclusive; seek additional approved evidence rather than assigning by cloudiness. The tests do not establish one pure compound or exclude all other ions. If any conclusion outran its evidence, redo [the controls task](#evidence-check) before trying a new unknown.
</details>
