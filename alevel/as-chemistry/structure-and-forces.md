<h2 id="start-here">What must move, and what must separate?</h2>

To explain a property, name the particles, their arrangement, the attraction and the change being tested. “Strong bonds” alone cannot explain both graphite conduction and iodine boiling. You need [bonding and shape](/alevel/as-chemistry/bonding-and-shape/). Here you will construct crystal sketches, distinguish intermolecular forces from covalent bonds and infer structures from evidence. All tasks and unknown-material records are self-written/constructed.

<h2 id="crystals">Six materials, four structure models</h2>

A crystal has a repeating ordered arrangement. A finite sketch shows only a fragment: boundary particles have further neighbours beyond the page. A bond line may show a covalent connection; a dashed intermolecular contact must have a different key. Ionic solids are extended arrays, not packets of one formula unit.

| Material | Particles and structure | Property explained by a mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium chloride | Alternating Na⁺/Cl⁻ in giant ionic lattice; actual 3D nearest-neighbour coordination 6 | High melting: overcoming substantial electrostatic lattice attraction. Solid ions fixed: no current; molten ions mobile: current |
| Magnesium | Positive ion cores with delocalised electrons throughout giant metal | Electrons move in solid/liquid, so both conduct. Deformation can preserve nondirectional metallic attraction |
| Diamond | Each C covalently bonded tetrahedrally to 4 C in a giant 3D network | Hard, very high temperature required to disrupt network; no mobile delocalised carriers in ordinary pure diamond |
| Graphite | Each C bonded to 3 C in hexagonal sheets; delocalised electrons within sheets; weaker dispersion between sheets | High thermal resistance of the covalent network, electrical conduction mainly along sheets; sheets slide relatively easily |
| Iodine | Separate I₂ molecules, covalent I–I within, dispersion between molecules | Far less energy to separate molecules than disrupt a giant covalent network; molecular iodine has no mobile ions/electrons for bulk conduction |
| Ice | Separate H₂O molecules joined by an open hydrogen-bond network; O–H covalent within each | Melting mainly rearranges intermolecular network, leaving H₂O intact; open solid arrangement gives lower density than liquid near melting |

Graphite is not made of “graphite molecules”, and its layers are not held together by the same covalent bonds as within a sheet. Diamond/graphite transform or sublime under some pressure conditions before ordinary melting; the structure explanation concerns the high energy needed to disrupt their network, not a promise of liquid carbon at room pressure. Conductivity of an aqueous sample depends on whether it dissolves or reacts to produce ions; water itself does not automatically turn every molecule into ions.

<h2 id="dispersion">All molecules fluctuate; some also have permanent dipoles</h2>

Electrons fluctuate, creating an instantaneous dipole that induces a dipole in a neighbour. **London dispersion/induced dipole–dipole forces** occur between all atoms/molecules, including nonpolar ones. Larger, more easily polarised electron clouds generally give stronger dispersion. Shape and contact area matter too: a straight-chain isomer usually has more surface contact than a compact branched one. This is a comparison under similar conditions, not a law that molar mass alone ranks every melting point. Crystal packing can strongly affect melting.

Polar molecules additionally attract via permanent dipole–dipole interactions. You must first establish geometry and the overall dipole, not just identify a polar bond. Two molecules of similar size may differ because one has a permanent dipole, but differences in polarizability/shape can outweigh it: the label “polar” alone does not rank all substances.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Self-written explanation.** F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂ are all nonpolar diatomic molecules. More electrons and greater polarizability down this series strengthen dispersion, requiring more energy to separate molecules. The explanation concerns intermolecular attractions; it does not say the I–I covalent bond is hardest to break.
</div>

<h2 id="hydrogen">Hydrogen bonding connects specific donors and acceptors</h2>

In the AS model, a hydrogen bonded to strongly electronegative N, O or F is attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F in another molecule. Mark Hδ⁺ and the acceptor δ⁻, with the dotted hydrogen bond along O–H···O (for water). This is not an extra full O–H covalent bond. Draw the covalent O–H line and intermolecular dotted contact with distinct keys. Hydrogen bonding is especially strong among these intermolecular interactions, but an individual strength label does not override total dispersion in a very large molecule.

Water’s unusually high boiling temperature relative to comparable small hydrides follows from the energy needed to disrupt its hydrogen-bond network. Melting ice partly collapses the open network, letting molecules occupy less volume; hence liquid near 0°C is denser and ice floats. Not every hydrogen bond disappears on melting, and boiling does not dissociate water into hydrogen and oxygen. Ethanol can donate through O–H and accept at O; its H bonding to water helps mixing. The growing nonpolar hydrocarbon part of larger alcohols reduces water compatibility, so “has OH” is not a guarantee of unlimited solubility.

<h2 id="phase-and-evidence">A flat temperature does not mean no energy transfer</h2>

Heating a single phase usually increases average kinetic energy and temperature. During melting/boiling of a pure substance at fixed pressure, supplied energy changes intermolecular/structural potential energy while temperature stays approximately constant until the transition is complete. A plateau requires the stated pure-substance, pressure and near-equilibrium conditions; mixtures and rapid nonequilibrium heating need not show a perfect flat line. Freezing/condensation release energy as attractions reform.

For a molecular material, the liquid/gas still contains molecules. Ionic melting permits ions to move without converting them to neutral atoms. Metal melting disrupts ordered packing but retains metallic attraction; it does not remove every electron as in gas-phase ionisation.
