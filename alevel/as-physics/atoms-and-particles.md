<h2 id="start-here">How can a few deflections change a model?</h2>

A model must explain both common and rare observations. Rutherford-type thin-foil scattering shows most alpha particles travelling nearly straight: most atomic volume offers little obstruction. Rare large-angle deflections support a tiny, massive, positively charged nucleus causing strong local repulsion. Diffuse positive charge cannot readily produce the same effect. This is an inference from scattering, not a photograph of electron orbits.

<h2 id="nucleus">A nucleus is not a positive ion</h2>

In $\,{}_Z^AX$, proton number is $Z$, nucleon number $A$, neutron number $A-Z$. A neutral atom has $Z$ electrons; a positive ion has lost electrons. Proton charge is $+e$, neutron charge zero and electron charge $-e$. Approximate masses are $m_p\approx m_n=1.67\times10^{-27}$ kg and $m_e=9.11\times10^{-31}$ kg; relative masses about 1, 1, 1/1836 and relative charges +1, 0, −1. Specific charge is signed charge divided by mass, in C/kg. Prerequisites are ratios and these definitions; [photon physics](/alevel/as-physics/photons-and-wave-particle-duality/) later explains eV.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">
**Worked example — self-written.**
<!-- task:atom-worked -->
A helium-4 nucleus contains two protons and two neutrons. Use nucleon mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and elementary charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Find its specific charge, and compare it with a singly ionised helium-4 atom, neglecting electron mass.
<!-- /task:atom-worked -->

Nuclear charge is $2e=3.20\times10^{-19}$ C and mass $6.68\times10^{-27}$ kg, giving $4.79\times10^7\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}$. The singly positive atom has one electron and charge +e with nearly the same mass, so its specific charge is half the nuclear value. Do not replace proton number with ion charge.
</div>

<h2 id="atom-check">Use the nuclear model</h2>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:atom-check -->
An ion has nuclide notation ²⁷₁₃Al and charge +3e. Give proton, neutron and electron numbers and estimate specific charge. Use nucleon mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and elementary charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Most alpha particles cross thin foil with little deflection, but very few turn back: explain what each observation supports and why neither reveals fixed electron orbits.
<!-- /task:atom-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Count nucleons before electrons; separate observations from inferences.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and repair</summary>
13 protons, 14 neutrons, 10 electrons; specific charge $3(1.60\times10^{-19})/[27(1.67\times10^{-27})]=1.06\times10^7\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}$. Straight travel supports mostly empty volume; rare backward scattering supports concentrated positive charge and mass. Neither fixes electron trajectories. If the ion was treated as a nucleus, rebuild its electron count before the retry.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="antiparticles">Equal rest energies, different identities</h2>

AS comparisons include electron/positron, proton/antiproton, neutron/antineutron and neutrino/antineutrino. Partners have equal mass and rest energy, with opposite electric charge when charged. Neutral antiparticles remain distinct through other particle properties. Charge alone cannot identify them. Quark classification and atomic-mass-unit calculations are not extra requirements here.

Annihilation transfers rest energy plus any initial kinetic energy to other particles. At-rest electron–positron annihilation can produce two equal-energy photons in opposite directions: energy and momentum both conserve. A single photon cannot carry zero total momentum. Pair production needs at least both rest energies; a nearby nucleus takes recoil momentum, even when recoil energy is negligible. Use supplied rest energies in MeV: AS does not require $E=mc^2$ calculations.

<h2 id="energy-check">Test the threshold and budget</h2>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:particle-energy -->
An electron and positron annihilate at rest. Each has rest energy 0.511 MeV. Find the total photon energy and each photon energy for a two-photon outcome. A separate 1.50 MeV photon creates an electron–positron pair near a nucleus. Find the maximum kinetic energy available to the pair if recoil energy is negligible, and explain the role of the nucleus.
<!-- /task:particle-energy -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Add both rest energies, then allocate the remainder to kinetic energy.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and repair</summary>
Total photon energy is 1.022 MeV; each photon has 0.511 MeV and opposite momentum. The pair has at most $1.50-1.022=0.478$ MeV kinetic energy. Nuclear recoil enables momentum conservation; it does not supply missing rest energy. If only one rest energy was counted, use the fresh threshold decision below.
</details>
</div>

<h2 id="retry">Separate charge, identity and energy</h2>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:particle-retry -->
Compare a lithium-7 nucleus (Z = 3) with a lithium-7 ion of charge +2e. Give all proton, neutron and electron numbers and the specific-charge ratio, neglecting electron mass. Assess these claims: an antineutron is identical to a neutron because both are neutral; a 0.90 MeV photon can create an electron–positron pair whose members each have rest energy 0.511 MeV.
<!-- /task:particle-retry -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
A nucleus contains no electrons. Pair production creates two rest energies.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and repair</summary>
Both have 3 protons and 4 neutrons. The nucleus has no electrons, charge +3e; the ion has 1 electron, charge +2e. Specific-charge ratio is 3:2. Neutral antiparticles need not be identical to their partners; electric charge is not their only property. 0.90 MeV is below the 1.022 MeV threshold. Later start [Unit 1 review](/alevel/as-physics/unit-1-review/) with these answers closed.
</details>
</div>


<h3 id="specific-particles-check">Compare particle specific charges</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:specific-particles-check -->
Use proton mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Calculate the signed specific charges of a proton and an electron and their magnitude ratio. Explain why an electron's negative sign and its much larger magnitude have different causes.
<!-- /task:specific-particles-check -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Specific charge is q/m, retaining charge sign.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and repair</summary>
Proton q/m = +9.58 × 10⁷ C/kg; electron = −1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg. Electron magnitude is about 1833 times the proton's. Opposite charge gives the sign; much smaller mass gives the larger magnitude. Now close this answer and use the separate specific-charge retry below.
</details>
</div>

<h3 id="specific-charge-retry">Retry with changed conditions</h3>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">
<!-- task:specific-charge-retry -->
Retry with help closed: a positron has mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and charge +1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Find its signed specific charge. A helium-4 ion (Z = 2) has charge −e; neglect electron mass and use nucleon mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Give its electron count and signed specific charge. Explain why changing sign does not by itself change mass.
<!-- /task:specific-charge-retry -->
<details markdown="1"><summary>Hint</summary>
Retain the charge sign and count electrons from Z and net charge.
</details>
<details markdown="1"><summary>Solution and repair</summary>
Positron q/m = +1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg. The helium ion has 3 electrons and q/m = −1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹/[4(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷)] = −2.40 × 10⁷ C/kg. Charge sign and mass are distinct properties; an antiparticle has the same mass as its corresponding particle.
</details>
</div>
