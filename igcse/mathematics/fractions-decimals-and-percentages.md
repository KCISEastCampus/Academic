Half a bottle and 50% of a bottle describe the same amount. In this lesson, you will connect these different ways of writing a share, then use them to answer everyday questions.

You need multiplication, division and simple decimals. Use the [short recap](#before-start) if those feel rusty. Work on paper before opening a hint or solution.

Except for the labelled examination question, the examples and tasks below are self-written.
{:.lesson-meta}

## One amount, different names {#same-amount}

Imagine one whole strip cut into **five equal parts**. We take three parts:

![One strip divided into five equal parts. The first three are shaded and labelled taken; the remaining two are labelled left.](/assets/img/igcse-fraction-strip.svg)

We took the **fraction** $\frac35$ of the strip. The bottom number, 5, is the **denominator**: it says how many equal parts make the whole. The top number, 3, is the **numerator**: it says how many of those parts we took.

Now split each part into two smaller equal parts. There are ten parts altogether, and we have six. We have not taken any more of the strip:

$$\frac35=\frac6{10}=0.6.$$

These are **equivalent**: they have the same value. Multiplying the top and bottom by the same positive whole number, such as 2 or 5, splits every old part into that many smaller parts. The share stays the same. Dividing both numbers by a **common factor**—a whole number that divides both exactly—can group the small parts back together. For example, $6/10=3/5$ after dividing both numbers by 2.

The fraction is in its **simplest form** when no whole number greater than 1 divides both the numerator and denominator exactly. That is why we can simplify six tenths to three fifths, but cannot simplify three fifths any further.

The whole matters. Half of a small bottle need not contain the same amount as half of a large bottle.

<details id="before-start" markdown="1">
<summary>Recap: equal parts and decimal places</summary>

To share 20 equally among 5 people, divide: $20\div5=4$. Three of those shares contain $3\times4=12$.

In 0.6, the 6 means six tenths: $6/10$. In 0.06 it means six hundredths: $6/100$. Think of money: 0.06 of one dollar is 6 cents, not 60 cents. When a fraction does not simplify easily, a calculator can carry out the division; keep the fraction if an exact answer is needed.

</details>

## What does percent mean? {#change-form}

**Percent means “out of one hundred”.** So 60% means $60/100$. Divide each of our five original parts into twenty equal pieces: the same strip now has a hundred pieces, and we still have sixty.

$$\frac35=\frac6{10}=\frac{60}{100}=0.6=60\%.$$

A fraction is also a division: $3/5$ means $3\div5$. This gives another way to find the decimal. A percentage is a number of hundredths, so **divide the percentage number by 100** to get the decimal. To go back, multiply the decimal by 100 and write the percent sign.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

### A complete example: keep the same share {#example-forms}

**Question:** Write $\frac38$ as a decimal and as a percentage.

Divide the top by the bottom:

$$3\div8=0.375.$$

Here 0.375 means $\frac{375}{1000}$: three tenths, seven hundredths and five thousandths. The third decimal place counts thousandths, just as the second counts hundredths.

One whole is 100%. Our share is 0.375 of a whole, so:

$$0.375\times100=37.5,\qquad \frac38=0.375=37.5\%.$$

Check: $0.375\times8=3$, so the decimal really is three eighths. The percentage is below 50%, which fits a share smaller than one half. A percentage does not have to be a whole number.

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Your turn {#practice-forms}

**Question:** Write $\frac7{20}$ as a decimal and as a percentage. Explain why multiplying both the numerator and denominator by 5 keeps the value unchanged.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

How many twentieths fit into one whole? If each twentieth is split into five equal pieces, how many pieces are there altogether?

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$\frac7{20}=\frac{35}{100}=0.35=35\%.$$

Each old part becomes five smaller parts. The whole and the selected part are both split in the same way, so the share does not change. Check by dividing: $7\div20=0.35$.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### An examination question {#exam-check}

OxfordAQA 9260/1C, **Specimen 2018**, Q3, **1 mark**. Original English question and options:

**Question:** Circle the percentage that is more than $\frac14$ and less than $\frac12$.

15% &emsp; 20% &emsp; 35% &emsp; 55% &emsp; 60%

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Write both fractions as percentages before comparing the choices.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

The official answer is **35%**, B1. The following explanation is added for learning:

$$\frac14=25\%,\qquad \frac12=50\%,\qquad 25\%<35\%<50\%.$$

The other choices fall outside the interval. “More than” and “less than” exclude the two endpoints.

</details>

</div>

## How much is that part? {#find-part}

“Find 15% of 60” asks for an **amount**, not another percentage. The whole amount is 60. We can first find one hundredth, then take fifteen of them.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

### A complete example: part of a collection {#example-part}

**Question:** A box contains 60 pencils. 15% are red. How many pencils are red?

100% means all 60 pencils. One hundredth is $60\div100=0.6$, so fifteen hundredths are:

$$60\div100\times15=9.$$

There are **9 red pencils**. We did not physically cut a pencil into pieces: 0.6 is an intermediate calculation.

You can write the same calculation using a decimal:

$$15\%=\frac{15}{100}=0.15,\qquad 0.15\times60=9.$$

Check: 10% of 60 is 6 and 5% is 3. Together they give 9. Multiplying by a share below 1 makes the result smaller than the whole.

For a fraction, the idea is the same: $\frac35$ of 60 means divide 60 into five equal shares, then take three: $60\div5\times3=36$.

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Try a new situation {#practice-part}

**Question:** A library has 160 books. $\frac38$ of them are fiction. The rest are non-fiction. How many books are non-fiction? Show your working.

Fiction means made-up stories; non-fiction means factual books.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

First find how many books are fiction. Has the question asked for that group or the other group?

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$160\div8\times3=60,\qquad 160-60=100.$$

There are **100 non-fiction books**. Check: 60 fiction and 100 non-fiction make all 160. Do not stop at 60: that is the other group.

</details>

</div>

## What share is it? {#find-share}

Now turn the question around. If 18 of 24 places are filled, what percentage is filled?

We already know the amount of the part and the whole. **Divide the part by the whole** to find the share. Then write that share as a percentage.

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

### A complete example: decide what counts as the whole {#example-share}

**Question:** A club has 24 places. 18 are filled. What percentage of the places are filled?

The whole is all 24 places, so the fraction filled is $18/24$. Divide both numbers by 6:

$$\frac{18}{24}=\frac34=0.75=75\%.$$

The answer is **75%**, not 75 places. Check: one quarter of 24 is 6; three quarters are 18.

Percentages can exceed 100. If a club sells 36 tickets against a target of 30, the **target** is the comparison amount:

$$\frac{36}{30}=1.2=120\%.$$

It sold 120% of its target. This does not mean that 120% of a fixed collection was removed; the target is something that can be exceeded.

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Check your reasoning {#practice-share}

**Question:** A small box holds 12 pens and a large box holds 30 pens. Express 12 as a percentage of 30, and 30 as a percentage of 12. Explain why the answers differ.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In “A as a percentage of B”, B is the comparison amount. Which box is used as the whole each time?

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$\frac{12}{30}=0.4=40\%,\qquad \frac{30}{12}=2.5=250\%.$$

The first compares with 30 pens; the second compares with 12. The reference amount changes, so the percentages differ. 30 is two and a half times 12, which checks 250%.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Put the ideas together {#mixed-check}

**Question:** A club has 80 tickets to sell. It sells 0.35 of all the tickets in the morning and $\frac38$ of all the tickets in the afternoon. How many tickets remain unsold? What percentage of all the tickets is this? Show your reasoning.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Both sales figures refer to the original 80 tickets. Find the two numbers sold before finding the number left.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$0.35\times80=28,\qquad 80\div8\times3=30.$$

There are $80-28-30=22$ unsold tickets. Relative to all 80:

$$\frac{22}{80}=0.275=27.5\%.$$

Check: $35\%+37.5\%+27.5\%=100\%$. Applying the afternoon fraction only to the remaining tickets would answer a different question.

</details>

</div>

## If something went wrong {#repair}

- A decimal changed size when you added a percent sign: return to [what percent means](#change-form), then try [A](#retry-forms).
- You found a percentage when the question asked for a number of objects, or stopped at the wrong group: return to [finding a part](#find-part), then try [B](#retry-part).
- You reversed the two quantities or rejected an answer above 100%: return to [finding a share](#find-share), then try [C](#retry-share).
- You used the amount left after the morning sale as the afternoon whole: reread what “all the tickets” refers to in [the mixed task](#mixed-check), then try [B](#retry-part). The whole changes only if the question says so.

Close the old solution before retrying. Record whether you needed a hint.

### Retry A {#retry-forms}

**Question:** Write 18% as a decimal and as a fraction in its simplest form. A learner writes $18\%=18/10$. Explain the error.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Percent counts hundredths, not tenths. Simplify by dividing both numbers by a common factor.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$18\%=\frac{18}{100}=0.18=\frac9{50}.$$

The learner used tenths: $18/10=1.8=180\%$, ten times the intended share.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry B {#retry-part}

**Question:** A box contains 150 cards. 24% of all the cards are given out on Monday and $\frac13$ of all the cards are given out on Tuesday. How many cards remain? Check your answer.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

What does “all the cards” refer to on each day? Find the two numbers given out, then account for all the cards.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$0.24\times150=36,\qquad 150\div3=50,\qquad 150-36-50=64.$$

There are **64 cards left**. Both shares refer to the original 150 cards. Using one third of Monday's remaining cards would change the question. Check: $36+50+64=150$.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry C {#retry-share}

**Question:** A team's target is 40 points. It scores 46 points. What percentage of its target has it scored? Explain your choice of comparison amount.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Which quantity is described as the target? That is the reference amount.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$\frac{46}{40}=1.15=115\%.$$

The target 40 is the reference amount. The team exceeded it, so an answer above 100% makes sense. Check: $1.15\times40=46$.

</details>

</div>

### Come back to this later {#later-check}

**Question:** Two groups each have 40 students. In group A, $\frac58$ of the students walk to school. In group B, 0.6 of the students walk to school. Which group has more students walking, and by how many? Express each share as a percentage.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Check that both groups have the same total size. Find the number walking in each group.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$40\div8\times5=25,\qquad 0.6\times40=24.$$

Group A has **one more student** walking. The shares are $5/8=0.625=62.5\%$ and $0.6=60\%$. Check: the difference of 2.5% of 40 is one student.

</details>
