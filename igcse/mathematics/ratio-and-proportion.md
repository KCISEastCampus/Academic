## What stays the same when quantities change? {#start}

A recipe can be enlarged, a price can be compared per kilogram, and more identical workers may take less time for one job. “Both numbers change” is not enough to choose a model. Look for equal shares or a quantity that remains constant. You need [fraction operations](/igcse/mathematics/number-operations/#fraction-arithmetic) and [percentage multipliers](/igcse/mathematics/percentage-change/#multipliers). All tasks and data are self-written, with no official marks.

## A ratio counts matching shares {#ratio-parts}

Juice and water in ratio 3:2 means three equal-volume shares of juice for every two of water. It does not mean three litres unless the share size is one litre. There are five shares altogether:

$$\text{juice fraction}=\frac3{3+2}=\frac35,\qquad
\frac{\text{juice}}{\text{water}}=\frac32.$$

The fraction of the whole and the ratio of the two parts have different denominators. To simplify a ratio, first use matching units, then divide all entries by a common factor:

$$150\text{ cm}:1\text{ m}=150:100=3:2.$$

Order matters: juice:water=3:2 means water:juice=2:3.

![Five equal shares grouped into three shares of juice and two of water. A brace identifies all five shares as the total mixture.](/assets/img/igcse-number-ratio-parts.svg)

<div class="worked-example" markdown="1">

### Start with one share {#example-sharing}

**Question:** A 20-litre drink contains juice and water in ratio 3:2. How much of each is needed? In another mixture with the same ratio, juice exceeds water by 6 litres. Find the total volume.

For the first mixture, the total 20 litres represents five shares:

$$20\div(3+2)=4,\qquad3\times4=12,\qquad2\times4=8.$$

Use 12 litres of juice and 8 litres of water. Check both the total and ratio: 12+8=20 and 12:8=3:2.

For the second, the difference represents one share, not five:

$$3-2=1,\qquad6\div1=6,\qquad(3+2)\times6=30.$$

The total is 30 litres, with 18 litres of juice and 12 litres of water. Dividing the difference by the sum of the ratio entries would misidentify what the given 6 litres measures.

</div>

## Rates put two different units together {#unit-rates}

A rate compares unlike quantities: dollars per hour, kilometres per hour or dollars per kilogram. “Per” asks for one unit of the denominator. A worker earning 54 dollars for 4.5 hours earns $54\div4.5=12$ dollars per hour. At that rate, 7 hours gives 84 dollars, provided there is no different overtime rate.

For a best buy, compare the same quantity and quality. A 400 g pack for 2.40 dollars costs 6 dollars/kg; a 1 kg pack for 5.50 dollars costs 5.50 dollars/kg. The larger pack is cheaper per kilogram, but not necessarily the cheapest purchase when you need a small fixed amount and cannot buy a fraction of a pack.

## Two different ways quantities can be linked {#direct-inverse}

If 3 identical notebooks cost 7.50 dollars, one costs 2.50, and 8 cost 20. Doubling the quantity doubles the cost. This is direct proportion: the ratio cost/quantity stays constant, with no fixed starting charge.

For a fixed job, suppose 4 identical workers take 6 hours and can work independently without getting in each other's way. The job needs 24 worker-hours. Eight workers then take 3 hours, not 12: the product workers times time stays constant. This is inverse proportion. Without those work-rate assumptions, adding people need not halve the time.

For a graphical view, these constructed points describe a direct relationship and an inverse relationship:

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| y, direct | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 12 |
| y, inverse | 12 | 6 | 4 | 3 | 2 |

The direct graph is a straight line through the origin. The inverse points lie on a decreasing curve for positive x; it does not touch either axis. Both are models, not observations that prove a real-world law.

Repeated proportional change means applying a factor to the latest amount each time. Starting with 40 and increasing by 50% twice gives $40\times1.5=60$ then $60\times1.5=90$. The additions are 20 then 30; this is not a constant-addition model.

## Use the relationship, not the page title {#practice}

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Shares and units {#practice-sharing}

**Question:** A length ratio is 180 cm : 1.2 m. Simplify it and state the first length as a fraction of the combined length. Share 120 counters in ratio 2:3:5. A separate pair of quantities has ratio 5:3 and difference 12; find both quantities.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Convert metres to centimetres. Decide whether the given number represents the total or a difference.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$180:120=3:2,\qquad\text{first fraction}=\frac35.$$

For the counters, there are ten shares, each worth 12, giving 24, 36 and 60. For the pair:

$$5-3=2,\quad12\div2=6,\quad5\times6=30,\quad3\times6=18.$$

Check 30-18=12 and 30:18=5:3. Using 5/3 as the fraction of the whole would make the first part bigger than the combined amount.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Build a cost from a recipe {#practice-mixture}

**Question:** A 24-litre mixture has liquid A and liquid B in ratio 5:3. A costs 2 dollars per litre and B costs 3 dollars per litre. Find the total cost. Another 8 litres of B is added. Find the new A:B ratio and explain whether the original recipe is preserved.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Find the two actual volumes before pricing them or changing the mixture.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$24\div8=3,\quad A=15,\quad B=9.$$

$$15\times2+9\times3=57.$$

The cost is 57 dollars. After adding B, the ratio is 15:17. The original 5:3 is not preserved: adding only one ingredient does not scale both ingredients by the same factor.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Which purchase answers the actual need? {#practice-rates}

**Question:** Pack A contains 750 g for 3.60 dollars; pack B contains 1.2 kg for 5.40 dollars. Compare their prices per kilogram. If you need exactly 750 g or more and must buy whole packs, which purchase costs least? A worker earns 63 dollars for 4.5 hours at a constant rate. What should 6 hours pay?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Unit value and minimum total spending are different comparisons.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$3.60\div0.75=4.80,\qquad5.40\div1.2=4.50.$$

B is cheaper per kilogram. But one A pack meets the 750 g requirement for 3.60, less than any purchase including B at 5.40 or more.

$$63\div4.5=14,\qquad6\times14=84.$$

The pay is 84 dollars assuming the same hourly rate and no fixed deductions. Dividing 3.60 by 750 without unit conversion gives dollars per gram, which cannot be compared directly with dollars per kilogram.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Decide what remains constant {#practice-models}

**Question:** Table P gives pairs (x,y): (1,3), (2,6), (4,12). Table Q gives (1,12), (2,6), (4,3). Identify direct or inverse proportion and predict y at x=3. Plot both sets on separate axes with x from 0 to 4 and y from 0 to 12; add your predicted points and describe each graph. Separately, 6 identical pumps empty a tank in 10 hours: predict the time for 4 pumps and state an assumption. A quantity starts at 80 and falls by 25% twice; find the final quantity.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Test y divided by x and x times y. The pumps do the same total job.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

P has constant y/x=3, so at x=3, y=9. Q has constant product xy=12, so at x=3, y=4. Plot P with a ruled straight line through (0,0), (1,3), (2,6), (3,9), (4,12). For Q, plot (1,12), (2,6), (3,4), (4,3) and draw a smooth decreasing curve in the positive region; do not join it to the origin.

$$6\times10=60,\qquad60\div4=15.$$

The pumps need 15 hours if they have equal constant rates and do not interfere; a constant inflow would invalidate this simple model.

$$80\times0.75=60,\qquad60\times0.75=45.$$

The final quantity is 45. Two 25% decreases do not amount to subtracting 50% of the original.

</details>

</div>

## put the constant into an equation {#variation}

Direct variation is written $y\propto x$, meaning $y=kx$ for a constant $k$. Find $k$ from a known pair, then use it for a new pair. If $x=6$ gives $y=15$, then $k=15/6=2.5$ and $y=2.5x$.

Inverse variation is $y\propto1/x$, meaning $y=k/x$, with $x\ne0$. If $x=6$ gives $y=15$, the constant is now the product $k=15\times6=90$. Thus $y=90/x$. The same known pair can belong to different models; the statement of variation decides which constant to use. Positive contexts use $x>0$.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Form the equation {#practice-variation}

**Question:** For positive x, y varies directly as x and y=18 when x=4. Form an equation and find x when y=45. In a second model, z varies inversely as x and z=18 when x=4. Form its equation and find x when z=45.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

In the first model the quotient is constant; in the second the product is constant.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$k=18\div4=4.5,\quad y=4.5x,\quad x=45\div4.5=10.$$

$$k=18\times4=72,\quad z=\frac{72}{x},\quad45x=72,\quad x=1.6.$$

Check both with the known pair. In the inverse model, a larger output requires a smaller input, which fits 1.6 rather than 10.

</details>

</div>

## the same factor, not the same addition {#exponential}

For a starting amount $N_0$ multiplied by a positive constant factor $a$ each equal time interval:

$$N=N_0a^t.$$

Here $t$ counts those intervals. $a>1$ gives exponential growth; $0<a<1$ gives decay; $a=1$ gives no change. This differs from direct proportion between N and time: at time zero the starting amount is already present.

For the constructed model $N=200(1.5)^t$, the values at times 0,1,2,3 are 200, 300, 450, 675. The ratios between consecutive values are all 1.5; the additions are not constant. The model assumes the factor stays fixed and ignores resource limits. A mathematical model can predict non-integer values even for counts; say when the prediction is an approximation.

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Infer the factor {#practice-growth}

**Question:** A model population is 500 initially and 605 after two equal time intervals. Assume the same positive growth factor in each interval. Find the factor and a formula for N after t intervals, then predict N after four intervals. A separate decay model has 800 units initially and 512 after two intervals; find its factor and formula. Explain why a constant-addition model is not the stated assumption.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The ratio after two intervals is the square of the factor.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$a^2=\frac{605}{500}=1.21,\quad a=1.1,\quad N=500(1.1)^t.$$

$$N_4=500(1.1)^4=732.05.$$

This is a model prediction, about 732 individuals if a whole count is needed, not an exact observed count.

$$b^2=\frac{512}{800}=0.64,\quad b=0.8,\quad D=800(0.8)^t.$$

The population increases by 10% each interval; the second quantity loses 20% each interval. A constant addition would hold differences constant, not ratios. Two endpoint observations alone would not prove which model describes reality; the question supplies the modelling assumption.

</details>

</div>

### Retry shares {#retry-sharing}

**Question:** A ribbon ratio is 0.8 m : 120 cm. Simplify it and state the first share as a fraction of the whole. Split 84 tokens in ratio 2:5. In another pair with ratio 2:5, the difference is 21; find both quantities. A 14-litre mixture in ratio 2:5 uses liquids A and B costing 4 and 2 dollars per litre respectively; find the total cost. Then add 2 litres of A only to that mixture. Find the new ratio A:B and explain whether the original ratio is preserved.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

The same ratio can be applied to a total, a difference or ingredient costs, but first find one share. After adding one ingredient, update its actual volume while keeping the other unchanged.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$80:120=2:3,\qquad\text{first fraction}=\frac25.$$

84 tokens give one share of 12, so 24 and 60. The difference 21 represents three shares, so one share is 7; the quantities are 14 and 35.

$$14\div7=2,\quad A=4,\quad B=10,\quad4\times4+10\times2=36.$$

The original mixture costs 36 dollars. After adding A only:

$$A=4+2=6,\quad B=10,\quad A:B=6:10=3:5.$$

The original ratio is not preserved: A increases but B remains 10 litres, so both quantities have not been multiplied by the same factor. Increasing the total alone cannot determine a new ratio. The 2:3 length ratio is separate from the 2:5 token and original mixture ratios.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry unit rates {#retry-rates}

**Question:** Compare 500 g for 2.20 dollars with 1.5 kg for 6 dollars by unit price. If at least 2 kg is needed, with no mixing of pack types, compare the cheapest whole-pack purchase of each type. At 15 dollars per hour, what is the pay for 2 hours 24 minutes?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Convert minutes to a fraction of an hour, not decimal hundredths.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$2.20\div0.5=4.40,\qquad6\div1.5=4.00.$$

The larger pack is cheaper per kilogram, but four small packs cost 8.80 for 2 kg while two large packs cost 12 for 3 kg. Under the stated purchase condition the small packs cost less.

$$2+\frac{24}{60}=2.4,\qquad2.4\times15=36.$$

The pay is 36 dollars; 2 h 24 min is not 2.24 h.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry the model choice {#retry-models}

**Question:** Five tickets cost 18 dollars at a constant price per ticket. Find the cost of eight. Three identical machines take 8 hours to make an order; find the time for six machines under a suitable assumption. A quantity starts at 50 and is multiplied by 1.2 in each of two stages; find the result.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

For the machines hold total machine-hours fixed, not time divided by machines.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$18\div5\times8=28.8,\qquad3\times8\div6=4.$$

Eight tickets cost 28.80 dollars; the machines need 4 hours if rates are equal, constant and independent.

$$50\times1.2=60,\qquad60\times1.2=72.$$

Repeated 20% growth gives 72, not 70; the second stage uses 60 as its base.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry equations {#retry-variation}

**Question:** For positive x, u varies directly as x and u=24 when x=3. Find an equation and x when u=40. For a different model, v varies inversely as x and v=24 when x=3. Find an equation and x when v=40.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Use quotient for direct and product for inverse.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$u=8x,\qquad x=5;\qquad v=\frac{72}{x},\qquad x=1.8.$$

Both equations return 24 at x=3. The direct input increases with its output; the inverse input decreases.

</details>

</div>

<div class="lesson-check" markdown="1">

### Retry growth and decay {#retry-growth}

**Question:** A quantity has values 300, 360, 432 at equally spaced times 0, 1, 2. Assuming a fixed multiplicative model, write a formula and predict time 3. Another quantity has values 100, 90, 81 at those times; give its formula and time-3 prediction.

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare consecutive ratios rather than consecutive differences.

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Solution and check</summary>

$$N=300(1.2)^t,\quad N_3=518.4;\qquad M=100(0.9)^t,\quad M_3=72.9.$$

The models describe 20% growth and 10% decay per interval. Their differences change; their consecutive ratios stay constant.

</details>

</div>
